1. Perception & attention

On her study guide (slide 2) and in 3 of the 18 practice-test questions.

Perception
Organizing and interpreting what the senses take in so it means something.
Bottom-up vs. top-down processing
Bottom-up starts with the raw sensory input and builds up. Top-down is guided by what you already know and expect.
Schema / perceptual set
A schema is a mental framework for organizing information. A perceptual set is being predisposed to see things a certain way.
Gestalt psychology
The whole is different from the sum of its parts. Its grouping principles: closure (fill in gaps to see a complete shape), proximity (close together = one group), similarity (look alike = one group), figure and ground (an object stands out from its background).
Attention
Focusing awareness on a narrow range of things. Selective attention: focusing on one stimulus. Cocktail party effect: tuning into one voice in a noisy room, and catching your own name. Inattentional blindness: missing something in plain view because attention is elsewhere. Change blindness: not noticing that something changed.
Binocular depth cues (need both eyes)
Retinal disparity: the brain compares the two eyes' images; the bigger the difference, the closer the object. Convergence: how far the eyes turn inward to look at something.
Monocular depth cues (one eye is enough)
Relative size: of two same-size objects, the smaller image is farther. Texture gradient: texture looks finer with distance. Linear perspective: parallel lines seem to meet in the distance. Interposition: whatever blocks part of another object is closer.
Perceptual constancy
Seeing objects as keeping their shape, size and color even when the light or the image on the retina changes (a door seen at an angle is still a rectangle: shape constancy).
Apparent movement
Seeing a still object as moving.

2. Thinking & problem solving

Don't mix these upOneThe other
Changing a schema?Assimilation: fit the new thing into the schema you have (a Labrador is still "dog").Accommodation: change the schema or make a new one (a cat is not a dog).
Solving a problemAlgorithm: step by step, tries every possibility, guaranteed but slow (walk every store aisle).Heuristic: a mental shortcut, faster but can be wrong (read the aisle signs).
Which heuristic?Representativeness: judge by how well it matches your prototype or stereotype, ignoring the real numbers (her "truck driver or professor?" slide). People also ignore sample size: they draw strong conclusions from just a few cases.Availability: judge how likely by how easily examples come to mind: vivid, recent, in the news (fearing a plane crash more than a car crash).
Creative thinkingDivergent: many possible answers (uses for a paperclip).Convergent: narrow down to the one best answer (a riddle).
Stuck thinkingMental set: approaching a problem the way that worked before.Functional fixedness: seeing an object only for its usual use.
Bad decisionsGambler's fallacy: thinking past random results change future odds (a coin is "due" for heads).Sunk-cost fallacy: sticking with something because of what you already put in (finishing a boring movie because you paid).
Prototype
The best example of a category in your head; new things are sorted by matching it.
Priming
Exposure to one thing unconsciously shapes your response to the next (after "rabbit", you spell the spoken word "hair/hare" as h-a-r-e).
Framing
How a choice is worded changes the decision ("90% success" sounds better than "10% failure").
Executive functions
Higher-order thinking: generating, organizing, planning and carrying out goal-directed behavior, and thinking critically. Her study guide also lists inhibition and decision-making.
Creativity
Producing ideas that are new and valuable. Overcoming functional fixedness takes divergent thinking (George Washington Carver's 300 uses for peanuts).
Metacognition
Thinking about your own thinking: planning, monitoring and checking what you understand.
Testing effect
Being tested on material makes you remember it better than studying it more.
Memory trickFrom her slides: assimilation has "ss" = same schema; accommodation has "cc" = change concept.

3. Memory systems

Three steps: encoding (getting it in) → storage (keeping it) → retrieval (getting it out). Memory retention is keeping information over time through storage and retrieval.

Multi-store modelWhat it holdsHow long
Sensory memoryAn immediate, very brief record of what the senses take in. Iconic = visual; echoic = sounds.Iconic: no more than a few tenths of a second. Echoic: sounds can still be recalled within 3 or 4 seconds, even if your attention was elsewhere.
Short-term memoryA few items (about 7 plus or minus 2), like a phone number while you dial.About 15 to 30 seconds.
Long-term memoryKnowledge, skills, experiences; virtually unlimited.Relatively permanent.
Picture thisHer library analogy: sensory memory is the book drop, short-term memory is the cart waiting to be shelved, long-term memory is the shelves.

Working memory model

The updated version of short-term memory: you hold information and work with it (following a recipe while you cook).

  • Central executive: the "CEO". Directs attention and coordinates the other parts (multiplying 22 x 11 in your head).
  • Phonological loop: words and sounds. Two parts: the phonological store ("inner ear") holds speech for about 1.5 to 2 seconds; the articulatory control process ("inner voice") repeats it (saying your shopping list to yourself).
  • Visuospatial sketchpad: the "inner eye" for images and space (picturing your route to school; navigation).

Kinds of long-term memory

Explicit (you can say it)Implicit (you can't easily explain it)Future
Episodic: your own experiences, the what, where and when, from your point of view (your graduation day).
Semantic: general facts and knowledge (facts about the Roman Empire).
Autobiographical: your life story, episodic plus semantic facts about yourself.		Procedural: skills and habits (riding a bike). Implicit memory also covers classically conditioned associations.Prospective: remembering to do something later (returning a library book tomorrow).
Long-term potentiation (LTP)
After brief, rapid stimulation a synapse gets stronger, so the cell fires more easily. Believed to be a neural basis of learning and memory. "Neurons that fire together wire together."

4. Encoding & storing

Don't mix these upOneThe other
EffortAutomatic processing: encoding without trying (where, when, how often; well-learned word meanings).Effortful processing: encoding that takes attention and effort.
Depth (levels of processing)Shallow: structural (what it looks like) and phonemic (what it sounds like).Deep: semantic (what it means). Deeper processing = better recall.
RehearsalMaintenance: just repeating it to hold it in short-term memory.Elaborative: linking it to what you already know; lasts longer.
Study timingMassed practice: cramming all at once.Distributed practice: spread out over time. This is the spacing effect: spread-out study gives better long-term retention.
List position (serial position effect)Primacy effect: the first items are remembered well (they got the most rehearsal). Showing the list fast weakens it; a distractor task after the list does not.Recency effect: the last items are remembered well, but only when tested right away. A distractor task right after the list can wipe it out; showing the list fast does not.
Mnemonic devices
Memory aids using vivid images or organization ("My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Nachos" for the planets).
Method of loci
Place each item along a route or in rooms you know well, then walk back through it in your mind (the memory palace).
Chunking
Grouping items into familiar units, often without trying (1776 1812 1861 1914 1941 instead of 20 single digits). Chess experts remember real board positions in chunks, but not random ones (Chase & Simon); a runner went from about 7 digits to 80 by turning numbers into running times (Chase & Ericsson).
Categories and hierarchies
Grouping items that share features (fruits, dairy...), or ranking them from general to specific (Exams → Biology → Cell Biology).
Memory consolidation
The neural storage of a long-term memory: the process that makes it stable in the brain.

When storage goes wrong

TermWhat it is
Retrograde amnesiaCan't retrieve memories from the past.
Anterograde amnesiaCan't form new memories. Hippocampus damage can cause it; the person can still learn new skills (procedural memory, the cerebellum) without remembering learning them. Her slides point to the HM and Clive Wearing videos.
Infantile amnesiaNo memories from early childhood, usually before age 3 or 4; the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex are still developing.
Alzheimer's diseaseA progressive, irreversible brain disorder: memory, reasoning, language and finally physical function decline. Linked to lower levels of acetylcholine.
Memory trickFrom her study guide: Retrograde = can't recall the past (think "retro"). Anterograde = after the incident, no new memories.

Her slides also mention people with highly superior autobiographical memory, a hint that there are biological processes behind superior memory storage.

5. Retrieval & forgetting

Don't mix these upOneThe other
Kind of testRecall: pull it out with no cues (fill in the blank, an essay).Recognition: pick it out when you see it (multiple choice).
Matching conditionsContext-dependent: better recall in the same place or surroundings where you learned it.State-dependent: better recall in the same state of mind or body (tired, happy, caffeinated) as when you learned it.
MoodMood-congruent memory: you recall experiences that match your current mood.(vs. state-dependent: about the state you learned in, not the mood of the memory.)
InterferenceProactive: OLD learning gets in the way of new (you keep dialing the pizza place's old number).Retroactive: NEW learning gets in the way of old (you can only remember your new password).
Where did it come from?Misinformation effect: misleading information after the event gets built into your memory of it.Source amnesia (also called source misattribution): you remember the information but credit the wrong source.
Interference
One set of information blocks or disrupts the recall of another. Believed to be a primary source of forgetting.
Retrieval cues
Anything that helps bring a memory back: outside (a place, a smell) or inside (a thought, a feeling).
Forgetting curve
A graph of retention over time: memory drops fastest soon after learning; regular review slows the drop.
Encoding failure
It never got into long-term memory in the first place (you weren't paying attention or didn't connect it to anything).
Tip-of-the-tongue phenomenon
You know you know it, you may know the first letter or syllables, but you can't pull it out yet.
Repression
In Freud's psychoanalytic theory, the defense mechanism that pushes anxiety-arousing memories out of awareness.
Constructive memory
Memories are rebuilt each time from inferences as well as what happened, so biases can change them. Imagination inflation: vividly imagining an event makes you more confident it really happened. Elizabeth Loftus (in her talk) compares memory to a Wikipedia page, not a video recording.
Memory trickHer slide: "the problem is with the prefix." Proactive = the old memory pushes forward into the new. Retroactive = the new memory reaches back and replaces the old.
Try itHer slide on sleep: sleep protects against retroactive interference, so studying before bed helps (about 55% retained vs. 15% when awake, 8 hours later). The night before the test: study, then sleep.

6. Intelligence & testing

Intelligence
The ability to learn from experience, solve problems and use knowledge to adapt to new situations.
g (general intelligence)
Spearman's idea: one general ability underlies all the specific ones, so every task on an intelligence test measures it.
Multiple intelligences
Howard Gardner's theory of eight distinct kinds of intelligence.
Mental age vs. chronological age
Mental age (Binet): the age that typically matches a person's test performance. Chronological age: time since birth.
IQ
Originally mental age / chronological age x 100. On today's tests the average for each age is set at 100.
Standardization
Giving a test the same way every time and scoring it against a pretested group.
Flynn effect
Average IQ scores have risen over the decades in many countries.
Don't mix these upOneThe other
Consistent vs. accurateReliability: the test gives consistent results. Test-retest: same test, two occasions. Split-half: compare scores on the two halves. It can also be checked with alternative forms of the test.Validity (on her term list; she defines two kinds): Construct validity: evidence it measures the idea it claims to. Predictive validity: it predicts the behavior it's meant to (check by correlating scores with that behavior).
What's measuredAchievement test: what you have learned.Aptitude test: your capacity to learn; predicts future performance.
ExpectationsStereotype threat: worry about being judged by a negative stereotype lowers performance.Stereotype lift: knowing about positive expectations raises performance.
BeliefsFixed mindset: abilities can't change.Growth mindset: abilities grow with dedication and hard work.

7. AAQ statistics and research methods

Her Cognition warm-ups each ask you to describe the meaning of a statistic in a study. Her frame has two moves: (1) say what the statistic measures, then (2) say what it tells us in THIS study, with the study's numbers.

StatisticWhat it measuresRead it like this
Standard deviation (SD)How spread out scores are from the mean.Higher SD = more spread; lower = more clustered. In a normal (bell) curve about 68% of scores fall within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% within 2 SD, about 99.7% within 3 SD.
Correlation coefficient (r)How strongly two variables are related, and which way. Runs from -1 to +1.+ = they rise together; - = one goes up as the other goes down. Near 0 = weak or no relationship; near +1 or -1 = strong.
Percentile rankWhat percentage of scores fall below a score.75th percentile = better than 75% of scores. 50th = exactly the middle.
Try itIQ: her practice test treats a score of 130 as exactly 2 SD above the mean of 100, so on that test 1 SD = 15 points. That puts about 68% of people between 85 and 115.
Practice scenario (made-up study, to practice the frame)

Researchers timed 120 students solving a puzzle. The mean time was 40 seconds (SD = 6), and the times followed a normal curve. Describe the meaning of the standard deviation in this study.

Show a model answer
The standard deviation (SD = 6) measures how spread out the solving times were from the mean, which tells us that about 68% of the students solved the puzzle in between 34 and 46 seconds (within 6 seconds of the 40-second mean).
Practice scenario (made-up study)

A study of 90 students found r = -0.62 between hours of sleep lost and recall test scores. Describe the meaning of the correlation coefficient.

Show a model answer
The correlation coefficient (r = -0.62) measures the strength and direction of the relationship between sleep lost and recall scores, which tells us there is a fairly strong negative relationship (closer to -1 than to 0): students who lost more sleep tended to score lower.

Research-methods questions

5 of her 18 practice-test questions are about how a study was done, and her answer key puts research methods at about 25% of the multiple-choice section. What her tutoring guide says about each method:

Method or problemWhat it is
Experimental designThe researcher manipulates a variable. No manipulation = not an experiment.
Correlational researchLooks at how variables are related (e.g. scores on a beliefs questionnaire vs. GPA, reported as r). Nothing is manipulated.
Case studyDetailed, in-depth observation of one person or a small group.
Survey researchCollects data from a large population with questionnaires.
Naturalistic observationObserving without manipulating or controlling anything.
Cross-sectional researchCompares different groups at a single point in time.
Confounding variablesVariables the researchers didn't control (e.g. people's usual caffeine use or sleep) that weaken the validity of the conclusion.
Operational definitionsExactly how a variable is measured. Vague ones ("we measured perceptual set by showing ambiguous photos") weaken a study's scientific rigor.
Try itOn a methods question, ask two things first: did the researcher change something (experiment) or just measure it (correlational, survey, observation)? And how many people: a handful studied in depth points to a case study.
