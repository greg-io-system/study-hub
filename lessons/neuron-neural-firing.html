The Neuron & Neural Firing

For Monday's quiz. Three things to own: the parts of a neuron, the order a neuron fires in, and the major neurotransmitters. Tap around, then test yourself.

1 · The neuron — tap each part

Tap a part of the neuron to see what it does. The message travels: dendrites → soma → axon → axon terminals.

Also know: glial cells (glia) support, feed and protect neurons; Schwann cells build the myelin. Neuron types by job: sensory (in), motor (out), interneurons (in between); the reflex arc skips the brain for speed.

2 · How a neuron fires (in order)

The one thing graders love: the sequence. Walk it top to bottom.

  1. Resting potential — at rest the inside of the neuron is slightly negative (~−70mV), charged up and ready.
  2. Threshold — incoming signals add up; if they pass the threshold level, the neuron fires.
  3. Action potential — the electrical impulse fires and shoots down the axon. Depolarization: positive ions rush in and flip the charge.
  4. All-or-nothing principle — it fires fully or not at all. A stronger stimulus makes it fire more often, not "harder."
  5. The signal jumps node to node down the myelin (faster), reaching the axon terminals.
  6. Neurotransmitters are released across the synapse (synaptic cleft) to the next neuron's dendrites — excitatory (go) or inhibitory (stop).
  7. Refractory period — a brief pause before it can fire again. Leftover neurotransmitters are pulled back in by reuptake.

Drugs at the synapse: an agonist mimics/boosts a neurotransmitter; an antagonist blocks it; a reuptake inhibitor (like many antidepressants) stops reabsorption so more stays in the synapse.

Also on 1.3: the endocrine system (hormones via the bloodstream — slower, longer-lasting than neurotransmitters: adrenaline, melatonin, oxytocin, leptin, ghrelin), the fight-flight-freeze response, and psychoactive drugs (tolerance, addiction, withdrawal; stimulants / depressants / hallucinogens / opioids).

4 · Quick self-check

1

Put the neuron parts in the order a message travels.

Show answer
Dendrites (receive) → soma (integrate) → axon (carry) → axon terminals (release neurotransmitters).
2

What does the "all-or-nothing principle" mean?

Show answer
A neuron either fires completely or not at all once threshold is reached — intensity is coded by how often it fires, not how strongly.
3

What's the job of the myelin sheath, and what are the nodes of Ranvier?

Show answer
Myelin is fatty insulation that speeds the signal; the nodes are gaps where the impulse jumps, speeding it further. (Damaged myelin → multiple sclerosis.)
4

Match: Parkinson's, depression, Alzheimer's — to a neurotransmitter.

Show answer
Parkinson's → low dopamine; depression → low serotonin (and norepinephrine); Alzheimer's → loss of acetylcholine.
5

Agonist vs. antagonist?

Show answer
Agonist = mimics or boosts a neurotransmitter's effect; antagonist = blocks it.
6

Which is the main excitatory vs. main inhibitory neurotransmitter?

Show answer
Excitatory (go) = glutamate; inhibitory (calm) = GABA.
