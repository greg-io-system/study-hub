The Neuron & Neural Firing

Three things to own: the parts of a neuron, the order a neuron fires in, and the neurotransmitters (plus how drugs and hormones fit in). Tap around, then test yourself.

1 · The neuron — tap each part

Neurons are the nerve cells that transmit information through the body with electrical and chemical signals. Neural transmission is how neurons communicate with each other.

Tap a part of the neuron to see what it does. The message travels: dendrites → cell body → axon → terminal branches.

Also know: glial cells are the support cells — structure, insulation, communication and waste transport. Schwann cells are glia that make the myelin sheath. Neuron types by job: sensory neurons carry signals from the body to the brain and spinal cord; motor neurons carry signals out to muscles and glands; interneurons (in the spinal cord) connect the two. Together they make the reflex arc — a fast, automatic response without conscious thought (hand off the hot stove).

2 · How a neuron fires (in order)

Learn it as a sequence. Walk it top to bottom.

  1. Resting potential — at rest the inside of the neuron is more negative than the outside (about −70mV).
  2. Threshold — the minimum level of stimulation needed to make the neuron fire.
  3. Depolarization — positive ions (like sodium) rush in, so the inside becomes less negative. That triggers the action potential: a brief electrical charge that travels down the axon. Picture this: a dam’s floodgate opening.
  4. All-or-nothing principle — once threshold is reached it fires at full strength; below threshold it doesn’t fire at all. Picture this: a light switch.
  5. A myelinated axon conducts the signal faster, out to the terminal branches.
  6. Neurotransmitters carry the signal to the next neuron’s receptor sites — excitatory ones make the next neuron more likely to fire, inhibitory ones make it less likely (the membrane becomes more + or more −).
  7. Refractory period — the neuron can’t fire again until the ions are back to the resting state. Released neurotransmitter is reabsorbed by the neuron that sent it: reuptake.

3 · Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are the body’s internal chemical messengers. Each can have several jobs, depending on where it is in the nervous system.

NeurotransmitterWhat it doesLinked to
Acetylcholine (ACh)Skeletal-muscle contraction, heart muscle, arousal & alertness, brain–spinal cord messages (low ACh = low arousal and attention)Depletion → Alzheimer’s; receptors attacked (autoimmune) → myasthenia gravis (muscle weakness)
Dopamine (+)Mood, reward, motivation, smooth movement, attention, learningDeath of dopamine neurons → Parkinson’s; release in the nucleus accumbens linked to addictive drugs
Serotonin (−)Mood, eating, sleep–wake cycle, arousal, pain, dreamingMost drugs that relieve depression increase serotonin activity
NorepinephrineFound in the autonomic nervous system; fight or flight (activates heart and blood vessels); attention and memory for emotional events—
GABA (−)Most abundant inhibitory neurotransmitter — calming; sleep–wake cyclesLack → seizures, tremors, insomnia, anxiety, epilepsy, Huntington’s
Glutamate (+)Most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter — learning & memory, sensory and motor functionsToo much → overstimulation, migraines (MSG)
Endorphins (−)The brain’s own painkillers — pain or pleasureOpioids act on this system
Substance P (+)Pain signals; contracts smooth muscle and dilates blood vessels—

(+) = excitatory, (−) = inhibitory, as marked on the class slides. Disorder of neural transmission: multiple sclerosis — the myelin sheath is damaged, slowing or blocking signals.

Drugs and the neuron

Drugs are external chemicals that change how neurotransmitters work. Psychoactive drugs alter consciousness, mood and perception. To be psychoactive, a drug must get into the bloodstream and cross the blood-brain barrier. An agonist mimics or boosts a neurotransmitter; an antagonist blocks it; a reuptake inhibitor stops reuptake, so the neurotransmitter’s effect is stronger.
TypeEffectClass examples
StimulantsIncrease neural activityCaffeine — mild; in coffee, tea, chocolate, sodas; less drowsiness, better problem-solving and physical ability; high doses → tremors, anxiety; withdrawal = headache, fatigue. Cocaine — powerful; euphoria; blocks dopamine reuptake; high doses can bring hallucinations and paranoia.
DepressantsDecrease neural activity, mostly by boosting GABAAlcohol — common and socially accepted but impairs reasoning and motor skills; a “messy” drug (many neurotransmitter systems); 0.08% BAC = legal intoxication in the US; disrupts memory and attention; lethal dose around 0.4–0.5% BAC; high dependence potential, with genetic factors; withdrawal can be severe, even lethal.
HallucinogensDistort perception and thinkingMarijuana (THC) — well-being, relaxation, appetite; also some depressant and stimulant effects; mild memory impairment and time distortion; research on dependence is mixed.
OpioidsRelieve pain; euphoria; act on the endorphin systemHeroin — from morphine but more powerful; highly addictive and dangerous (high potential for dependence).

Tolerance = larger doses needed for the same effect. Addiction = physical and/or psychological dependence. Withdrawal = symptoms after stopping, usually the opposite of the drug’s effect (heroin kills pain, so withdrawal brings extra pain sensitivity).

Hormones (the endocrine system)

Hormones are chemical messengers made by glands; they travel in the bloodstream and are longer lasting than neurotransmitters. Adrenaline — fight or flight (raises blood sugar, speeds and strengthens the heartbeat, stimulates breathing). Melatonin — sleep / circadian rhythm. Ghrelin — hunger (high when you’re hungry). Leptin — fullness (high when you’re full). Oxytocin — labor, lactation, love.

4 · Quick self-check

1

Put the neuron parts in the order a message travels.

Show answer
Dendrites (receive) → cell body / soma (holds the nucleus) → axon (carries the impulse away) → terminal branches (junctions with other cells).
2

What does the "all-or-nothing principle" mean?

Show answer
Once a neuron reaches threshold it fires at full strength; below threshold it doesn’t fire at all.
3

What does the myelin sheath do, and what makes it?

Show answer
Myelin insulates the axon and speeds the signal; Schwann cells (glia) make it. (Damaged myelin → multiple sclerosis.)
4

Match: Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, myasthenia gravis — to a neurotransmitter.

Show answer
Parkinson’s → death of dopamine neurons; Alzheimer’s → depletion of acetylcholine; myasthenia gravis → acetylcholine receptors attacked.
5

Agonist vs. antagonist?

Show answer
Agonist = mimics or boosts a neurotransmitter's effect; antagonist = blocks it.
6

Which is the main excitatory vs. main inhibitory neurotransmitter?

Show answer
Most abundant excitatory = glutamate; most abundant inhibitory (calming) = GABA.
