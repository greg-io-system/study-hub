1. Is the point a solution?

The idea

A system is two equations that are true at the same time. A solution is a point (x, y) that makes both of them true. On a graph, it's where the two graphs cross.

To check a point, plug its x and y into each equation and see whether both sides come out equal. If it works in both, it's a solution. If it fails in even one, it isn't.

Example
Is (−1, 3) a solution of y = x + 4 and y = x² + 1?
  1. The point means x = −1 and y = 3.The first number is always x, the second is y.
  2. First equation: 3 = (−1) + 4 → 3 = 3 ✓Both sides match, so the first equation works.
  3. Second equation: 3 = (−1)² + 1 → 3 = 1 + 1 = 2 ✗(−1)² means (−1)(−1) = +1. Without the parentheses you'd get −1, which is wrong.
  4. It fails the second equation, so it is NOT a solution. Write "No" and show both checks.
Watch out: your sheet says algebraically. That means show the plug-in work for both equations, not just "yes" or "no."
Your turn

Is (2, 5) a solution of y = x + 3 and y = x² + 1?

Check your answer

5 = 2 + 3 ✓ and 5 = 2² + 1 = 5 ✓. Both work → yes.

2. Substitution

The idea

If one equation already says what x (or y) equals, you can swap that expression into the other equation. That leaves one equation with one letter, which you already know how to solve.

Then put the number you found back in to get the other letter.

Example
x = 2y + 1  and  3x − 4y = 9
  1. Find the equation that's already solved for a letter: x = 2y + 1.x is alone, so we know exactly what x is in terms of y.
  2. In the other equation, replace x with (2y + 1): 3(2y + 1) − 4y = 9Always put it in parentheses, because the 3 has to multiply ALL of it.
  3. Distribute: 6y + 3 − 4y = 93 × 2y = 6y and 3 × 1 = 3.
  4. Combine and solve: 2y + 3 = 9 → 2y = 6 → y = 3
  5. Put y = 3 back into the easy equation: x = 2(3) + 1 = 7
  6. Answer: (7, 3). Check in the other equation: 3(7) − 4(3) = 21 − 12 = 9 ✓
When the letters disappear

Sometimes after you substitute, both letters cancel out. That's not a mistake. It tells you something:

Left with something TRUE, like 2 = 2

The two equations are the same line → infinitely many solutions.

Example: y = 2x − 1, 4x − 2y = 2 → 4x − 2(2x − 1) = 2 → 4x − 4x + 2 = 2 → 2 = 2

Left with something FALSE, like 8 = 5

The lines are parallel and never cross → no solution.

Example: y = −3x + 4, 6x + 2y = 5 → 6x + 2(−3x + 4) = 5 → 8 = 5

Watch out: put the plugged-in number back into the easy equation, then check with the other one. Two of the three on your sheet end with the letters disappearing.
Your turn

Solve by substitution: y = x + 2 and 3x + y = 14.

Check your answer

3x + (x + 2) = 14 → 4x = 12 → x = 3; y = 3 + 2 = 5. Answer (3, 5).

3. Elimination

The idea

If you add two true equations, the result is also true. So line them up and add them. If one letter has opposite numbers in front (like +6y and −6y), it cancels out and you're left with one letter.

If the numbers aren't opposites yet, multiply a whole equation by something first to make them opposites. You can do that because doing the same thing to both sides keeps an equation true.

Example
2x + 3y = 12  and  5x − 6y = 3
  1. Pick a letter to get rid of. The y's are +3y and −6y.If the top were +6y, it would cancel the −6y.
  2. Multiply the WHOLE first equation by 2: 4x + 6y = 24Every term, both sides: 2×2x, 2×3y, AND 2×12. Forgetting the right side is the most common mistake.
  3. Add the two equations, column by column: (4x + 5x) + (6y − 6y) = 24 + 3 → 9x = 27
  4. Solve: x = 3
  5. Put x = 3 into an original equation: 2(3) + 3y = 12 → 3y = 6 → y = 2
  6. Answer: (3, 2). Check: 5(3) − 6(2) = 15 − 12 = 3 ✓
Spot it before you start: if one equation is just the other one multiplied by a number, they're the same line, so infinitely many solutions. Example: 2x + y = 5 and 8x + 4y = 20 (the second is 4 × the first). If the left sides match after multiplying but the right sides don't, the result is no solution. Example: x − y = 2 and −3x + 3y = 4 add up to 0 = 10.
Your turn

Solve by elimination: 2x + 5y = 1 and 4x + 3y = 9.

Hint

Multiply the first by −2 so the x's become −4x and +4x.

Check your answer

−4x − 10y = −2; add to 4x + 3y = 9: −7y = 7 → y = −1. Then 2x − 5 = 1 → x = 3. Answer (3, −1).

4. Solving by graphing

The idea

Graph both equations on the same grid. Wherever they cross is a solution, because that point is on both graphs. Two lines cross once, or never if they're parallel. A line and a parabola can cross twice.

To graph a line in y = mx + b form: put a dot at b on the y-axis, then use the slope m as "rise over run" to step to the next dots.

Example A: two lines
y = −½x + 1  and  y = 2x − 4
  1. First line: dot at (0, 1).b = 1, so the line crosses the y-axis at 1.
  2. Slope −½ = down 1, right 2: dots at (2, 0), (4, −1). Connect them.A negative slope goes downhill left to right.
  3. Second line: dot at (0, −4), slope 2 = up 2, right 1: (1, −2), (2, 0). Connect.
  4. They cross at (2, 0). That's the solution. Write it in the bottom row.
Example B: a parabola and a line
y = (x − 2)²  and  y = x + 4
  1. The parabola y = (x − h)² has its bottom point (the vertex) at (h, 0), here (2, 0).The sign flips: (x − 2) means h = +2, so move right 2.
  2. Make a table around the vertex:
    x012345
    y410149
    Plug each x in: (0 − 2)² = 4, (5 − 2)² = 9, and so on. It's symmetric around x = 2.
  3. Graph the line: dot at (0, 4), slope 1 = up 1, right 1.
  4. They cross TWICE: (0, 4) and (5, 9). Write both.
Example C: one equation isn't in y = form
y = 2x + 1  and  4x − 2y = 6
  1. Get the second equation into y = form. Subtract 4x from both sides: −2y = −4x + 6
  2. Divide EVERY term by −2: y = 2x − 3Dividing by a negative flips the signs of both terms.
  3. Compare the lines: both have slope 2, but different starting points (1 and −3).
  4. They're parallel and never cross, so there's NO solution. You can say this before drawing anything.
Your turn

Where do y = (x − 1)² and y = x + 1 cross? Make the table, then graph both.

Check your answer

Vertex (1, 0); table: x = −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 gives y = 4, 1, 0, 1, 4. The line passes through (0, 1) and (3, 4). They cross at (0, 1) and (3, 4).

5. Word problems: write the system yourself

The idea

A word problem is a system in disguise. There are two things you don't know (your two letters) and the story gives you two facts (your two equations). Same four steps every time:

1. Name the two unknowns in words.  2. Turn each fact into an equation.  3. Solve with substitution or elimination.  4. Answer in a sentence, with units.

Example A: vehicles (like the vans and buses)
One group fills 3 cars and 2 vans with 26 people. Another fills 5 cars and 4 vans with 48 people. Every car holds the same number, and so does every van. How many per car and per van?
  1. Unknowns: c = people per car, v = people per van.
  2. Fact 1: 3 cars and 2 vans hold 26 → 3c + 2v = 263 cars with c people each is 3c.
  3. Fact 2: 5c + 4v = 48
  4. Elimination: double fact 1 to get 6c + 4v = 52, then subtract fact 2: c = 4.
  5. Back in: 3(4) + 2v = 26 → 2v = 14 → v = 7
  6. 4 people per car and 7 per van. Check fact 2: 5(4) + 4(7) = 48 ✓
Example B: the current (like the boat)
A kayak goes 36 miles downstream in 3 hours. The trip back upstream takes 9 hours. How fast is the kayak in still water, and how fast is the current?
  1. Unknowns: k = kayak's speed in still water, c = current's speed.
  2. Downstream the current pushes you along, so the speeds add: k + c. Upstream it pushes against you: k − c.This is the whole trick of the problem.
  3. Speed = distance ÷ time. Downstream: k + c = 36 ÷ 3 = 12
  4. Upstream: k − c = 36 ÷ 9 = 4
  5. Add the two equations (c cancels): 2k = 16 → k = 8, then 8 + c = 12 → c = 4
  6. The kayak goes 8 mph in still water, and the current is 4 mph.
Example C: count and weight (like the candy)
A jar holds 30 marbles weighing 96 grams. Big marbles weigh 5 g and small ones weigh 2 g. How many of each?
  1. Unknowns: B = big marbles, S = small marbles.
  2. Count fact: B + S = 30. Weight fact: 5B + 2S = 96Each big one adds 5 g, so B of them weigh 5B.
  3. Double the count: 2B + 2S = 60. Subtract it from the weight: 3B = 36 → B = 12
  4. 12 big and 18 small. Check: 5(12) + 2(18) = 60 + 36 = 96 ✓
Your turn (two of them)

a) A bakery sells 2 muffins and 3 cookies for $13, and 4 muffins and 1 cookie for $11. Find each price. (Same type as the ticket problem.)

Check your answer

2m + 3c = 13, 4m + c = 11. From the second, c = 11 − 4m. Substitute: 2m + 33 − 12m = 13 → m = 2, c = 3. Muffin $2, cookie $3.

b) A plane flies 1200 miles with the wind in 3 hours and 1200 miles against it in 4 hours. Find the plane's speed and the wind's speed.

Check your answer

p + w = 400, p − w = 300 → 2p = 700. Plane 350 mph, wind 50 mph.

6. Piecewise functions: evaluating

The idea

A piecewise function uses different rules for different x-values. Think of a phone plan that charges one rate up to your data limit and a different rate after it.

It's written with a big brace. Each line has a rule on the left and a condition on the right that says when to use that rule. To evaluate, check the conditions first, pick the ONE that fits your x, and use only that rule.

Example
Find p(−6), p(−2), p(3) and p(7) for:
p(x) ={
x + 3if x < −2
x²if −2 ≤ x ≤ 3
10if x > 3
  1. p(−6): is −6 < −2? Yes. Use the top rule: −6 + 3 = −3Ignore the other two rules completely.
  2. p(−2): is −2 < −2? No (it's equal, not less). Is −2 ≤ −2? Yes. Middle rule: (−2)² = 4This is the edge case. < does NOT include the number; ≤ DOES.
  3. p(3): is 3 ≤ 3? Yes, so the middle rule: 3² = 9
  4. p(7): 7 > 3, so the bottom rule. It's just the number 10, so p(7) = 10.A rule with no x in it gives the same answer for every x in its range.
  5. p(−6) = −3,  p(−2) = 4,  p(3) = 9,  p(7) = 10
Your turn

Find f(−2), f(0), f(3) and f(5):

f(x) ={
x − 4if x < 0
2xif 0 ≤ x < 3
1if x ≥ 3
Check your answer

f(−2) = −6 (top). f(0) = 0 (middle, because 0 ≤ 0). f(3) = 1 (bottom, because 3 is NOT < 3 but 3 ≥ 3). f(5) = 1 (bottom).

7. Piecewise functions: graphing

The idea

Graph each rule only on its own part of the x-axis. The break point is the x-value where the rule changes.

At the break point, each piece ends in a dot: a closed (filled) dot ● if its condition uses ≤ or ≥ (that point is included), or an open (hollow) dot ○ if it uses < or > (it gets right up to the point but stops short).

Example
Graph
q(x) ={
−2if x < 1
2x − 1if x ≥ 1
  1. The break point is x = 1. Draw a light dashed line there as a guide.
  2. Left piece, y = −2: a flat line at height −2, drawn only to the LEFT of x = 1.
  3. Its end at x = 1 is (1, −2). The condition is x < 1, which does not include 1, so draw an open dot ○.
  4. Right piece: plug in the break point first: 2(1) − 1 = 1. The condition x ≥ 1 includes 1, so draw a closed dot ● at (1, 1).Always start a piece by plugging the break point into ITS rule.
  5. More points for the right piece: (2, 3), (3, 5). Draw the line to the right.
  6. The two dots are at different heights, so the graph jumps at x = 1. If they were at the same height, the pieces would join.
Watch out: on your #6 the y-axis goes up by 2s and runs to 18, so count grid lines carefully when you plot.
Your turn

Graph g(x) = x + 1 if x ≤ 2, and g(x) = 4 if x > 2. Which dot is open and which is closed? Does it jump?

Check your answer

Left piece: the line y = x + 1 up to a closed dot at (2, 3), because ≤ includes 2.

Right piece: a flat line at y = 4 starting with an open dot at (2, 4), because > doesn't include 2.

Different heights (3 vs 4), so it jumps.

8. Writing a piecewise function from a story

The idea

When a price (or rate) changes partway through, you need a piecewise function: one rule before the change and another after it. The second rule has to include everything already paid during the first stretch.

Example
A gym charges $12 per visit for your first 5 visits in a month, then $8 per visit after that. (a) Write C(v) for v visits. (b) What do 9 visits cost? (c) How much more do 7 visits cost than 3?
  1. Where does the rule change? After visit 5. So the break point is v = 5, and there will be two lines.
  2. First stretch (5 visits or fewer): every visit is $12, so C(v) = 12v if 0 ≤ v ≤ 5.
  3. After 5 visits, you've already paid 5 × $12 = $60.This is the part people forget.
  4. Each extra visit beyond 5 costs $8, and there are (v − 5) of them. So C(v) = 60 + 8(v − 5) if v > 5.At 9 visits, v − 5 = 4 extra visits.
  5. Write it with the brace:
    C(v) ={
    12vif 0 ≤ v ≤ 5
    60 + 8(v − 5)if v > 5
  6. (b) 9 > 5, so use the second rule: 60 + 8(4) = $92
  7. (c) 7 visits: 60 + 8(2) = $76. 3 visits: first rule, 12(3) = $36. Difference: $40.
The big mistake: writing ONE formula, like 12v − 8v. If you catch yourself with one formula, stop: the rule changes, so you need two lines with two conditions. Quick check: plug the break point into both rules. Here both give $60 at v = 5, so they agree.
Your turn

A streaming service costs $10 a month for the first 3 months, then $15 a month after that. Write C(m) for m months. What do 6 months cost?

Check your answer

C(m) = 10m if 0 ≤ m ≤ 3; C(m) = 30 + 15(m − 3) if m > 3.

6 months: 30 + 15(3) = $75.
