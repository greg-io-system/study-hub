1. Structure of the skin

The three main layers

LayerWhat it isMain job
Epidermis (top)Thin sheet of stacked epithelial cells. No blood vessels — fed from the dermis below.Barrier: blocks water loss, germs, scrapes, UV.
Dermis (middle)Thick connective tissue (collagen + elastic fibers). "Where all the goodies live."Strength and stretch; holds the glands, follicles, vessels, nerves and receptors.
Hypodermis / subcutaneous (bottom)Fat (adipose) + loose connective tissue; bigger vessels and nerves.Anchors skin to muscle and bone; insulates; cushions.

The five layers of the epidermis — and the cells in them

Top → bottom (the class mnemonic): Corneum · Lucidum · Granulosum · Spinosum · Basale → “Creamy Lotion Gets Skin Beautiful.”
Cells are born at the bottom (basale) and pushed up, filling with keratin and dying on the way; the top layer is shed and fully replaced about once a month.

Structures of the dermis (the skin diagram)

StructureWhat it does
Dermal papillaeBumps on top of the dermis that lock it to the epidermis; on fingertips they form fingerprint ridges.
Blood vesselsFeed the skin (epidermis by diffusion); widen or narrow to control heat loss.
Sweat (sudoriferous) glandsRelease sweat through pores; evaporation cools the body; also excretes salts and urea.
Oil (sebaceous) glandsRelease sebum into hair follicles; keeps skin and hair soft and waterproof, slows bacteria.
Hair follicleTube of epidermal cells that grows the hair; its cells can help regrow skin after injury.
Arrector pili muscleTiny muscle on the follicle; contracts in cold or fear → hair stands up (goosebumps), traps a little warm air.
Sensory receptorsCorpuscles sense pressure / touch; free nerve endings sense pain, itch, temperature.
NervesCarry signals from receptors to the brain, and signals to glands and vessels.

Fingerprints

  • Three classes: arch (~5%, 0 deltas; plain or tented), loop (~60%, 1 delta; radial or ulnar), whorl (~35%, 2+ deltas; plain, central pocket, double loop, accidental).
  • Radial vs. ulnar loop: named for the forearm bone the loop opens toward — radius on the thumb side, ulna on the pinky side.
  • Three principles: every print is individual; the pattern doesn't change over a lifetime (unless permanently scarred); patterns can be classified systematically.
  • Ridge characteristics (ridge ending, fork/bifurcation, dot, island, lake/enclosure, hook, delta, core) are the points of identification — the more shared, the stronger the match.
  • Dactyloscopy = the study of fingerprint identification.

2. Functions of the skin

The skin's jobs

FunctionHow
ProtectionPhysical barrier against scrapes, bacteria, drying out, UV (melanin).
Temperature regulationSweat; blood vessels widening/narrowing; arrector pili.
SensationReceptors for pain, pressure, touch, temperature.
ExcretionSweat carries out salts (electrolytes), urea, small wastes.
ImmunityLangerhans cells catch invaders and alert the immune system.
Blood reservoirSkin vessels hold a lot of blood. Moderate exercise: they widen to dump heat. Hard exercise: they narrow to send that blood to the muscles.
Vitamin D synthesisUV light triggers vitamin D production; vitamin D helps absorb calcium for bones.

Which layer does which? Epidermis = barrier, UV shield, immunity, vitamin D, touch (Merkel). Dermis = temperature control, sensation, excretion, blood reservoir, strength. Hypodermis = insulation, cushioning, energy storage (fat), anchoring.

How dermal structures keep you in balance (homeostasis)

Body temperature is a negative feedback loop (from Unit 1): the brain's thermostat (hypothalamus) senses a change and sends orders to the skin to push temperature back toward normal.

Too hot ↑

  • Sweat glands release sweat → evaporation cools.
  • Blood vessels widen (vasodilation) → warm blood near the surface loses heat → skin looks flushed.

Too cold ↓

  • Blood vessels narrow (vasoconstriction) → blood stays deep, heat kept in → skin looks pale.
  • Arrector pili contract → goosebumps.
  • Sweat stops; muscles shiver to make heat.

Other homeostasis jobs: sweat helps balance salts and water; oil and the waterproof granulosum stop dehydration; receptors warn the brain about damage (pain) so you react.

The four epidermal cells

CellWhereJob
KeratinocytesAll layers (most common cell)Make keratin — the tough, waterproof protein of skin, hair and nails. Born in the basale, die and shed at the corneum.
MelanocytesStratum basaleMake melanin (pigment) that absorbs UV and protects cell DNA; gives skin its color.
Langerhans cellsStratum spinosumImmune cells: tell harmful invaders from harmless, grab them and alert the immune system.
Merkel cellsStratum basaleTouch receptors: light touch, pressure, fine texture.

3. Disorders & trauma

The four types of burns + healing

TypeReachesSignsHealing
1stEpidermis onlyRed, painful, mild swelling, no blistersHeals itself in 3–7 days as basale keratinocytes divide
2ndInto the dermisBlisters, severe pain, swellingHeals in 2–3 weeks; epidermis and part of dermis regrow; often scars
3rdAll of epidermis + dermis, to subcutaneousWhite, charred, leathery; little pain (nerves destroyed)Can't heal on its own — melanocytes, follicles, glands are gone → skin graft
4thInto muscle, tendon, boneCharred; permanent damageEmergency surgery; grafts
  • The healing process: inflammation comes first — blood vessels widen and white blood cells arrive to fight infection. Then fibroblasts in the dermis make collagen to rebuild and close the wound. Too much collagen = a raised hypertrophic scar.
  • Myths: butter or oil (traps heat, raises infection risk) — ice (damages tissue; use cool running water) — "burns never scar" (2nd degree and deeper often do).
  • Signs of infection: spreading redness, pus, fever, red streaks from the wound.

Four skin disorders

Sort them by cause first — that's half the answer: scabies = parasite, candidiasis = fungus, psoriasis and vitiligo = autoimmune (the body attacks itself).
DisorderCauseSymptomsSpread / treatment
ScabiesParasite: a microscopic mite (Sarcoptes scabiei) that burrows into the epidermis to lay eggsIntense itching (worse at night), tiny bumps and thin burrow lines, often between fingers and at wristsContagious: skin-to-skin contact or shared bedding, clothes, towels. Treated with creams or pills that kill the mites.
CandidiasisFungus: Candida yeast, which normally lives on skin harmlessly, overgrowsRed, itchy, sometimes raw rash, usually in warm, moist folds (armpits, groin, under breasts, diaper area); in the mouth it's called thrushAntifungal creams; keeping the area dry. Overgrowth is more likely after antibiotics or with a weak immune system.
PsoriasisAutoimmune; genes + environmental triggers (stress, infection, injury)Raised, red, scaly patches (plaques), often on elbows, knees, scalpNot contagious. Skin cells are overproduced — they reach the surface in days instead of about a month, so they pile up as scales. Managed, not cured.
VitiligoAutoimmune (believed): the immune system destroys melanocytesWhite patches where pigment (melanin) is lostNot contagious. No cure; treatments can improve appearance and slow the spread. Patches sunburn easily (no melanin).

Other infections from the notes, by type — viral: warts (HPV), herpes, chicken pox, shingles; bacterial: impetigo (strep; sores on face and hands, common in kids), folliculitis; fungal: ringworm, athlete's foot; parasitic: scabies, swimmer's itch.

Skin cancer — types, causes, prevention

TypeStarts inDanger
Basal cell carcinomaBasal cells (stratum basale)Most common; grows slowly, easy to treat if caught early
Squamous cell carcinomaKeratinocytes higher upSecond most common; also fairly slow
MelanomaMelanocytesMost dangerous — can spread to other organs quickly
  • Main cause: UV radiation (sun, tanning beds) damages the DNA in skin cells. Risk factors: sunburn history, fair skin, family history, lots of moles, tanning beds (up to 75% higher melanoma risk if used before age 35).
  • Prevention: avoid peak sun (10 am–4 pm); broad-spectrum SPF 30+, reapplied every 2 hours; protective clothing, hats, sunglasses; no tanning beds; check your skin monthly for new or changing spots.

Any one warning sign is a reason to get a spot checked by a doctor. E (evolving) is often the most important — a mole that's changing.

Tattoos — why the ink stays

  • The needle pushes ink through the epidermis into the dermis.
  • Ink left in the epidermis would be gone in weeks — those cells ride up and shed. The dermis is stable: its cells aren't constantly replaced.
  • The body treats the ink as an invader: immune cells called macrophages swallow the ink particles — but the particles are too big to break down or carry away, so the macrophages just stay put, holding the ink. When one dies, a new one takes up the ink. Fibroblasts in the dermis hold some too.
  • That's why tattoos slowly blur and fade over years, and why laser removal works: it shatters the ink into pieces small enough for the immune system to clear.
