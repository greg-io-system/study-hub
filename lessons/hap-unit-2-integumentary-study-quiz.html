1. Structure of the skin

The three main layers

LayerWhat it isWhat's there / what it does
Epidermis (top)Five strata (layers) of cells; about 0.05 mm thick on the eyelids up to 1.5 mm on the feet.Its strata hold the waterproof barrier (granulosum) and the cells for pigment, immunity and touch (see the four cells below).
Dermis (middle)1–4 mm thick.Holds the sweat glands, oil-making, hair follicles, arrector pili muscles, pressure corpuscles, pain and itch receptors, blood vessels and nerves.
Hypodermis / subcutaneous (bottom)About 1 mm on the eyelids up to 3 cm on the abdomen or buttocks.Attaches the dermis to muscles and bones; has bigger blood vessels and nerves; its adipose (fat) gives insulation and protection.

Fingerprints

  • Three classes: arch (5% of people, no deltas; plain or tented), loop (60%, one delta; radial or ulnar), whorl (35%, at least two deltas; plain, central pocket, double loop, accidental — more than two deltas is most likely an accidental).
  • Radial vs. ulnar loop: named for their position relative to the radius and ulna bones. On the left hand, a loop opening to the left is ulnar; one opening to the right is radial.
  • Three principles: every print is individual; the pattern stays the same for life (the print itself can change from permanent scars or skin disease); patterns can be identified systematically.
  • Ridge characteristics are your points of identification when comparing two prints — more points in common means a better match. The ridge characteristics are on Mrs. Kalec's slide chart — use that.
  • Dactyloscopy = the study of fingerprint identification.

2. Functions of the skin

The skin's jobs

FunctionHow
ProtectionPhysical barrier against abrasion, bacteria, dehydration and UV light.
Temperature regulationSweat; muscle contraction in the blood vessels and arrector pili.
SensationReceptors for pain, pressure and temperature.
ExcretionWastes: ions, electrolytes, urea, amino acids.
ImmunityLangerhans cells (which come from bone marrow).
Blood reservoirModerate exercise: skin vessels expand and heat escapes. Strenuous exercise: they contract, sending that blood to the muscles.
Vitamin D synthesisUV light triggers it; vitamin D lets the body take in calcium, which it uses for bone and healthy tissue.

How dermal structures keep you in balance (homeostasis)

Skin helps hold body temperature steady using sweat and muscle contraction in the blood vessels and arrector pili.

Letting heat out ↑

  • Sweat glands make sweat.
  • Blood vessels expand (moderate exercise) → heat escapes.

Contracting ↓

  • Blood vessels contract (strenuous exercise) → blood goes to the muscles.
  • Arrector pili contract as part of temperature control.

3. Disorders & trauma

The four types of burns + healing

TypeReachesSignsHealing
1stEpidermis only (corneum and outer layers)Red, painful, mild swelling, no blistersHeals in 3–7 days as keratinocytes in the basale divide
2ndThrough the epidermis into the dermisBlisters (fluid between layers), severe pain, swellingHeals in 2–3 weeks; epidermis and parts of the dermis regrow; often scars
3rdDestroys all of the epidermis + dermis, reaching the subcutaneous layerWhite, charred or leathery; little to no pain (nerve damage)Can't heal on its own — melanocytes, hair follicles, sweat glands are lost → skin graft
4thBeyond skin into muscle, tendons, boneOften permanent damageNeeds immediate medical help; can't heal on its own → grafts
  • The healing process: inflammation starts right away — blood vessels dilate and white blood cells arrive to prevent infection. Fibroblasts in the dermis make collagen to rebuild tissue and close the wound. Too much collagen = a hypertrophic scar.
  • Myths: butter or oil (traps heat, raises infection risk) — ice (more tissue damage; use cool running water) — "all burns heal without scars" (2nd degree and deeper often scar).
  • Infection: risk jumps when the epidermis barrier is broken. Signs: more redness, pus, fever, red streaks from the wound.

Other infections from the notes, by type — viral: herpes, chicken pox, shingles, warts (HPV family; mostly feet or hands); bacterial: folliculitis, scalded skin syndrome, impetigo (strep; blisters or sores on face, neck, hands, diaper area; most common in kids); fungal: ringworm, athlete's foot, yeast infection; parasitic: swimmer's itch, scabies.

Skin cancer — types, causes, prevention

TypeWhat to know
Basal cell carcinomaMost common; grows slowly; easier to treat when caught early
Squamous cell carcinomaSecond most common; also grows fairly slowly
MelanomaMost dangerous — starts in melanocytes and can travel quickly to other parts of the body
  • Risk factors: family history, fair skin, a history of sunburns. Tanning beds raise melanoma risk by 75% for people under 35. For the rest of the "causes" exam bullet, use your class notes or ask Mrs. Kalec.
  • Prevention: limit UV — avoid peak sun (10 am–4 pm); broad-spectrum SPF 30+, reapplied every 2 hours; long sleeves, wide-brimmed hats, UV-blocking sunglasses; avoid tanning beds; check your skin monthly for new or changing moles.
