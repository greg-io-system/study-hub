2. The 6 FRQs

Each box: her prompt, then what a full answer covers, in our words, from her slides and her study app. Write your own answer first, then tick what you included.

FRQ 1 · Hypertrophy vs. atrophy

Describe the physiology of muscle hypertrophy and atrophy. Compare and contrast both processes.

Related: strength depends on fiber thickness and how many fibers contract at one time. Anabolic steroids act like testosterone and fool the body into building muscle, with irreversible side effects.

FRQ 2 · When oxygen runs short

During intense exercise, muscle cells may not receive enough oxygen to meet their energy demands. Explain how muscle cells respond to limited oxygen availability and describe the products of this process.

Memory trick: "no O₂, low dough": without oxygen the cell only earns 2 ATP, and the bill (lactic acid) gets paid later by heavy breathing.

FRQ 3 · Cardiac vs. smooth vs. skeletal

Identify and describe the major differences between cardiac, smooth, and skeletal muscle. Be sure to include location, structural characteristics, and functions.

The big idea: each muscle type has its fibers lined up differently so it can do its own job. Structure explains function.

FRQ 4 · The Sliding Filament Theory

Describe the Sliding Filament Theory. Include the structure of the muscle cell, the proteins involved, and the resulting actions.

Picture this: Mrs. Kalec calls the myosin heads molecular "rowers". The rowers (myosin heads) dip their oars into the water (actin), pull, lift out, reach forward and dip again. In a muscle, the "water" is what moves: the actin is pulled toward the middle of the sarcomere.

FRQ 5 · Muscles work in pairs

Why do muscles need to work in pairs? What role do prime movers, synergists, and antagonists play in muscle movement?

Picture this: a rope can pull a door shut but can't push it open. Each muscle is a rope, so every joint needs a second rope on the other side.

Try it: do a slow curl with your hand on your upper arm. Feel the biceps bunch up on the way up while the back of your arm stays soft.

FRQ 6 · Contraction, starting from the nerve

Explain the process of muscle contraction beginning with nervous stimulation.

Memory trick for the order: "Never Ask Rude Sodium To Carry": Nerve → ACh → Receptors → Sodium (Na⁺) → T-tubules → Calcium.

        4. Multiple-choice topics

        The rest of her review list. Hypertrophy/atrophy, lactic acid, the hierarchy, contraction and the movement roles are covered in the FRQ boxes above.

        Function, types and number of muscles

        • Functions: locomotion, moving food through the digestive system, pumping blood. Skeletal muscle also holds posture and balance and makes heat, which helps keep body temperature steady (homeostasis).
        • Muscle tone: muscles are always slightly contracted.
        • Number: between 640 and 850 muscles, depending on the source. Muscles are 40–50% of total body mass.
        • Largest: the gluteus maximus. The heart is a muscle. The fingers have no muscles: forearm muscles move them by pulling long tendons.
        • 3 types: smooth (organs and blood vessels, involuntary), cardiac (only the heart, involuntary), skeletal (attached to bone, voluntary).

        A muscle must be attached to at least 2 bones to create movement.

        Picture this (Mrs. Kalec's): pushing a box. The box is the bone that moves, the floor is the stationary bone, and you are the muscle. You can't move the box by sitting on it or without touching it; you have to touch the box and the floor.
        Memory trick: Insertion = the end that I move. Ligament Links bone to bone.

        How muscles are named

        By fiber direction, relative size, location, number of origins, where the origin and insertion are, shape, and action. Example from her slides: biceps femoris = a two-headed muscle attached to the femur.

        Rigor mortis

        • Stiffening of the muscles after death, due to chemical changes. Both voluntary and involuntary muscles stiffen.
        • Why: after death, cellular respiration stops and ATP runs out. Without ATP the myosin heads can't detach from actin, so the muscles lock.
        • Timeline: begins 1–2 hours after death → full at 10–12 hours → gone after 24–36 hours, when the muscle proteins break down. Usually starts in the upper body and moves down.
        • Used to estimate time of death.
        Link it: rigor mortis is the FRQ 4 rule in reverse. ATP is what lets a cross-bridge let go, so no ATP = stuck.
