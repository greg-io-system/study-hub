Study guide

What you should be able to do

  • Explain what turned settlements into civilizations — and name the features historians look for.
  • Describe ancient Mesopotamia: Sumer's city-states, cuneiform, religion, and the first empire (Sargon).
  • Describe ancient Egypt: the Nile, the pharaoh, and the Old / Middle Kingdom pattern with "Intermediate" periods between.
  • Describe the Indus valley cities and the mystery of their decline.
  • Compare the three river civilizations — what they shared and how they differed.
  • Go beyond the river valleys: explain Hammurabi's law code, Shang China, and the Olmec and Chavín of the Americas — and compare them.

What makes a "civilization"? (Section 3.1)

Farming let people settle; settling in large numbers produced civilizations — societies marked by:

  • urban settings (real cities),
  • specialization of labor (not everyone farms — you get artisans, scribes, priests, rulers),
  • social stratification (ranked classes: wealth and status separate people).

Your teacher's five characteristics of a civilization (Unit 1 study guide and the "5 Characteristics" slides) -- learn these for the class test: advanced cities, specialized workers, complex institutions, record keeping (writing), and advanced technology. In larger civilizations, priests and kings had the authority to command obedience, and farmers paid taxes to support them. Early Neolithic seeds of this: Jericho (its famous stone tower implies organized labor / some government) and Çatalhöyük in today's Turkey (whose people may have shared a mother-deity belief).

Mesopotamia — "the land between the rivers" (3.2)

Between the Tigris and Euphrates. Key points:

  • Uruk in Sumer was one of the first true cities (population as high as ~fifty thousand by the end of the 4th millennium BCE).
  • Independent city-states, each ruled by a lugal (king), often at war.
  • Inventions that spread: bronze-making (the Bronze Age) and cuneiform — writing made by pressing a reed stylus into wet clay.
  • Religion: many gods (polytheism) seen as fickle and easily angered; temples were stepped towers called ziggurats. The afterlife was imagined as a gloomy underworld.
  • Economy: temples and royal palaces were also economic redistribution centers. Ur-Nammu of the Third Dynasty of Ur issued an early law code.
  • Lugals rose to power mainly as warlords, since the city-states fought over farmland and water.
  • First empire: Sargon of Akkad conquered the city-states → the world's first-known empire. Later powers: the Third Dynasty of Ur and Hammurabi's Babylon (the Code of Hammurabi law code). The Epic of Gilgamesh survives from this world.

Egypt — the gift of the Nile (3.3)

Along the Nile, from the 4th millennium BCE.

  • Ruled by the pharaoh ("big house") — both political head and high priest, and revered as a god: the human form of Horus, god of justice and truth. Belief in Ma'at: order, truth, justice hold the universe together.
  • Polytheism and two scripts: hieroglyphics (symbols = sounds + ideas) and everyday hieratic, written on papyrus.
  • The kingdom cycle to memorize:
  • Early Dynastic Period — Narmer/Menes united Egypt; Imhotep built the first step pyramid for pharaoh Djoser, just before the Old Kingdom.
  • Old Kingdom — the age of pyramid building, above all the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza (Herodotus later wrote that it took 100,000 workers twenty years).
  • First Intermediate Period — nomarchs (regional governors) grabbed power → central control weakened.
  • Middle Kingdom — renewed strength and expansion; its height came under Senusret III (1870s–1860s BCE). Pharaohs introduced the cult of Amon-Re at Thebes and built massive temples instead of pyramids. Sobekneferu, the last ruler in the line of Amenemhat I, was the first woman to rule Egypt since before the Old Kingdom.
  • Second Intermediate Period — the Hyksos took the delta, Kush the far south → later reconquered to begin the New Kingdom.

Indus Valley — the well-planned cities (3.4)

  • Emerged early 3rd millennium BCE; great cities Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro (the civilization is called Harappan). Mesopotamians called the region Meluhha.
  • Famous for city planning: grid streets, drainage, a raised citadel (possible ceremonial center), and a large public bath at Mohenjo-Daro where priests' religious duties may have required them to bathe (the quiz calls it ritual cleansing). Traded lapis lazuli to Mesopotamia.
  • Religion: seals show a horned, three-faced figure surrounded by animals, much like the Hindu god Shiva as "Lord of the Animals," and there are goddess figurines -- but no evidence of temples or monumental tombs.
  • The script is undeciphered, so much is guesswork. Decline (~early 2nd millennium BCE; trade with Mesopotamia ended around 1800 BCE) is debated: climate change and shifting floodplains, environmental damage, river changes from earth movements, disease, or Indo-European–speaking newcomers — or a few or all of these together.

Social classes and gender (your teacher's study guide)

  • Upper class: rulers, priests, nobles, high-ranking officials.
  • Middle class: artisans, merchants, farmers, lower-level officials.
  • Lower class: enslaved people, prisoners of war, laborers.
  • Gender: patriarchal societies; upper-class women had a restricted home life, lower-class women worked in public. (The textbook, 3.2, adds that men and women were not equal under the Code of Hammurabi.)
  • Technology: irrigation systems, writing, metalworking, advanced building techniques. Sumer: modern-day Iraq, around 3500 BCE, city-states, cuneiform. Egypt: the Nile, a unified state under pharaohs, advanced irrigation, hieroglyphics.

Beyond the river valleys — what your class added

Heads-up on the textbook: in OpenStax these are spread across different chapters. Hammurabi is still in Ch. 3 (Mesopotamia), but Shang China is Ch. 5 and the Olmec and Chavín are Ch. 8. Same big idea — the first complex, city-building societies — just other regions and later pages.

Hammurabi's Code (Babylon, c. 1780 BCE)

  • Hammurabi was the energetic king of Babylon who beat his rivals and pulled nearly all of Mesopotamia back under one ruler.
  • His famous Code of Hammurabi was a set of laws carved on stone pillars (stelae) and set up around the empire so everyone could know the rules. (The most complete copy was found at Susa.)
  • It was remarkably clear and may have introduced ideas we still use — the presumption of innocence and the use of evidence in court.
  • Its signature principle: "an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth" — the punishment should fit the crime. But justice was not equal: that rule applied only within the same social class. Harm a fellow noble and you paid in kind; a noble who harmed a commoner might just pay a fine. Men and women were not equal under it either.
  • The stone even states its purpose: "so that the strong should not harm the weak."

Shang China (~1600–1045 BCE) — OpenStax Ch. 5

  • The Shang was the first Chinese dynasty we have solid proof of (people once thought it was a legend). A Bronze Age, socially ranked society.
  • Signature achievement: writing. We know the Shang were real because of oracle bones — questions about harvests, war, or an heir were carved onto turtle shells and animal bones, which the kings (themselves high priests) cracked with heat to read the spirits' answers. That script grew into Chinese writing.
  • Shang kings were both war-leaders and high priests, honoring their ancestors and a supreme god called Di. (Royal women could hold power too: Fu Hao was a general who led armies.)
  • Power came from war: bronze weapons, horse-drawn chariots, and walled towns. Captured enemies were enslaved or sacrificed — human sacrifice was central and grew to shocking scale.
  • The Shang fell ~1045 BCE to the Zhou, who justified the takeover with the Mandate of Heaven (a corrupt ruler loses heaven's right to rule).

The Olmec (Mesoamerica, ~1350–400 BCE) — OpenStax Ch. 8

  • Mesoamerica's first complex civilization, on the Gulf coast of Mexico — first at San Lorenzo, later La Venta.
  • Famous for monumental building: huge earthen platforms and colossal stone heads — up to 11 feet tall and 50 tons, carved from basalt hauled ~90 miles. They're thought to be portraits of rulers, advertising the power to command huge amounts of labor.
  • Farmed maize, beans, squash, and cacao (chocolate). A rich religion with a were-jaguar and a feathered serpent; leaders may have acted as shamans; they played a rubber-ball ritual game and practiced bloodletting.
  • Called a "mother culture" — their ideas about gods, art, the ball game, and the calendar spread by trade and shaped later peoples like the Maya and Aztec.

Chavín (the Andes, South America, ~900–200 BCE) — OpenStax Ch. 8

  • Around 900 BCE a single culture, the Chavín, spread across the Andes, opening what scholars call the Early Horizon.
  • Its heart was Chavín de Huántar, a mountain temple center over 10,000 feet up — not a big city but a ceremonial site — with a maze of tunnels and a fanged human-animal god statue, El Lanzón.
  • Known for a distinctive pottery style, plus advances in weaving and metalworking (gold, silver, copper); llamas carried goods along trade routes.
  • After ~200 BCE it broke apart into later Andean cultures — the Moche, Nazca, and Tiwanaku.

Norte Chico (coastal Peru) — OpenStax Ch. 8

  • Similar sites in central coastal Peru (Aspero, El Paraiso, and the largest, Caral) are grouped as one culture, the Norte Chico or Caral civilization, known for its many mounds.

  2. Who built the first-known empire, and where?
  3. What was cuneiform pressed into, and who invented it?
  4. Put these in order: Middle Kingdom, Old Kingdom, First Intermediate Period.
  5. Why do we know so little about how the Indus cities were governed?
  6. What everyday-court ideas may Hammurabi's Code have introduced — and why was its "eye for an eye" not equal for everyone?
  7. What evidence proved the Shang dynasty was real, not a legend?
  8. Why is the Olmec called a "mother culture," and what did the Chavín's Chavín de Huántar mainly serve as?

Common traps

  • City-state vs. empire: Sumer = many independent city-states; Sargon's Akkad = the first empire (many under one ruler). Don't blur them.
  • Old vs. Middle Kingdom: Old = pyramids; Middle = renewed strength/expansion. The Intermediate Periods are the weak gaps between them.
  • The pharaoh was political AND religious (high priest) — not just a king.
  • The Indus script is undeciphered — that's why so much about them is "maybe."
  • Hammurabi's "eye for an eye" was class-based — equal payback only within the same social rank, not across ranks. Don't call it equal justice.
  • Of the three "beyond" civilizations, only the Shang is known to us through its own writing (oracle bones) in the textbook; the Olmec and Chavín are read through art and ruins.
  • Chavín de Huántar was a temple/ceremonial center, not a big city — its importance was religious, not its population.

Part B — Short answer (model answers; 2 pts each)

Jericho & Çatalhöyük (3.1): Jericho's stone tower and walls imply some form of government organizing labor (the tower may even have been an observatory); a food surplus freed specialists for work like bull sacrifices and frescoes; Jericho's plastered skulls suggest ancestor veneration, and Çatalhöyük's figurines and bull horns (bucrania) a possible mother deity -- signs of a shared belief system or unity behind a leader, in towns of up to about six thousand people. The text adds that we may simply never know for sure. (1 pt organization/government, 1 pt shared religion/culture.)

19

What was the Legend of Sargon, and how does it explain Sargon of Akkad's rise to power?

Show answer

Legend of Sargon (3.2): his unwed mother put him in a basket and cast it on the Euphrates; a farmer found and raised him, and the goddess Ishtar raised him up from a commoner to rule. The text calls the legend "a powerful piece of propaganda justifying Sargon's rule": a god chose him despite lowly birth. (1 pt the basket-on-the-river / humble-origin story, 1 pt that it explains/justifies his rise to power.)

20

How were gods honored and served in the Sumerian religion?

Show answer

Sumerian religion (3.2): people supplied the gods with food through sacrifices of sheep and cattle and honored them with temples (ziggurats), hymns, and expensive gifts; each city had its patron god. Commoners honored the gods by living virtuously and giving to the poor, priests performed the rituals, and rulers funded and beautified the temples. (1 pt temple/ziggurat + priests, 1 pt offerings/rituals to care for the gods.)

21

In what respects were the Sumerian lugals and Egyptian pharaohs different?

Show answer

Lugals vs. pharaohs (3.2/3.3): a Sumerian lugal was the king of a single city-state, one ruler among many rival cities, who rose to power primarily as a warlord in wars over farmland and water (some later lugals of Ur were also honored as gods). An Egyptian pharaoh ruled a unified kingdom, was high priest, and was revered as a god -- the human form of Horus, god of justice and truth. (1 pt scale — one city-state vs. unified Egypt, 1 pt the pharaoh's divine/religious role.)

22

What are some of the theories about the decline of the Indus valley civilization?

Show answer

Indus decline theories (3.4): any of — changes in river courses or floodplains, arrival of Indo-European–speaking nomads, climate change, environmental degradation, disease, or a combination of these. Because the script is undeciphered, the cause is uncertain. (2 pts for naming two or more valid theories and noting the uncertainty.)
