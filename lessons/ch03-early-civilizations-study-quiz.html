Study guide

What you should be able to do

  • Explain what turned settlements into civilizations — and name the features historians look for.
  • Describe ancient Mesopotamia: Sumer's city-states, cuneiform, religion, and the first empire (Sargon).
  • Describe ancient Egypt: the Nile, the pharaoh, and the Old / Middle Kingdom pattern with "Intermediate" periods between.
  • Describe the Indus valley cities and the mystery of their decline.
  • Compare the three river civilizations — what they shared and how they differed.
  • Go beyond the river valleys: explain Hammurabi's law code, Shang China, and the Olmec and Chavín of the Americas — and compare them.

What makes a "civilization"? (Section 3.1)

Farming let people settle; settling in large numbers produced civilizations — societies marked by:

  • urban settings (real cities),
  • specialization of labor (not everyone farms — you get artisans, scribes, priests, rulers),
  • social stratification (ranked classes: wealth and status separate people).

Early Neolithic seeds of this: Jericho (its famous stone tower implies organized labor / some government) and Çatalhöyük in today's Turkey (whose people may have shared a mother-deity belief).

Mesopotamia — "the land between the rivers" (3.2)

Between the Tigris and Euphrates. Key points:

  • Uruk in Sumer was one of the first true cities (population as high as ~fifty thousand by the end of the 4th millennium BCE).
  • Independent city-states, each ruled by a lugal (king), often at war.
  • Inventions that spread: bronze-making (the Bronze Age) and cuneiform — writing made by pressing a reed stylus into wet clay.
  • Religion: many gods (polytheism) seen as fickle and easily angered; temples were stepped towers called ziggurats. The afterlife was imagined as a gloomy underworld.
  • First empire: Sargon of Akkad conquered the city-states → the world's first-known empire. Later powers: the Third Dynasty of Ur and Hammurabi's Babylon (the Code of Hammurabi law code). The Epic of Gilgamesh survives from this world.

Egypt — the gift of the Nile (3.3)

Along the Nile, from the 4th millennium BCE.

  • Ruled by the pharaoh ("big house") — both political head and high priest. Belief in Ma'at: order, truth, justice hold the universe together.
  • Polytheism and two scripts: hieroglyphics (symbols = sounds + ideas) and everyday hieratic, written on papyrus.
  • The kingdom cycle to memorize:
  • Old Kingdom — the age of pyramid building (Imhotep built the first step pyramid for pharaoh Djoser; Narmer/Menes had earlier united Egypt).
  • First Intermediate Period — nomarchs (regional governors) grabbed power → central control weakened.
  • Middle Kingdom — renewed strength and expansion; ends with Sobekneferu, the first woman to rule Egypt since before the Old Kingdom.
  • Second Intermediate Period — the Hyksos took the delta, Kush the far south → later reconquered to begin the New Kingdom.

Indus Valley — the well-planned cities (3.4)

  • Emerged early 3rd millennium BCE; great cities Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro (the civilization is called Harappan). Mesopotamians called the region Meluhha.
  • Famous for city planning: grid streets, drainage, a raised citadel (possible ceremonial center), and the Great Bath at Mohenjo-Daro (likely ritual cleansing). Traded lapis lazuli to Mesopotamia.
  • The script is undeciphered, so much is guesswork. Decline (~early 2nd millennium BCE) is debated: shifting rivers, trade disruption, climate change, environmental damage, disease, or Indo-European–speaking newcomers — probably a combination.

Beyond the river valleys — what your class added

Your teacher is treating "early civilizations" as a worldwide theme, so the class went past the three river valleys above to Hammurabi's Babylon, Shang China, and two civilizations in the Americas — the Olmec and Chavín. Heads-up on the textbook: in OpenStax these are spread across different chapters. Hammurabi is still in Ch. 3 (Mesopotamia), but Shang China is Ch. 5 and the Olmec and Chavín are Ch. 8. Same big idea — the first complex, city-building societies — just other regions and later pages.

Hammurabi's Code (Babylon, ~1750 BCE)

  • Hammurabi was the energetic king of Babylon who beat his rivals and pulled nearly all of Mesopotamia back under one ruler.
  • His famous Code of Hammurabi was a set of laws carved on tall stone pillars (stelae) and set up around the empire so everyone could know the rules. (The most complete copy was found at Susa.)
  • It was remarkably clear and may have introduced ideas we still use — the presumption of innocence and the use of evidence in court.
  • Its signature principle: "an eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth" — the punishment should fit the crime. But justice was not equal: that rule applied only within the same social class. Harm a fellow noble and you paid in kind; a noble who harmed a commoner might just pay a fine. Men and women were not equal under it either.
  • The stone even states its purpose: "so that the strong should not harm the weak."

Shang China (~1600–1045 BCE) — OpenStax Ch. 5

  • The Shang was the first Chinese dynasty we have solid proof of (people once thought it was a legend). A Bronze Age, socially ranked society.
  • Signature achievement: writing. We know the Shang were real because of oracle bones — questions about harvests, war, or an heir were carved onto turtle shells and animal bones, which priests then cracked with heat to read the spirits' answers. That script grew into Chinese writing.
  • Shang kings were both war-leaders and high priests, honoring their ancestors and a supreme god called Di. (One queen, Fu Hao, was also a general who led armies.)
  • Power came from war: bronze weapons, horse-drawn chariots, and walled towns. Captured enemies were enslaved or sacrificed — human sacrifice was central and grew to shocking scale.
  • The Shang fell ~1045 BCE to the Zhou, who justified the takeover with the Mandate of Heaven (a corrupt ruler loses heaven's right to rule).

The Olmec (Mesoamerica, ~1350–400 BCE) — OpenStax Ch. 8

  • Mesoamerica's first complex civilization, on the Gulf coast of Mexico — first at San Lorenzo, later La Venta.
  • Famous for monumental building with no metal tools: huge earthen platforms and colossal stone heads — up to 11 feet tall and 50 tons, carved from basalt hauled ~90 miles. They're thought to be portraits of rulers, advertising the power to command huge amounts of labor.
  • Farmed maize, beans, squash, and cacao (chocolate). A rich religion with a were-jaguar and a feathered serpent; leaders may have acted as shamans; they played a rubber-ball ritual game and practiced bloodletting.
  • Called a "mother culture" — their ideas about gods, art, the ball game, and the calendar spread by trade and shaped later peoples like the Maya and Aztec. (No decipherable Olmec writing survives.)

Chavín (the Andes, South America, ~900–200 BCE) — OpenStax Ch. 8

  • Around 900 BCE a single culture, the Chavín, spread across the Andes, opening what scholars call the Early Horizon.
  • Its heart was Chavín de Huántar, a mountain temple center over 10,000 feet up — not a big city but a ceremonial site — with a maze of tunnels and a fanged human-animal god statue, El Lanzón.
  • Known for a distinctive pottery style, plus advances in weaving and metalworking (gold, silver, copper); llamas carried goods along trade routes.
  • After ~200 BCE it broke apart into later Andean cultures — the Moche, Nazca, and Tiwanaku.

The shared thread: all three show the marks of civilization from 3.1 — cities or big ceremonial centers, specialized labor (artisans, priests, rulers), and social ranking — even though they never met one another.

Quick self-check

  1. Name the three features that define a "civilization."
  2. Who built the first-known empire, and where?
  3. What was cuneiform pressed into, and who invented it?
  4. Put these in order: Middle Kingdom, Old Kingdom, First Intermediate Period.
  5. Why do we know so little about how the Indus cities were governed?
  6. What everyday-court ideas may Hammurabi's Code have introduced — and why was its "eye for an eye" not equal for everyone?
  7. What evidence proved the Shang dynasty was real, not a legend?
  8. Why is the Olmec called a "mother culture," and what did the Chavín's Chavín de Huántar mainly serve as?

Common traps

  • City-state vs. empire: Sumer = many independent city-states; Sargon's Akkad = the first empire (many under one ruler). Don't blur them.
  • Old vs. Middle Kingdom: Old = pyramids; Middle = renewed strength/expansion. The Intermediate Periods are the weak gaps between them.
  • The pharaoh was political AND religious (high priest) — not just a king.
  • The Indus script is undeciphered — that's why so much about them is "maybe."
  • Hammurabi's "eye for an eye" was class-based — equal payback only within the same social rank, not across ranks. Don't call it equal justice.
  • Only the Shang left writing of the three "beyond" civilizations — the Olmec and Chavín left no readable script, so we read them through art and ruins.
  • Chavín de Huántar was a temple/ceremonial center, not a big city — its importance was religious, not its population.

What do the artifacts and other archaeological finds from Jericho and Çatalhöyük suggest about life in these early urban environments?

Part B — Short answer (model answers; 2 pts each)

Jericho & Çatalhöyük (3.1): finds like Jericho's massive stone tower/walls and Çatalhöyük's dense mud-brick housing, wall paintings, and figurines suggest these settlements had organized labor, some form of governance or shared authority, trade, and shared religious/ritual belief (e.g., a possible mother-deity at Çatalhöyük) — i.e., the beginnings of urban, stratified life rather than simple villages. (1 pt organization/government, 1 pt shared religion/culture.)

19

What was the Legend of Sargon, and how does it explain Sargon of Akkad's rise to power?

Show answer

Legend of Sargon (3.2): a later origin story in which Sargon was born to a priestess, set adrift on the river in a reed basket, rescued and raised by a gardener, and rose from humble beginnings (cupbearer) to seize power and build his empire. It legitimizes his rule by casting his rise as destined / divinely favored despite lowly birth. (1 pt the basket-on-the-river / humble-origin story, 1 pt that it explains/justifies his rise to power.)

20

How were gods honored and served in the Sumerian religion?

Show answer

Sumerian religion (3.2): the gods were served much like human lords — housed in temples (ziggurats), fed, clothed, and tended by priests through daily rituals, offerings, and sacrifices, with festivals and hymns in their honor; each city had its patron deity. (1 pt temple/ziggurat + priests, 1 pt offerings/rituals to care for the gods.)

21

In what respects were the Sumerian lugals and Egyptian pharaohs different?

Show answer

Lugals vs. pharaohs (3.2/3.3): a Sumerian lugal was the king of a single city-state, one ruler among many rival cities, primarily a military/political leader (not usually regarded as a god). An Egyptian pharaoh ruled a unified kingdom, was both political head and high priest, and was regarded as divine / semi-divine (guarantor of Ma'at). (1 pt scale — one city-state vs. unified Egypt, 1 pt the pharaoh's divine/religious role.)

22

What are some of the theories about the decline of the Indus valley civilization?

Show answer

Indus decline theories (3.4): any of — trade disruptions, changes in river courses, arrival of Indo-European–speaking nomads, climate change, environmental degradation, disease, or a combination of these. Because the script is undeciphered, the cause is uncertain. (2 pts for naming two or more valid theories and noting the uncertainty.)
