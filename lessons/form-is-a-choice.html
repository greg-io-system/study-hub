Form Is a Choice

When Mr. Evans asks "is this a memoir or an argument?" (or narrative or analysis, story or essay…), the label is the easy half. The points are in why.

The one idea: a writer's form is a choice, not a category. A memoir can BE an argument — the story is the vehicle for the claim. So don't stop at "it's a memoir." Ask: why did the writer choose this form, and how does it serve the argument? That's the same "choice → purpose → effect" move you use for a device (the "in order to" formula) — just zoomed out to the whole text.

Weak label vs. real answer

Just a label (not enough)
"This is a memoir because it tells the author's personal story." — True, and it stops at the category. No points for naming a genre.
Classify + function (what he wants)
"It's memoir in form but argument in purpose: the writer chose to tell his own story in order to make his claim feel earned and hard to dismiss — you can't argue with what someone lived."

The answer pattern (3 moves)

[Which form], because [the feature that makes it that] —
but the writer chose it in order to [purpose / effect on the reader].

Use Mr. Evans's Rogerian scaffold to hold both sides in one sentence:

"While it reads as a ___, it is really an argument, because ___."

The "because" clause is required — a classification with no reason earns nothing. And "form is really argument" is usually the stronger, more sophisticated read: writers rarely tell a story just to tell it.

Worked example (on a made-up text, so your real one stays yours)

The text

A writer recounts the summer they failed their driving test three times before finally passing. No thesis sentence — just the story, told with humor and frustration.

Weak answer

"It's a memoir because it's a personal story about the author's life."

Developed answer

"While it takes the form of a memoir, it is really an argument, because the writer selects and shapes the events to prove a point — each failed test builds toward what persistence taught them. The personal-story form is a choice made in order to make the claim feel earned and relatable rather than preachy: we trust the lesson because we watched it cost something."

See the moves: names the form → "because" (events shaped to prove a point) → "in order to" (why that form works on the reader).

Its partner question: find the central claim

Classification almost always comes paired with "what is the central claim?" In a narrative the claim is usually unstated — there's no thesis sentence to copy. Find it by asking:

  • What is this story for? What does the writer want you to believe or do after reading?
  • Every scene is chosen — what point do the scenes add up to?
  • State it as one arguable sentence (something a reasonable person could disagree with). For the driving-test text: "failure is a necessary teacher, not a verdict."

An unstated claim is a bold move by the writer — naming it clearly is the skill.

Before you answer — the check

  • Did I name the form AND give a "because"?
  • Did I say what the form is "in order to" do — its effect on the reader?
  • Did I consider that the form serves an argument (usually the stronger read), not just "it's a story"?
  • Central claim: did I state it as one arguable sentence, even though the text never says it outright?
  • Did I anchor it with direct evidence — a specific moment from the text, not a summary?

Reusable method — it works for any "is this X or Y, and what's the claim?" question. The driving-test text is made up so the moves are modeled without touching your assigned reading.
