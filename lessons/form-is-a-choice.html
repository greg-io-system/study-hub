Form Is a Choice

Mr. Evans asked (9/15, and again in class 9/17): "Would you classify this essay more as memoir or an argument? Your answer would be incomplete without a 'because' clause after your stated answer."

The one idea: the label is only half an answer — the "because" is the other half. And either way, there is a claim: "Whether or not Rose is writing a memoir or an argument, he must be making a claim" (9/15). His 9/17 feedback gives the reading move: if you were entertained by the stories, "you must now analyze this entertainment as a choice and not an end in itself; rather, it is a means with an end in mind." That is the same "in order to" thinking he teaches for strategies (9/14: explain "the intent, the use, and the e/affect") — applied to the form of the whole text.

Label only vs. label + because

Made-up example — both answers are about the made-up driving-test text below, not your reading.

Just a label
"It's a memoir." — No "because" clause, so by his standard the answer is incomplete.
Label + because
"It reads more as a memoir because the writer only tells what happened to her and never states a lesson outright."

The answer pattern

[Memoir or argument], because [what in the text makes you say so].

He pointed you to the language of the Rogerian for this question (9/15):

"While_______"   "; However, "

His 9/17 feedback on the Rogerian and the Precis: "There is not a single way to approach any of these questions, and these tools give you the language to support whatever direction you take." Either classification can work — the "because" and the evidence are what make it complete.

Try it: worked example on a made-up text

Made-up text

A writer recounts the summer she failed her driving test three times before finally passing. No thesis sentence — just the story, told with humor and frustration.

Weak answer

"It's a memoir because it's a personal story." (A "because" — but it only restates the label.)

Developed answer A (argues "argument")

"While the essay takes the shape of a memoir, never stepping out of the story to state a lesson; however, it works as an argument, because each failed test is chosen and ordered to build toward what persistence taught her. She tells it as a story in order to make the lesson feel earned rather than lectured."

Developed answer B (argues "memoir")

"While the essay ends on a lesson about persistence; however, it stays a memoir, because most of its pages go to the humor and frustration of the three tests rather than to proving a point. She tells it as a story in order to share the experience, with the lesson as a closing note rather than the reason for writing."

See the moves in both: names the form → "While ... ; however," → "because" → "in order to." Either side can be defended; the moves and the evidence are what count.

Its partner question: the central claim

He asked you to combine the classification and the central claim into a single response (9/15). On 9/17 he added:

"A claim should be a sentence within the piece. Most times they are directly stated. An unstated claim is a bold move. [This is also writing advice: State your claims]"

Try it — guiding questions for finding a claim in a story:

  • What is this story for? What does the writer want you to believe after reading?
  • Every scene is chosen — what point do the scenes add up to?
  • Don't stop at the title. His 9/17 walkthrough: "Title: NOT the claim."
  • Is there a sentence in the piece that says it? If not, state it yourself in one sentence. (Made-up text: "failure is a teacher, not a verdict.")

Before you answer — the check

  • Did I name the form AND give a "because" clause?
  • Did I use the Rogerian language ("While ... ; however,") where it helps?
  • Did I treat the form as a choice — what it is "in order to" do?
  • Central claim: did I state it as one sentence and combine it with the classification in a single response?
  • Did I use DIRECT evidence from the text? (9/15: "the development of your responses depends on your use of DIRECT evidence from the text")

Reusable method for any "memoir or argument, and what's the claim?" question. The driving-test text is made up so the moves are modeled without touching your assigned reading.
