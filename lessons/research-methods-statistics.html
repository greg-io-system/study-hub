The big picture

Three questions, that's all statistics is here

When a psychologist has a pile of numbers (reaction times, test scores, hours of sleep), descriptive statistics answer three questions:

QuestionTool
Where's the center? (the "typical" score)mean, median, mode
How spread out is it?range, standard deviation

Keep this frame. Center → spread → relationship. If you know which question a term answers, you already half-understand it.

What each one means (in plain words)

  • Mean — add them all up, divide by how many. The everyday "average." It gets dragged by extreme scores.
  • Median — line the scores up in order; the middle one. Half are below, half above. It ignores how extreme the outliers are — that's why it's the honest "typical" when data is lopsided (like income).
  • Mode — the score that appears most often. The only one you can use for non-number categories (favorite color).
Classic test question: "A billionaire walks into a room of 10 teachers — which measure of center changes the most?" The mean (it chases the outlier); the median barely budges. Drag a dot to the far right above and see it happen.
Spread

Range vs. standard deviation

Range

The crudest spread measure: highest − lowest. Fast, but one weird score wrecks it — it only looks at the two extremes.

Standard deviation (SD)

The good one: roughly the typical distance of a score from the mean. Small SD = everyone's bunched near the average; big SD = scores are all over the place.

You won't compute SD by hand on the test as much as interpret it: "Class A had the same mean as Class B but a smaller SD" → Class A's scores were more consistent. Drag the dots above together (SD shrinks) or spread them apart (SD grows) to feel it.

The shape data loves

The normal curve & the 68–95–99.7 rule

Lots of natural measurements (height, IQ, reaction time) pile up in a symmetric bell curve. In a perfect one, mean = median = mode, all dead center.

Memorize the rule: about 68% of scores fall within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD, 99.7% within 3 SD. Example: IQ has mean 100, SD 15 → about 68% of people score between 85 and 115.
When the bell tips over

Skew — and where the mean runs off to

Real data isn't always symmetric. A skew is a tail pulled out to one side. The name comes from where the tail points, and the one rule that matters:

the mean gets pulled toward the tail.

median left of mean. Ex: income (a few billionaires pull the mean up).

mean left of median. Ex: an easy exam (most score high, a few low scores pull the mean down).

Memory hook: the skew is named for the tail, and the mean chases the tail. Positive skew → tail & mean to the right. This is the same outlier effect you dragged in the dot plot.
Do two things go together?

Correlation

A correlation coefficient (r) is one number from −1 to +1 that measures how tightly two variables move together. Drag the slider and watch the cloud of points tighten and tilt.

+0.60

Reading r — two parts

Sign (direction)Size (strength)
+ both rise together (more sleep, better mood)
− one rises as the other falls (more stress, less sleep)		near ±1 = tight, points hug the line
near 0 = no relationship, random cloud
The one they'll test hardest — correlation ≠ causation. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths rise together (r > 0), but ice cream doesn't cause drowning — summer heat drives both (a "third variable"). A correlation shows two things move together; it cannot prove one causes the other. Only a controlled experiment (with random assignment) can support cause.
Test yourself

Quick check (answer, then reveal)

1. Which measure of center is most affected by an outlier?

The mean — it's calculated from every value, so an extreme score drags it. The median (position-based) barely moves.

2. Scores: 4, 4, 5, 6, 100. Mean vs. median?

Mean = 119/5 = 23.8; median = 5. The 100 is an outlier — the median is the honest "typical" score here.

3. IQ has mean 100, SD 15. Roughly what % score between 85 and 115?

85–115 is ±1 SD → about 68% (the 68–95–99.7 rule).

4. A test's scores are negatively (left) skewed. Where's the mean vs. the median?

The tail points left (a few low scores), so the mean is pulled left, below the median. Most students did well; a few low scores dragged the average down.

5. r = −0.85 means…

A strong, negative relationship — as one variable goes up the other goes down, and the points hug the line tightly. (Sign = direction, size = strength.)

6. Hours of TV and grades correlate negatively. Does TV cause bad grades?

Not necessarily — correlation ≠ causation. A third variable (less studying, home environment) could drive both. Only an experiment can show cause.
