Statistics, made intuitive

Psychologists collect a lot of data, and statistics is the tool that turns data into information. Every tool here answers a plain question — play with the pictures and the formulas stop being scary.

The big picture

Three questions, that's all statistics is here

Descriptive statistics summarize and describe the data a study collected. Inferential statistics use data from a sample to make predictions or generalizations about a larger population. The descriptive tools answer three questions:

QuestionTool
Where's the center? (the "typical" score — measures of central tendency)mean, median, mode
How spread out is it? (measures of variability, or dispersion)range, standard deviation
Do two things go together?correlation
Keep this frame. Center → spread → relationship. If you know which question a term answers, you already half-understand it.

What each one means (in plain words)

  • Mean — add them all up, divide by how many. The everyday "average" — the most commonly used measure, because it's the most sensitive. It gets dragged by extreme scores.
  • Median — line the scores up in order; the middle one (the 50th percentile). With an even number of scores, average the two middle ones: 1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 4 → (2+3)/2 = 2.5. Outliers affect it much less than the mean — that's why income is reported as the median.
  • Mode — the score that appears most often. A set can have one mode (unimodal), several (multimodal), or none (if every value occurs equally often). Outliers don't affect it at all.
Try it: drag one dot far to the right. The mean chases the outlier (it's the measure most impacted by outliers); the median moves much less; the mode doesn't move.
Spread

Range vs. standard deviation

Range

The simplest spread measure: highest − lowest. It uses only the two extreme scores.

Standard deviation (SD)

How broadly or narrowly the scores spread out from the mean. It's found from how far each score is from the mean, and it's in the same units as the scores. Small SD = scores cluster near the average; large SD = scores are spread out. Symbol: σ (sigma).

Try it: "Class A had the same mean as Class B but a smaller SD" → Class A's scores were bunched closer together. Drag the dots above together (SD shrinks) or spread them apart (SD grows) to feel it.

The shape data loves

The normal curve & the 68–95–99.5 rule

In a normal distribution (the bell curve), most scores cluster around the mean and there are fewer and fewer the farther you go out. It is symmetrical, so mean = median = mode, all at the peak. Intelligence (IQ) is the class example.

Memorize it the way class taught it: about 34% of scores fall between the mean and 1 SD (each side), about 13–14% between 1 and 2 SD, and about 2% between 2 and 3 SD. So about 68% are within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD, and 99.5% within 3 SD. Class example: IQ has a mean of 100 and an SD of 10, so 1 SD below is 90 and 1 SD above is 110.

Percentile rank = the percentage of scores at or below a given score — a way to compare one person's score with a group. On the class bell curve, −2 SD ≈ 2nd percentile, −1 SD ≈ 16th, the mean = 50th, +1 SD ≈ 84th, +2 SD ≈ 98th.

A bimodal distribution has two peaks (two modes) — a sign the data may hold two distinct groups. (Class example: heights of a group of teens clustering at 5′7″ and 5′9″.)

When the bell tips over

Skew — and where the mean runs off to

Not every distribution is symmetric. Skewed means pulled — extreme scores pull a tail out to one side. (Only distributions are skewed, not the data themselves.) The name comes from where the tail points, and the one rule that matters:

the mean gets pulled toward the tail.

median left of mean. Most scores are low; a few very high ones stretch the mean up. Ex: a very hard exam where most do poorly but one or two do very well.

mean left of median. Ex: an easy exam (most score high, a few low scores pull the mean down).

Memory trick: the skew is named for the tail, and the mean chases the tail. Outliers skew distributions: one high outlier → positive skew; a low outlier → negative skew. The median is less affected, the mode not at all — so in a highly skewed distribution the median is the best "typical" score.
Do two things go together?

Correlation

A correlation coefficient (r) is one number from −1 to +1 showing how strongly two variables are related. Correlations are shown on a scatterplot (the variable you think predicts goes on the x-axis — often called the independent variable — and the one being predicted on the y-axis, often called the dependent variable). Drag the slider and watch the cloud of points tighten and tilt.

Correlation ≠ causation — correlations predict. Class example: kids at a birthday party who eat more sugar seem more hyperactive, but the excitement of the party, the loud games and music, or parents expecting hyperactivity could explain it — the third variable problem. There's also the directionality problem: people who retire later are healthier, but maybe working longer keeps you healthy, or maybe less healthy people retire earlier. Only an experiment (manipulating a variable, with random assignment) can show cause and effect.

Correlational research — strengths: shows how strong a relationship is and can predict; can use existing (archival) data; works for variables you can't or shouldn't manipulate; can dispel illusory correlations. Weaknesses: nothing is manipulated, so it can't show cause or which way the effect runs, and it's hard to judge the effect of other variables.

Is the result real — and does it matter?

Significance, effect size and regression to the mean

  • Statistical significance — how likely it is that a result is not due to chance. It's tested with statistical tests such as t-tests and shown by a p-value; below the cutoff (conventionally .05 or .01) the result is called statistically significant, meaning a repeat of the study should give comparable results. Larger samples make a chance result less likely. It never proves a hypothesis true — it's about probability.
  • Effect size — how big the effect is. About .8 and above = large; .2 and below = small. Significance says an effect exists; effect size says whether it's large enough to matter in the real world.
  • Meta-analysis — statistically combining the results of several studies (effect sizes are widely used in meta-analysis).
  • Regression to the mean — an extreme score tends to be closer to average when measured again, because extremes are often due to temporary factors.
Test yourself

Quick check (answer, then reveal)

1. Which measure of center is most affected by an outlier?

The mean — it's calculated from every value, so an extreme score drags it. The median moves much less, and the mode not at all.

2. Scores: 4, 4, 5, 6, 100. Mean vs. median?

Mean = 119/5 = 23.8; median = 5. The 100 is an outlier — the median is the honest "typical" score here.

3. In the class IQ example the mean is 100 and the SD is 10. Roughly what % score between 90 and 110?

90–110 is ±1 SD → about 68% (34% on each side of the mean).

4. A test's scores are negatively (left) skewed. Where's the mean vs. the median?

The tail points left (a few low scores), so the mean is pulled left, below the median. Most students did well; a few low scores dragged the average down.

5. r = −0.85 means…

A strong, negative relationship — as one variable goes up the other goes down, and the points hug the line tightly. (Sign = direction, size = strength.)

6. A study finds that kids who eat more sugar at a party are more hyperactive. Does sugar cause it?

Not necessarily — correlation ≠ causation. A third variable (party excitement, loud games, parents' expectations) could explain it. Only an experiment can show cause.

7. A result is statistically significant but has an effect size of .1. What does that tell you?

The effect probably isn't due to chance, but it's small (.2 and below), so it may not matter much in the real world.

8. On the class bell curve, a score 1 SD above the mean is at about which percentile?

About the 84th percentile (50% below the mean + 34%).
