Hammurabi's Code: Order, Kingship & the Gods

Two ideas do most of the work here: what the Code was for, and where a king said his power came from. Then the laws themselves show you what Babylonian society looked like.

The one-minute version

Hammurabi became king of Babylon in 1792 BCE and ruled until 1750 BCE. He united Mesopotamia under the Babylonian Empire. His law code (about 1780 BCE) is a list of 282 laws, one of the oldest law codes in the world, carved on stone pillars set up in different places across the empire. Two things to hold onto:
  • It was created in part to help unify a quickly expanding empire — to tell his subjects how to behave and what the laws of the land were.
  • Hammurabi said his right to rule came from the gods. That link between religion and government is the point of this lesson.

1 · What was it for?

Before the Code

Individuals basically decided for themselves how to get revenge on people who hurt them.

With the Code

The government set certain punishments for certain crimes, written down and posted across a big new empire, so one set of rules held it together.

Your First Civilizations notes describe this same move: states increasingly took over the use of violence from individuals.

The Code itself says Hammurabi was called "to bring about the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak." (OpenStax 3.2)

OpenStax adds that the Code was unusually clear, may have introduced ideas like the presumption of innocence and the use of evidence, and shaped Mesopotamian law for centuries after him.

2 · Where did his power come from? (religion & government)

In the First Civilizations there were two centers of power: the temple and the palace. Kings sought religious authority, and states were presented as normal, natural and ordained by the gods.

In the Code's introduction, the gods Anu and Bel give Marduk rule over the earth and make Babylon great, and then "called by name me, Hammurabi" to rule. So Hammurabi presents himself as a divine-right king: his power, and by extension his laws, come from the gods, so they should be obeyed. During his dynasty Marduk became the patron god of Babylonia.

The top of the stele shows Hammurabi with a god. Your teacher's slide and OpenStax 3.2 name him Shamash, the sun god; OpenStax 4.1 says the seated god is "likely Marduk." Either way the picture says the same thing: his right to rule comes from the heavens.

The same pattern shows up across the First Civilizations: Egyptian pharaohs were gods, Zhou emperors ruled by the Mandate of Heaven, and Hammurabi claimed the divine right of his laws. And since the introduction names several gods (Anu, Bel, Ea, Shamash), it is also evidence that the Babylonians were polytheistic.

3 · What the laws show about Babylonian society

The Code follows the idea that punishment should fit the crime ("an eye for an eye") — but penalties depended on social class. The Code names three classes: nobles (awelum), commoners (mushkenum) and the enslaved (wardum). Laws from the handout you read in class:

LawWhat it says (in short)What it shows
196Put out a man's eye → your eye is put out.Equal retaliation between equals
199Put out the eye of a man's slave → pay half the slave's value.Enslaved people treated as property
202 / 203Strike someone of higher rank → 60 blows with an ox-whip in public. Strike an equal → pay one gold mina.Rank changes the penalty
205A slave who strikes a freed man has his ear cut off.Harsh control of the enslaved
117A debtor who sells himself and his family works 3 years; freed in the 4th.Debt slavery, but with a limit
229–231A builder whose house collapses and kills the owner is put to death; if it kills a slave, he pays slave for slave.Responsibility — and class again
53–55A careless dam or ditch that floods neighbors' fields → repay their corn.Irrigation and property mattered
132A wife accused (not caught) of adultery must "jump into the river" for her husband.Women judged differently from men

OpenStax also says a noble who injured a commoner could expect to merely pay a fine, and that men and women were not equal under the Code. So the "eye for an eye" laws are evidence of a divided society, not an equal one.

Your teacher's own example reads "so that the strong should not harm the weak" as an early equal protection idea among free citizens — while noting Babylon struggled to live up to it. His closing questions: was Babylonian society egalitarian or divided, and what are the limits of the Code as a historical source?
