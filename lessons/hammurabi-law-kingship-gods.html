Hammurabi's Code: Order, Kingship & the Gods

Two ideas do most of the work here: what the Code was for, and where a king said his power came from. Get those two and the rest falls into place.

The one-minute version

Around 1750 BCE, Hammurabi, king of Babylon, had about 282 laws carved onto a tall stone pillar (a stele) and set up in public. It is one of the earliest written law codes we have. Two things to hold onto:
  • Its purpose was to create a stable, orderly society — a shared, public standard of justice so disputes weren't settled by whoever was stronger.
  • Hammurabi said his right to rule and to make law came from the gods. That link between religion and government is the whole point of this week.

1 · What was it for?

This is the question that trips people up, because the "obvious" answers are all wrong:

Not this
  • Not to spread democracy (kings, not voters, made the rules).
  • Not to make peace with other cultures (it governed Babylon's own people).
  • Not mainly about life after death (that's more an Egyptian emphasis).
This

To create a stable society. A single, written, public set of rules so everyone knew the law, punishments were predictable, and order held together across a large kingdom.

In the prologue Hammurabi says the gods called him "to bring about the rule of righteousness in the land… so that the strong should not harm the weak." That's a mission statement for order and justice.

2 · Where did his power come from? (religion & government)

In the First Civilizations, religion and government were tied together. A ruler didn't just claim to be strong — he claimed the gods had chosen him. That claim is what made the law feel legitimate rather than just one man's opinion.

Hammurabi claimed his law code was inspired by the gods — above all Marduk, the chief god of Babylon, who (he said) handed him the kingship. The top of the stele even shows Hammurabi standing before a seated god (Shamash, god of the sun and justice) receiving the symbols of authority. Message: break these laws and you defy the gods, not just the king.

This is the pattern to carry into the Belief Systems conversation: across early civilizations, belief systems propped up political power — Sumerian city-gods and their temple-kings, Egyptian pharaohs as living gods, and here, a Babylonian king ruling as the gods' chosen agent. Religion answered "why should we obey?"

3 · How the Code actually worked

"An eye for an eye" — but not for everyone equally

Many laws follow lex talionis (retaliation in kind): the punishment mirrors the crime. But the punishment depended on your social class — the Code assumes a divided society, it doesn't treat everyone the same:

If a noble injured a…The penalty was…
fellow noble (equal rank)the same done to him — "knock out his tooth"
commoner (lower rank)a fine (e.g., pay silver), not equal retaliation
enslaved persona smaller payment to the owner (treated as property loss)

So the tooth-for-a-tooth law is really evidence of social class divisions, not of everyone being equal under the law.

Why writing it down mattered

Hammurabi didn't invent most of these rules — he recorded existing laws and made them public. Putting them in stone where people could see them meant the law was fixed and knowable, not whatever a local official decided that day. That public, written standard is a real building block of civilization.
