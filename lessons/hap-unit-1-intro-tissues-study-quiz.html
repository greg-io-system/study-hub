Study guide

Part 1 — Intro to Anatomy & Physiology

  • Anatomy = the study of the body's shape and structure. Physiology = the study of how the body works. The link: structure → function — a part's structure decides how it works. Her example: a bowl is deep so small or runny food stays in and spills less; a plate is flat so foods stay separate and you can cut on it.
  • Levels of organization (small → big), with her examples: chemical (atoms) → molecules (macromolecules) → organelles (e.g. mitochondria, nucleus, ribosome) → cells (each has a specialized job) → tissues (4 types) → organs (e.g. heart, stomach, brain) → organ systems (e.g. nervous, muscular, digestive) → organism (that's you).
    Memory trick: Crazy Monkeys Often Chase Tigers Over Open Oceans — Chemical, Molecules, Organelles, Cells, Tissues, Organs (Over), Organ systems (Open), Organism (Oceans).
  • Maintaining life — survival needs: nutrients (carbohydrates = fuel; proteins = build cell structures; fats = cushion the organs + reserve fuel), oxygen (about 20% of air), water (60–89% of body weight). The body is always working to keep an internal balance.
  • Necessary life functions (the "characteristics of life") — know all nine and what each means:
    • Maintaining boundaries — every part (cells, skin, organs) has a boundary that protects it from damage.
    • Movement — not just skeletal muscles moving you; blood, urine and food/waste move too.
    • Responsiveness — sensing a change and responding to it.
    • Digestion — breaking food into simple molecules so the cardiovascular system can carry the nutrients to the body's cells.
    • Respiration — taking in O2, getting rid of CO2.
    • Metabolism — breaking complex substances down into simple ones.
    • Excretion — removing waste from the body (urine and feces).
    • Growth — getting bigger and increasing in number.
    • Reproduction — making new cells and new organisms. Trick fact: this is the one you do NOT need to stay alive — it's needed for the species to survive.

Homeostasis — the big idea of the unit

  • Homeostasis = the body holding an internal value constant. If something changes (like temperature or pH), the body responds to correct it. If the body can't maintain homeostasis, you die — that's why it matters.
  • Factors the body keeps balanced (her list): nutrients, wastes, temperature, ions (electrolytes), water, pressure (air and blood), gases.
  • Loop components: Stimulus (a factor shifts) → Receptor, also called the affector (detects the shift) → Control center (the brain) → Effector (a muscle or a gland).
  • Two control speeds: nervous system = fast (nerve impulses), endocrine system = slow (hormones released). The control works through a feedback loop, positive or negative.
  • Negative feedback responds by reversing the direction of the change → keeps things constant → maintains homeostasis. Examples: blood glucose, pulse rate, blood pressure, body temperature. Sign pattern: + → − → +  OR  − → + → − (it keeps flipping back to hold the balance).
  • Positive feedback responds by amplifying the change → it is destabilizing, so it does not produce homeostasis and can be detrimental. Examples: childbirth, sneezing, blood clotting. Sign pattern: + → + → +  OR  − → − → −. For childbirth, the change keeps building until the baby is born.
  • Try it: ask "does the response oppose the change or add to it?" Oppose = negative. Add = positive.

Anatomical position, directions, planes, cavities

  • Anatomical position: standing up straight facing the observer, head level, eyes forward, feet flat and pointing forward, arms at the sides, palms turned forward. Every directional term assumes this pose — that's why the thumb is LATERAL to the fingers.
  • Directional pairs (learn as opposites, with the other names she gives):
    • superior (also cephalic / cranial) — toward the head; the upper part of a structure  /  inferior (also caudal) — away from the head; the lower part of a structure
    • anterior (ventral) — nearer the front  /  posterior (dorsal) — nearer the back
    • medial — nearer the midline (midsagittal plane)  /  lateral — farther from it
    • proximal — nearer to where a limb attaches to the trunk, OR nearer to the point of origin  /  distal — farther from the limb's attachment, OR farther from the point of origin
    • superficial (external) — toward the surface  /  deep (internal) — away from the body surface
  • Planes: sagittal = right/left (midsagittal = equal halves, parasagittal = unequal); frontal (coronal) = anterior/posterior (front/back); transverse = superior/inferior (top/bottom).
  • Anatomical regions are on her review list: study them from your own class notes, slides and labs.
  • Body cavities = spaces inside the body that contain, protect, separate and support the internal organs. Two main ones: dorsal and ventral.
    Dorsal (back)Cranial — made by the cranial bones; holds the brain and its coverings  ·  Vertebral/spinal — made by the vertebral column; holds the spinal cord and where the spinal nerves begin
    Ventral (front)Thoracic (upper) — lungs + heart  ·  Abdominopelvic (lower) — stomach, liver, intestines, pancreas, kidneys, reproductive organs and more. It splits into abdominal (upper: stomach, spleen, gallbladder, liver, small intestine, most of the large intestine) + pelvic (lower: urinary bladder, parts of the large intestine, internal reproductive organs). No wall separates abdominal from pelvic.

Part 2 — Cells

  • Cell theory: (1) all organisms are made of one or more cells, (2) the cell is the basic unit of organization (as an atom is to an element, the cell is to life), (3) all cells come from cells that already existed.
  • An animal cell has four main parts: nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, proteins.
  • Key structures + jobs:
    • Nucleus ("the manager") — the cell's control center; holds the DNA (chromosomes), the cell's blueprint, which decides how the cell works. Its nuclear envelope has large pores so materials move in and out easily. Inside is the nucleolus, which makes the ribosomes.
    • Ribosomes — where proteins are built (protein synthesis).
    • Cell membrane — selectively permeable: it lets the substances the cell needs in and out. For the membrane's structure (what it's built from, which the review sheet asks you to connect to permeability), use your own class notes.
    • Cytoplasm — gives the cell structure and supports the organelles.
    • Mitochondria — one of the organelles on her list. For what mitochondria do (it's on the review sheet), use your own class notes.
  • Genetic information: the DNA in the nucleus is the genetic code for all living things. Genes give an organism its characteristics. When a cell divides, the code is copied into the new cells. Protein synthesis = using the DNA code to build proteins; it happens at the ribosomes, from amino acids (small units that link into long chains called polypeptides). The proteins give each cell its characteristics (skin cell vs neuron vs muscle cell). Radiation and chemicals can affect DNA and its copying, with big effects; hormones change which proteins a cell makes.
  • Cellular respiration = turning what you eat into energy your cells can use. The body needs 3 essential macromolecules: proteins (build tissue, transport nutrients), carbohydrates (energy source), lipids (protection, energy storage).
  • ATP: aerobic respiration releases the energy in glucose and turns it into a more usable form — ATP. Equation: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → (enzymes) → 6CO2 + 6H2O + 38 ATP. ATP is the form of energy the cell uses.
  • Why cells reproduce: growth and development from a fertilized egg; replacing damaged or dead cells; passing on genetic material. Mitosis = body cells (growth, replacement, repair); meiosis = sex cells (sperm and egg), for reproducing offspring. The cycle differs between the two.
  • Mitosis stages: interphase → Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase → cytokinesis. Mrs. Kalec flags interphase and cytokinesis as "not really" mitosis stages — PMAT is the core. Memory trick: PMAT.
  • Interphase: the cell grows while the DNA gives instructions for making proteins and other important molecules (the DNA stores the information for the cell's function and survival).
  • Controlled growth: growth slows once the cell is too big for nutrients to move through it easily, so the cell divides into two smaller daughter cells. Cells divide until they touch others. This is essential for growth and tissue repair.
  • Cancer = some cells have lost control of their own growth rate — they don't stop dividing when they touch other cells, and keep going until nutrients run out.

Part 3 — Tissues

  • Histology = the study of tissues. A tissue = a group of similar cells with similar structures and jobs. The four main types: epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous.
  • Germ layers: about 18 days after fertilization, the first cells form a 3-layer disc; these layers later give rise to all the body's specialized cells ("germinate" = come into being and develop). Endoderm (inner) → lining of the digestive organs; mesoderm (middle) → muscle, bone, blood vessels; ectoderm (outer) → outermost layer of the skin + part of the nervous system.

  • Epithelial tissue: protects what's underneath, acts as a barrier, lets substances pass through, secretes and absorbs substances. 90% of all cancers start in epithelial tissue.
  • Epithelium is named shape × layers: shape — squamous (squished), cuboidal (cubes), columnar (tall columns); layers — simple (one layer) vs stratified (several layers). So "stratified squamous" = many layers of squished cells. Where each type is found: check your class slides (e.g. slide 73) and notes.
  • Connective tissue types: embryonic = stem cells; loose = padding for structures; fibrous = dense but elastic (tendons); blood = red and white cells + platelets (yes, blood IS connective tissue — a favorite trick question); adipose = fat; cartilage = dense padding between bones; bone = dense and spongy.
  • Muscle tissue: voluntary = conscious control; involuntary = unconscious. Skeletal = attached to bone (mostly voluntary); cardiac = heart (involuntary); smooth = inside hollow organs (mostly involuntary).
  • Nervous tissue: makes up the brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerves; talks to other tissues through action potentials. Two cell types: neurons (nerve cells — four main parts: dendrites, cell body, nucleus, axon) and glial cells (support, protect and nourish the neurons).

Common traps

  • Anatomy vs physiology: anatomy = structure, physiology = how it works. If the question says "function," it's physiology.
  • Positive feedback is not "good" — it's destabilizing and can be detrimental. Negative feedback is the one that maintains homeostasis.
  • Thumb is lateral, not medial — anatomical position has palms forward.
  • Proximal/distal have two meanings: nearer/farther from where a limb joins the trunk, AND nearer/farther from a point of origin.
  • Blood is a connective tissue; epithelial tissue has no blood vessels.
  • Muscle control: skeletal is mostly voluntary; cardiac is involuntary and ONLY the heart; smooth is mostly involuntary and sits within hollow organs.

Show answer

b — negative feedback: the response reverses the direction of the change, which keeps things constant.

c — childbirth: the change keeps building until the baby is born. (Also: sneezing, blood clotting.)

7

Part E — Short answer (explain it like you'd teach it)

39

Define homeostasis, explain why it matters, then walk through all four loop components (stimulus → receptor → control center → effector).

Show answer

Strong answer: Homeostasis is the body holding an internal value constant; if it can't, you die. A factor such as temperature shifts (stimulus) → a receptor (affector) detects the shift (receptor) → the brain (control center) → a muscle or gland (effector) responds to correct it. Because the response reverses the change, that's negative feedback.

40

Explain "structure determines function" using one example.

Show answer

Any example from class works: her bowl and plate (a bowl is deep, so it holds runny food; a plate is flat, so you can cut on it). Or: muscle tissue is made of long, contractile cells, so it can contract to cause movement. Or: the nuclear envelope has large pores, so materials move in and out easily. Name the structure, name the job, connect them with "so" or "because."

41

Positive and negative feedback: define both, give one example of each, and say which one maintains homeostasis.

Show answer

Negative reverses the direction of a change (blood glucose, pulse rate, blood pressure, body temperature) — this is the one that maintains homeostasis. Positive amplifies the change (childbirth, sneezing, blood clotting); it's destabilizing, so it doesn't maintain homeostasis and can be detrimental.

42

List the nine necessary life functions and define any three.

Show answer

Maintaining boundaries, movement, responsiveness, digestion, respiration, metabolism, excretion, growth, reproduction. Sample definitions: digestion = breaking food into simple molecules so the cardiovascular system can deliver nutrients to the cells; excretion = removing waste (urine and feces); growth = increasing in size and in number.
