1 · The prompt, plainly

Take a position on Marano's claim that, through over-parenting, the use of technology, and various "safety nets" from institutions, we are creating "a nation of wimps."

Read it twice. It is not "do you agree with Marano?" Mr. Evans calls plain agree/disagree an elementary view. Her claim is really three causes and one effect, and you can stand in a different place on each part:

Over-parenting
Technology
Institutional "safety nets"
→
"a nation of wimps"

That split is where a position lives. You might think one cause is real and another overblown. You might accept every cause and reject the effect: the thing is real, but "wimp" is the wrong word for it. Or you might think the effect is real and blame a cause Marano never names. Each of those is a position. "She's right" and "she's wrong" are not.

Two things Mr. Evans said about her early on (Sep 1): Marano sits "closer to the 100 than she is to the 50" on the Argument Continuum, but "she leaves room for a conversation IN HER LANGUAGE." And he warned against "easy solutions to complex questions." Your job is to find that room.

It's about a trend, not about you. Mr. Evans (9/29): it's easy to answer these pieces emotionally ("I am not a wimp!"). But the prompt asks about "a trend and a generation and a slow progression." Zoom out to Adam Smith's third-person view: look at the culture around you, and be open to the idea that you might be an outlier. Your own experience still counts as evidence. It just can't be the whole argument.

2 · Define your terms first

"Strong writing and thinking frequently comes down to defining your terms." (Mr. Evans, 9/8.) The whole argument turns on one word. On Aug 31 he asked the class: what if to be fragile, according to your generation, is to be ______? Answer it for yourself before you argue anything.

5 · The move, on a made-up twin

Here's the whole position move on an invented claim with the same shape as Marano's: three causes and one effect. Your real sources stay yours.

Made-up claim
Coach Dana Pryor, "The Bubble-Wrap League" (2019): overinvolved sideline parents, stats apps that post every game, and leagues that won't cut players are raising "a league of quitters."
Made-up sources
Dr. Lena Ortiz, "Let Them Lose": kids who lose regularly learn to cope, so protection robs them of that. (Pulls toward Pryor.)
Sam Whitfield, "The Scoreboard in Your Pocket": players aren't quitting because life is too soft; they quit because every game is now a public, permanent verdict. (Pushes against Pryor.)

Three thesis levels on that twin. Remember: on the AP essays a real thesis is the one guaranteed point, the difference between the 0 and the 1.

Summary — scores the 0
Pryor, Ortiz and Whitfield all discuss why kids quit youth sports.
Agree / disagree — a position, but elementary
Pryor is right that youth sports are raising a league of quitters.
A real position — defines, splits the claim, names a cause
While Pryor is right that kids are walking away from sports, "quitter" is the wrong word: the players Whitfield describes aren't too protected to lose, they're too watched to risk losing. That makes the problem pressure, not softness, and it means one of Pryor's three causes (the stats app) is working against the other two.

Look at what the third one does. It concedes the part of the claim it accepts ("While Pryor is right…"). It redefines the key word ("quitter"). It splits the causes instead of taking them as a bundle. And its last sentence is clearly the writer's own. You can see sections 1–3 of this page inside it.

6 · Fill Mr. Evans's blank

On Sep 25 Mr. Evans put one sentence to the class:

If Google is making us  ? , then AI is making us  ? .

That blank is a shortcut into this paper. Technology is one of Marano's three causes, and whatever word you put in the second gap is your answer on that strand. If your word means "wimps," you're siding with Marano. If it means something else (and most honest answers do), you're arguing she named the wrong problem. The word you choose is your position.

Below, the whole move is shown on a made-up twin, so the AI answer stays yours.

Made-up twin claim
Driving instructor Ray Colton, "Backseat Nation" (2021): parents who drive their kids everywhere, GPS that does the navigating, and cars that brake and steer for you are raising "a generation of passengers."
The twin blank
If GPS is making drivers ___, then self-driving cars will make them ___.

Same shape as yours: an older tool that helps (Google, GPS), then a newer one that does it for you (AI, self-driving). "Passengers" plays the part of Marano's "wimps."

Step 1 · Climb the ladder: vague word, weak paper

Most first answers are one vague word. A vague word gives you nothing to prove, so the paper ends up as an opinion. Watch the second gap of the twin get sharper:

Vague — feels true, proves nothing
…self-driving cars will make them lazy.
Lazy how? Every reader agrees or shrugs, and no source can back up "lazy."
Better — names what changes
…will make them out of practice.
Now there's a skill that fades. But out of practice at what, and does it matter?
Sharp — names what's lost and why it matters
…will make them unable to take over when the system quits.
This is a claim a source can support or attack. And it isn't the same as "passenger": these drivers might be willing, just not able.
Your own — the word nobody else in class picked
…will make them drivers who've never felt the road.
Specific, a little surprising, and the writer can spend a whole paragraph on what "feeling the road" means. That's your exigence showing.

Test for your own word: could a source on your shelf (section 4) prove it wrong? If nothing could, it's still too vague.

Step 2 · One blank, three different papers

Now put each answer next to Colton's label. Where it lands decides which paper you're writing:

Same as the claim

"…will make them passengers in their own lives."

Agrees with Colton. Fine, but now you need something he didn't say. Otherwise it's the agree-only thesis level from section 5.

Different from the claim

"…will make them unable to take over when the system quits."

Concedes something is lost, but says Colton's word is wrong: the problem isn't weakness, it's a missing skill. This is the "split the claim" paper.

Flips the claim

"…will make them better at the driving, worse at the finding."

Gains and losses together. It argues the technology changes which skills a generation has, not how tough it is.

None of the three is the right answer. Each is a different, defensible paper. What matters is that you know which one you're writing.

Step 3 · From the blank to a paragraph

Here the "different from the claim" answer becomes one body paragraph of the twin paper. The source is made up too.

Colton is right that today's new drivers lean on their cars more than any generation before them; however, calling them "passengers" misses what is actually being lost. GPS made drivers dependent on directions, and self-driving cars will make them unable to take over when the system quits. In "The Handoff Problem," engineer Mara Quinn describes test drivers who were alert and willing to grab the wheel yet took several seconds too long to react, because the car had done the steering for the previous hour. Quinn's drivers are not timid or soft, which is what Colton's word suggests. They are rusty at the one moment that counts. That distinction matters for the fix: if the problem were toughness, the answer would be tougher parenting, but if it is a fading skill, the answer is practice the technology no longer forces us to get.
concedes first (Rogerian)the filled-in blank, as a claima source in conversationwhat the writer makes of it

Notice the source doesn't make the argument. It supplies one piece of evidence, and the writer says what it proves. The last sentences also tie the word back to the claim's label ("toughness" vs. "skill"). That link back to Marano's "wimps" is what keeps an AI paragraph inside this paper instead of drifting into a general essay about AI.

9 · From framework to draft (for Oct 7)

Mr. Evans hasn't given a set format for this paper, so treat this as one common shape, not a rule. What he has said is the test: a source-based position that you place "in conversation with other voices along the Argument Continuum," with his framework's first level at the center: your exigence, your audience, your purpose.

  • Opening
    Why this question matters now: your exigence. Marano's claim, fairly stated. Your working thesis (section 7), which concedes before it argues.
  • Body sections
    One section per strand or idea you're actually arguing, not one per source. In each: your point, then a source in conversation with it ("Twenge argues ___; however, ___"), then what you make of it. The source supports your claim. It isn't the claim.
  • The Rogerian move
    Somewhere the strongest opposing voice gets its fair say (your "complexity" from section 8), and you show why your position still stands, or how it bends.
  • Closing
    Not a summary. What changes if you're right? Where does this leave your generation, or the adults deciding things for it?

Paragraph test, borrowed from the AP readers Mr. Evans quoted on 9/29: they want "a mind at work." If a paragraph would read the same no matter what source you dropped into it, it's a script, and it needs rewriting.
