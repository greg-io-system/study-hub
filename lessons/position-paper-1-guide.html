1 · The prompt, plainly

Take a position on Marano's claim that, through over parenting, use of technology, and various "safety nets" from institutions, we are creating "a nation of wimps."

Read it twice. It is not "do you agree with Marano?" Mr. Evans (9/17): "an elementary view ... would only subscribe to simple agreement or disagreement." Look at how the prompt is built: three things it names, and one result. On 10/6 he asked the same kind of question: "So, what are the causes and what are the a/effects?"

Over parenting
Use of technology
Institutional "safety nets"
→
"a nation of wimps"

That split gives you room to think. You don't have to stand in the same place on every part. "She's right" and "she's wrong" are not positions on their own.

Mr. Evans on Marano (9/1): she "is certainly closer to the 100 than she is to the 50, but she leaves room for a conversation IN HER LANGUAGE." On 8/31 he warned: "You should be on guard against these easy solutions to complex questions." And on 10/8: "Do not lose credibility by misinterpreting or misrepresenting Marano's claim. It may be time to circle back for a re-read."

It's about a trend, not about you. Mr. Evans (9/29): it's easy to respond emotionally to these pieces ("I am not a wimp!"). "The prompting for this paper asks you about a trend and a generation and a slow progression. You need to be open to the idea that you may be an outlier." Zoom out to Adam Smith's third person perspective. Also 9/29: "Your experience is relevant and usable; however, your aim is to build your position by placing it (your position) in conversation with other voices along the Argument Continuum."

5 · The move, on a made-up twin

Here's the position move on an invented claim with the same shape as Marano's: three causes and one effect. Your real sources stay yours.

Made-up example · claim
Coach Dana Pryor, "The Bubble-Wrap League": overinvolved sideline parents, stats apps that post every game, and leagues that won't cut players are raising "a league of quitters."
Made-up sources
Dr. Lena Ortiz, "Let Them Lose": kids who lose regularly learn to cope, so protection robs them of that. (Pulls toward Pryor.)
Sam Whitfield, "The Scoreboard in Your Pocket": players aren't quitting because life is too soft; they quit because every game is now a public, permanent verdict. (Pushes against Pryor.)

Three thesis levels on that twin. Mr. Evans (9/17): learn "the difference between the 0 and the 1." Each AP essay (there are 3) has a 6 point scoring scale, and the thesis is one of those points. His warning (9/15): don't make "the mistake of failing to take a position."

Summary — no position
Pryor, Ortiz and Whitfield all discuss why kids quit youth sports.
Agree / disagree — a position, but elementary
Pryor is right that youth sports are raising a league of quitters.
A real position — concedes, redefines, splits the claim
While Pryor is right that kids are walking away from sports, "quitter" is the wrong word: the players Whitfield describes aren't too protected to lose, they're too watched to risk losing. That makes the problem pressure, not softness, and it means one of Pryor's three causes (the stats app) is working against the other two.

Look at what the third one does. It concedes the part of the claim it accepts ("While Pryor is right…"), the Rogerian move. It redefines the key word ("quitter"). It splits the causes instead of taking them as a bundle. And its last sentence is clearly the writer's own.

6 · Fill Mr. Evans's blank

In his Sep 25 email Mr. Evans wrote: "What are the implications of AI on YOUR GENERATION? In other words, If Google is making us _________, then AI is making us _____(?)_____."

If Google is making us  ? , then AI is making us  ? .

Technology is one of the three things in the prompt, so whatever word you put in the second gap is your answer on that part. Then ask: does your word mean "wimps," or something else?

Below, the move is shown on a made-up twin, so the AI answer stays yours.

Made-up example · twin claim
Driving instructor Ray Colton, "Backseat Nation": parents who drive their kids everywhere, GPS that does the navigating, and cars that brake and steer for you are raising "a generation of passengers."
The twin blank
If GPS is making drivers ___, then self-driving cars will make them ___.

Same shape as yours: an older tool that helps, then a newer one that does it for you. "Passengers" plays the part of Marano's "wimps."

Step 1 · Climb the ladder: vague word, weak paper

Most first answers are one vague word. A vague word gives you nothing to prove. Watch the second gap of the twin get sharper:

Vague — feels true, proves nothing
…self-driving cars will make them lazy.
Lazy how? No source can back up "lazy."
Better — names what changes
…will make them out of practice.
Now there's a skill that fades. But out of practice at what, and does it matter?
Sharp — names what's lost and why it matters
…will make them unable to take over when the system quits.
This is a claim a source can support or attack. And it isn't the same as "passenger": these drivers might be willing, just not able.
Your own — the word nobody else in class picked
…will make them drivers who've never felt the road.
Specific, a little surprising, and the writer can spend a whole paragraph on what "feeling the road" means.

Test for your own word: could a source on your shelf (section 4) prove it wrong? If nothing could, it's still too vague.

Step 2 · One blank, three different papers

Now put each twin answer next to Colton's label. Where it lands decides which paper you're writing:

Same as the claim

"…will make them passengers in their own lives."

Agrees with Colton. Now you need something he didn't say, or it's the agree-only level from section 5.

Different from the claim

"…will make them unable to take over when the system quits."

Concedes something is lost, but says Colton's word is wrong. This is the "split the claim" paper.

Flips the claim

"…will make them better at the driving, worse at the finding."

Gains and losses together: the technology changes which skills a generation has.

None of the three is the right answer. Each is a different paper. What matters is that you know which one you're writing.

Step 3 · From the blank to a paragraph

Here the "different from the claim" twin answer becomes one paragraph of the twin paper. The source is made up too.

Colton is right that today's new drivers lean on their cars more than any generation before them; however, calling them "passengers" misses what is actually being lost. GPS made drivers dependent on directions, and self-driving cars will make them unable to take over when the system quits. In "The Handoff Problem," engineer Mara Quinn describes test drivers who were alert and willing to grab the wheel yet took several seconds too long to react, because the car had done the steering for the previous hour. Quinn's drivers are not timid or soft, which is what Colton's word suggests. They are rusty at the one moment that counts. That distinction matters for the fix: if the problem were toughness, the answer would be tougher parenting, but if it is a fading skill, the answer is practice the technology no longer forces us to get.
concedes first (Rogerian)the filled-in blank, as a claima source in conversationwhat the writer makes of it

The source doesn't make the argument. It supplies one piece of evidence, and the writer says what it proves. The last sentences tie the word back to the claim's label. Mr. Evans (9/25): "My aim for the coming days/weeks is for everything to be targeted at your position on Marano's claim."

Step 4 · Your blank

9 · From framework to draft: what Mr. Evans said

He gave no fill-in-the-blank format. On 10/6: "You still need a structure. Your structure must be one that follows the structure of YOUR ACTIVE MIND rather than a fill-in-the-blank structure that is easy to grade." He called that structure a "LINE OF REASONING." Here is what his emails from 10/2 to 10/9 said, point by point:

  • Not the 5-paragraph essay (10/6, 10/8)
    "how are you going to create a structured argument that builds a line of reasoning rather than states your thesis over and over?" And: "It is not about the number of paragraphs, it is about the repetition and stasis of an argument."
  • Claims that connect (10/6)
    "Do they flow from one claim to the other? Do they have enough evidence? Did you leave anything unstated that an active reader may note and critique?" For logos, think syllogism and enthymeme: "What are your premises? What are your unstated premises?"
  • Your introduction (10/5)
    "Your introduction is a choice. What a/effect are you looking for the reader to feel?" "YOUR voice first. This is your first impression. Get to Marano later."
  • Paragraphs (10/8)
    "A paragraph is a PURPOSEFUL choice." Avoid "the dreaded MEGAGRAPH."
  • Personal experience (10/5, 10/6)
    Anecdotal evidence is "a story in order to________." Warning: "If you have too much personal experience, you may not be developing and supporting claims as much as writing a memoir."
  • The Rogerian (10/9)
    "Not only knowing but addressing the 'other side' will enforce your side." And: "make quite certain you are creating a position and not simply speaking about positions authors have put forward."
  • Ethos in blue (10/5, 10/8)
    "As you intentionally appeal to ethos, those points in your paper will be changed to the color blue by you, the author." "Your BLUE must move well beyond who you are ('I am a member of Gen-Z') and toward how you develop credibility as an academic."
  • Play (10/8)
    "If you are looking for permission to play, stop asking and start playing."
  • His "rubric" (10/5)
    "Your exigence must be clear, your audience (primary and secondary) should be understandable, and your purpose should come to the surface from your position in your position paper."
  • The reader test (10/6)
    He turned the reading questions on you: your exigence, your audiences, what you are "hoping to achieve," and "Is the presented argument convincing?" "A reader should be able to answer these questions while analyzing your position."
  • Sources need room (10/8)
    "Move beyond dropping a quote. Put in some work around the idea to give context and then lead your audience to an understanding of the idea itself."
  • Your choices (10/2)
    "'Should I include a _______' is a question of writer's choice." And: "Your purpose and audience does not include Mr. Evans or a rubric."
  • To what extent (10/8)
    "To what extent do you agree with the claim--and--to what extent do you disagree." But "riding the fence can be even more frustrating for your readers."
  • Language tools (10/8)
    He linked the AP They Say / I Say Sentence Templates + Transitions again, and asked you to "consider finding the different ways to use 'however' in your writing."
  • Your own way in (10/9)
    His Simone Biles line of reasoning, then: "are you giving yourself permission to use SPORTS as a way to enter the conversation about your generation? (or any other field in which you have interest and credibility?)"

Mr. Evans (9/29), on what AP Readers want: "What every Reader really wants is what is known as a mind at work." Not "some auto-pilot script."
