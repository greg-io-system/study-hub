How Mr. Evans's assignments work

His homework emails all follow the same shape. Learn the shape once and every assignment gets easier — and you stop losing points for the thing he flags most: summary instead of a view.

The big idea: the actual "assignment" (usually section C) is the smallest part. Most of the email is Mr. Evans handing you tools and building a habit before the task — then asking you to notice whether you used them. Skipping the setup is exactly what produces the "summary, not a view" work he keeps flagging.

The five moves, every time

1
The conversation continues. He opens by picking up an idea from class — because for him the course is one long conversation, not separate assignments.

"Much learning is available between classes through email, please read carefully and fully as there is significant context, feedback, and opportunity for curiosity." (For Monday, 9/14)

Your move: read the whole email before doing anything. The context is part of the assignment.
2
The tool check. Before the task, he reminds you of the tools you've been given (the They Say / I Say templates, the "appeals to" vocabulary) and asks — pointedly — whether you're using them.

"How are your verbs? How are your transitions? Are you moving writing tools and lessons into your writing?" (9/14)  |  "are you… using these templates and transitions?… Why are you or why aren't you?" (9/15)

Your move: actually open the documents and use them. That's not optional flavor — it's half of what he's grading.
3
Developed, not summary. He states, over and over, that notes and paragraphs must go beyond summary into analysis, interpretation, and synthesis — and carry your position.

"notes of summary will result in a conversation of summary. Your Constitution should demand more." (For Friday, 9/11)

Your move: in every paragraph, point to a sentence that is clearly yours, not a retelling anyone could write.
4
The task (A → B → C). Only now, lettered and built up in order, comes the actual work — and the early letters are usually the foundation ("learn the basics so I can teach the advanced") that make the last letter possible.

"Learn this as the base so that I can build upon what you are learning here… Learn the basics so that I can teach the advanced." (9/15, section A)

Your move: do A and B for real — they're the tools C needs. If you're stuck on C, you probably skipped one.
5
Reflect on the process. He closes with a reflective (rhetorical) question — often "no writing needed" — asking whether you actually used the tools. This is his "think about your thinking" move.

"How did you use your documents and lists…? (No writing is needed here… Now complete the reflecting.)" (9/15)

Your move: answer it honestly to yourself. Did the tools change how you wrote? If not, that's the fix for next time.

The signals to watch for

When you see any of these, he's doing one of the five moves above — not making small talk:

  • "read carefully and fully… context, feedback, curiosity" → move 1 (read it all).
  • "Are you using the templates / your verbs / your transitions?" → move 2 (use the tools).
  • "developed, not summary" / "analysis, interpretation, synthesis" / "a voice, not an opinion" → move 3 (take a view).
  • "Learn the basics so I can teach the advanced" / lettered A, B, C → move 4 (don't skip the foundation).
  • "Why are you or why aren't you?" / "now complete the reflecting" → move 5 (reflect honestly).
  • "Let us meet here again — Socrates." Every email. It's the whole philosophy in one line: he asks questions so you arrive at the thinking — he isn't going to hand you the answer.

The playbook (works for any of his assignments)

  1. Read the entire email first — the intro and the reflection, not just the lettered task.
  2. Do the foundation letters (A, B) for real; open the documents he names and use them.
  3. Write each paragraph as a move, not a report — connect the sources and add your own line.
  4. Use the tools: They Say / I Say templates, "appeals to" (ethos/pathos/logos), the Glossary of Terms.
  5. End on your view; then answer his reflective question honestly before you hit reply.
