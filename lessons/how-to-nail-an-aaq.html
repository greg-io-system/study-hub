How to Nail an AAQ

The Article Analysis Question is the same six moves every time — only the study changes. Learn the moves once and every AAQ this year gets easier.

The one rule that wins (or loses) every part: apply, don't just define. A definition alone earns zero — you must tie the concept to a specific detail of the study in front of you. The formula for every part: name the concept → connect it to THIS study → one clear sentence.

What an AAQ is

  • You get one source — a described study — and answer in parts A–F.
  • Write in complete sentences and use psychology terms (that's part of the score).
  • Every part is graded only if you're clearly talking about this study, not psychology in general.
  • You're not penalized for extra info — unless it contradicts a right answer you already gave. So don't hedge two opposite ways.
The example study (made up — so your real AAQ stays yours)
Researchers randomly assigned 60 tenth-graders at one school to study a 20-word vocabulary list either with instrumental music playing or in silence, then counted how many words each student recalled. The music group recalled a mean of 14 words; the silence group recalled a mean of 11.

Every worked answer below uses this made-up study. On your real AAQ, run the same moves on your study.

AResearch method

Accurately identify the method used (experiment, correlational study, case study, naturalistic observation, survey…).

How to nail it
Look for the giveaway: random assignment + a manipulated variable = experiment. Just measuring how two things relate = correlational. Watching without interfering = naturalistic observation.
The trap
Naming the wrong method, or being vague ("a study"). Say the method by name.
Worked
"This was an experiment, because the researchers randomly assigned students to conditions and manipulated whether music played while they studied."

BOperational definition

State how a specific variable was defined or measured in this study.

How to nail it
An operational definition is the exact, measurable version of a variable. Find the number/procedure they used to pin it down.
The trap
Defining the abstract idea ("memory is the ability to recall information"). That's a textbook definition, not theirs. State how this study measured it.
Worked
"Recall was operationally defined as the number of the 20 vocabulary words a student correctly remembered."

CInterpret a result

Describe what a specific statistic (a mean, a correlation, a difference) actually indicates.

How to nail it
Don't just repeat the number — say what it means as a comparison or trend.
The trap
"The mean was 14." That restates data. What does 14 vs. 11 tell us?
Worked
"The music group's mean of 14 versus the silence group's 11 indicates that, on average, students who studied with music recalled about three more words than those who studied in silence."

DEthical guideline

Identify at least one ethics guideline the researchers applied.

How to nail it
Pick one and tie it to the study: informed consent, debriefing, confidentiality, protection from harm, no coercion, right to withdraw.
The trap
Naming a guideline in the air with no link to this study.
Worked
"The researchers applied informed consent by getting the tenth-graders' (and their parents') permission before they took part in the study."

EGeneralizability

Explain how far the findings can (or can't) be generalized — using specific evidence from the sample.

How to nail it
Point at who was studied and how they were chosen. A narrow or non-random sample limits how far it extends.
The trap
Saying "it can/can't generalize" with no evidence. Name the sample detail that decides it.
Worked
"The findings may not generalize widely, because the sample was only 60 tenth-graders at a single school — not representative of other ages, schools, or cultures — though they might apply to similar students."

FSupport or refute a claim

Explain how at least one finding supports or refutes a given idea (apply the data to an argument).

How to nail it
State support or refute, then use the finding as the reason. Data first, conclusion tied to it.
The trap
Giving an opinion with no data. The finding has to carry the claim.
Worked
"This finding supports the idea that background music can aid studying, because the music group recalled more words (14) than the silence group (11)."

Before you turn it in — the check

  • Did I answer all six parts (A–F), each in a complete sentence?
  • In every part, did I apply the concept to THIS study — not just define it?
  • Part C: did I say what the statistic means, not just repeat it?
  • Part E: did I name a specific sample detail as my evidence?
  • Part F: is my claim carried by a finding, not an opinion?
  • Did I use real psychology terms, and avoid contradicting myself?
