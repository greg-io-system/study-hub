The big idea for both sections

People adapted to their environment, and good ideas spread through trade and migration. In the Americas, cultures fit jungle, mountains, desert and river valleys, and borrowed from each other (ball games, gods, calendars). In Africa, farming and iron tools let Bantu-speaking farmers spread across half a continent, carrying their languages and skills with them.

8.2 · Mesoamerica (southern Mexico and Central America)

  • Olmec (about 1350–400 BCE): Mesoamerica's first complex civilization, on the Gulf coast. Main sites: San Lorenzo, then La Venta.
  • Known for the colossal stone heads: 17 found, some 11 feet tall and up to 50 tons. They were probably portraits of rulers, made to show power.
  • Religion: many gods, plus mixed beings like the were-jaguar and a feathered serpent. Rulers may have acted as shamans.
  • A ritual ball game played with rubber balls.
  • "Mother culture": through trade, Olmec ideas about religion, art and government spread to later cultures (ball games, gods, calendars). The book adds that how far this influence went is still debated.
  • Zapotec (from about 500 BCE): capital Monte Albán, built on a flattened mountaintop, likely for defense. Warfare was common.
  • Teotihuacán: a huge city northeast of modern Mexico City, about 100,000 people by 300 CE. Pyramids of the Sun and Moon sit along the Avenue of the Dead. Its power came from trade (especially obsidian), plus military force and diplomacy. It influenced the Zapotec and the Maya.
  • Maya:
  • About 40 independent city-states (Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque…), each ruled by "divine lords." Never one empire.
  • Adapted to poor tropical soil with slash-and-burn farming. Cities were built by lakes for water.
  • Two calendars working together (260-day + 365-day) made a 52-year cycle. The "Maya predicted the end of the world in 2012" idea is a myth.
  • A writing system scholars can read, which is why we know so much of their history.
  • Collapse in the 800s CE: likely several causes together. One is population growth leading to deforestation, soil erosion and bad harvests.

8.2 · South America (the Andes)

  • Early sites like Norte Chico go back before 3000 BCE.
  • Chavín (about 900–200 BCE): one culture spread across the whole Andes. That opened the Early Horizon period. The religious center Chavín de Huántar had a tunnel-maze temple and the fanged god-statue El Lanzón. Llamas carried trade goods.
  • After Chavín faded (about 200 BCE), regional civilizations grew:
  • Moche (north coast): irrigation farmers and highly militaristic. Their art shows warriors and violence. They had divine kings over nobles, bureaucrats and workers.
  • Nazca (dry south coast): small villages, and the Nazca Lines, giant animal and geometric pictures scraped into the desert floor.
  • Tiwanaku (high mountains by Lake Titicaca): about 40,000 people, potato fields watered by canals, and stone "trophy heads" in the walls.

8.2 · North America

  • Adena (Ohio River valley, from about 1000 BCE): burial mounds that grew as more people were buried in them.
  • Hopewell (from about 200 BCE): bigger mound complexes for ceremonies. The Newark site lined up with the moon.
  • People lived in small family groups, hunting and gathering plus growing plants like sunflowers and gourds.
  • Mostly equal, with no powerful rulers.
  • Huge trade network: copper from Lake Superior, shells from the Gulf, obsidian from the Rockies.
  • Southwest: maize arrived from Mexico but stayed a small part of hunter-gatherer life for centuries. Beans (about 500 BCE) helped, since maize and beans together are better nutrition.

9.2 · Farming starts in Africa (three places)

  1. Nile valley (Egypt): after 7000 BCE. Wheat, barley, sheep, goats and cattle came in from southwest Asia.
  2. Eastern Sahara (Sudan): started independently. The Nilo-Saharans grew sorghum and pearl millet and herded cattle. A long wet phase made the Sahara green then.
  3. West Africa (bend of the Niger River): also independent. The Niger-Congo peoples grew yams, oil palm, and their own kind of rice.

9.2 · Iron

  • Bronze plows (in Egypt a little before 3000 BCE) already boosted farming.
  • Iron is harder to make than bronze. It took over across the Near East after about 1000 BCE.
  • The key change in thinking: historians used to assume iron spread south from Egypt. Most now think sub-Saharan Africa developed ironworking on its own. The earliest evidence is about 1000 BCE in Central Africa (modern Chad, Central African Republic, South Sudan), a few centuries before Egypt.
  • The Nok culture (modern Nigeria) used iron by 500 BCE: first jewelry, then tools and weapons.
  • Migrating Bantu spread iron across central and southern Africa.

9.2 · The Bantu migrations

  • "Bantu" is a language family (400+ languages). The word means "people." It's not one nation.
  • Homeland: the area between Nigeria and Cameroon.
  • When and how: starting 3000–2000 BCE, in small groups over thousands of years. Older theories pictured conquering armies; now we know they mostly mixed and intermarried with hunter-gatherers they met.
  • Routes: an eastern stream to the Great Lakes (about 1500 BCE), then south, and a slower western stream down the coast and up the Congo rivers. They met in the Zambezi valley. By 500 CE they had settled the whole rainforest and much of southern Africa.
  • Learning from others: the Twa (rainforest people) taught them to adapt. They got cattle-herding from the Khoisan. That's why some southern Bantu languages have "click" sounds.
  • But also displacement: the San and Khoekhoe were pushed out of many areas. The dry western Kalahari stayed Khoisan-speaking.
  • Why it matters: the Bantu spread farming, herding, iron and language across Africa. That laid the base for later states like Great Zimbabwe.

Easy mix-ups

  • Olmec vs. Maya: Olmec came first (the "mother culture"). The Maya borrowed from them and built ~40 city-states later.
  • Chavín vs. Moche/Nazca/Tiwanaku: Chavín came first and spread everywhere. The other three grew after it faded.
  • Adena vs. Hopewell: both built mounds. Adena came earlier with smaller burial mounds. Hopewell built bigger ceremonial complexes and traded widely.
  • Nilo-Saharan vs. Niger-Congo: sorghum and millet in the eastern Sahara vs. yams in West Africa. The Bantu languages belong to the Niger-Congo family.

Part C — Short answer (write 2–4 sentences, then compare)

13

What evidence supports the conclusion that Teotihuacán wielded great influence in the larger Mesoamerican world during its height?

Show answer

Part C (model answers)

Teotihuacán's reach (8.2): it was huge (about 100,000 people by 300 CE, one of the world's largest cities) and controlled trade in obsidian, salt, cotton, cacao and shells. Its murals show rulers wearing quetzal feathers from as far as Guatemala. Monte Albán's buildings after 300 CE show Teotihuacán influence, and its sculptures show Teotihuacano diplomats meeting the Zapotec elite. At Maya sites there's evidence it intervened in Maya affairs, possibly even a coup at Tikal in 378. (Any two pieces of evidence = full credit.)

14

How did Maya civilization adapt to the environmental conditions of its surroundings?

Show answer

Maya adaptation (8.2): rainfall was often too low and the tropical soil poor. So they built their cities beside large shallow lakes for drinking and irrigation water, and used slash-and-burn agriculture to grow maize, squash, beans and cacao. (Also fair: clearing jungle to build; the later deforestation and erosion show the limits of that adaptation.)

15

How did the Neolithic Revolution affect African societies?

Show answer

Neolithic Revolution in Africa (9.2): starting around 7000 BCE, domestication let some groups settle permanently and support much larger populations. It spread up the Nile and across North Africa, and developed independently south of the Sahara. By about 1000 BCE, large populations across Africa lived by farming, which over time led to large, sophisticated civilizations.

16

Why did earlier scholars believe that ironworking technologies were disseminated to sub-Saharan Africa, and what evidence leads most scholars today to accept that these societies independently developed ironworking?

Show answer

Iron (9.2): earlier scholars assumed iron reached sub-Saharan Africa from Egypt (Egypt had iron objects early, e.g. Tutankhamun's dagger, and learned ironworking from the Near East). But the earliest sub-Saharan evidence (about 1000 BCE, Central Africa) is older than sophisticated ironworking in Egypt (7th century BCE), by a few centuries. So it most likely developed there independently, perhaps as a by-product of firing ceramics.

17

In what ways did the Bantu migrations affect African civilization?

Show answer

Bantu impact (9.2): they spread farming, herding and iron across central and southern Africa, and 400+ related languages. They mixed and intermarried with local peoples (learning from the Twa; cattle-herding and "click" sounds from the Khoisan) but also displaced groups like the San and Khoekhoe. In the long run they created a shared cultural foundation that later supported large states like Great Zimbabwe.

Part D — Think about it (from the section's own feature boxes)

18

What does the cyclical nature of the Maya calendar system suggest about their rituals and cosmology?

Show answer

Part D

Model answer (8.2): time ran in repeating cycles (52-year "bundles," Long Count "Great Cycles"), not toward an ending. That suggests rituals were scheduled to recur at sacred moments tied to the gods' actions and the heavens, and that the Maya saw the universe as renewing itself. The end of a cycle was a reason for big ceremonies, not the end of the world (which is why the 2012 "apocalypse" is a modern myth).
