Systems of Two Equations

Two equations, one question: which point makes both of them true at the same time? On a graph, that's just where the two lines cross. Once you can picture it, the algebra makes sense.

The one idea

A solution has to work in both

A system is two (or more) equations that have to be true together:

y = 2x − 1
y = −x + 5

Each equation on its own has infinitely many solutions: every point on its line. The system's solution is the point that's on both lines, which is where they cross.

Test it: try the point (2, 3). In the first equation: 2(2) − 1 = 3 ✓. In the second: −2 + 5 = 3 ✓. It works in both, so (2, 3) is the solution. Now try (0, −1): it works in the first (it's the y-intercept) but in the second −0 + 5 = 5 ≠ −1 ✗. On one line but not the other, so it's not a solution.

You always write the answer as an ordered pair (x, y). That ordered pair is the crossing point.

Only three things can happen

One, none, or infinitely many

Two straight lines can only do three things, so a system of two linear equations has only three possible answers:

The lines…PictureHow many solutionsHow to spot it from the equations
cross onceExactly one, the crossing pointDifferent slopes
are parallelNo solution: they never meetSame slope, different y-intercepts
are the same lineInfinitely many: every point on the lineSame slope and same y-intercept (one equation is just the other in disguise)
When you're solving with algebra, the variables can all cancel out. If what's left is something false like 0 = 7, the lines are parallel: no solution. If it's something always true like 0 = 0, it's the same line: infinitely many. Don't panic when the x's disappear. That disappearance is the answer.
Method 1

Solving by graphing

A twin problem, not one from your worksheet, so you can copy the method rather than the answer.

y = x + 1
y = −2x + 7

  1. Get both into y = mx + b. These already are. (If one looks like 2x + y = 7, solve it for y first.)
  2. Graph line 1: start at the y-intercept (0, 1), then use the slope 1 = "up 1, right 1" to get more points.
  3. Graph line 2: start at (0, 7), slope −2 = "down 2, right 1."
  4. Read the crossing: they meet at (2, 3).
  5. Check it in both: 2 + 1 = 3 ✓ and −2(2) + 7 = 3 ✓. Done: (2, 3).

Two curveballs your worksheet throws

1. An equation in standard form, like 2x + y = 5. You can't graph it from slope and intercept until you solve it for y:

2x + y = 5  →  subtract 2x from both sides  →  y = −2x + 5

If there's a number in front of y, divide everything by it at the end: 3x + 2y = 8 → 2y = −3x + 8 → y = −1.5x + 4.

2. An equation with only one letter, like x = 1 or y = −2.

  • x = 1 is a vertical line: every point on it has x equal to 1. Draw it straight up and down through 1 on the x-axis.
  • y = −2 is a horizontal line through −2 on the y-axis.

Twin: 2x + y = 5 and x = 1. The first becomes y = −2x + 5; the second is the vertical line through x = 1. They cross where x = 1, so y = −2(1) + 5 = 3, which gives (1, 3).

The catch with graphing: it's only as accurate as your drawing. If the lines cross at something like (1.4, 2.6) you can't read that off graph paper. That's when you switch to an algebra method, which gives you the exact answer.
Method 2

Substitution: swap one in for the other

If one equation tells you what y equals (or what x equals), you can replace that letter in the other equation. Now there's only one letter left, and you can solve it.

The recipe: (1) get one letter alone in one equation → (2) substitute that expression into the other equation → (3) solve for the one letter left → (4) plug that number back in to get the other letter → (5) check in both.

Twin A: one is already "y = …"

y = 3x − 5
y = x + 1

  1. Both say what y is, so they must equal each other: 3x − 5 = x + 1.
  2. Subtract x from both sides: 2x − 5 = 1. Add 5: 2x = 6. So x = 3.
  3. Plug back in (either equation): y = 3 + 1 = 4.
  4. Check the other one: 3(3) − 5 = 4 ✓. Answer: (3, 4).

Twin B: one equation is just "y = a number"

y = 4
2x + 3y = 6

  1. Put 4 in for y in the second: 2x + 3(4) = 6 → 2x + 12 = 6.
  2. 2x = −6, so x = −3. Answer: (−3, 4).

Use parentheses when you substitute: 3(4), not 34. They matter even more when you're putting a whole expression in.

Twin C: nothing is alone yet

3x + 2y = 4
x − y = 3

  1. Pick the easiest letter to get alone. Look for one with no number in front (really a 1 or −1). In x − y = 3, x is easy: add y to both sides → x = y + 3.
  2. Substitute into the other equation, in parentheses: 3(y + 3) + 2y = 4.
  3. Distribute: 3y + 9 + 2y = 4 → 5y + 9 = 4 → 5y = −5 → y = −1.
  4. Back into x = y + 3: x = −1 + 3 = 2.
  5. Check in the first: 3(2) + 2(−1) = 6 − 2 = 4 ✓. Answer: (2, −1).
Most common slip: substituting back into the same equation you just rearranged. That just gives you 3 = 3 and tells you nothing. Always substitute into the other one.
Method 3

Elimination: add the equations to make a letter vanish

Line the two equations up and add them together, left side plus left side, right side plus right side. If one letter has opposite numbers in front (like +2x and −2x), it cancels to zero and disappears.

Why is adding allowed? Each equation says two things are equal. Adding equal things to equal things keeps them equal: it's the same as doing the same thing to both sides.

Twin A: already opposite

2x + 3y = 12
−2x + y = −4

  1. 2x and −2x are opposites. Add straight down: 0x + 4y = 8.
  2. y = 2. Plug into the first: 2x + 6 = 12 → 2x = 6 → x = 3.
  3. Check in the second: −2(3) + 2 = −4 ✓. Answer: (3, 2).

Twin B: multiply ONE equation first

3x − 4y = 10
6x + 5y = 7

  1. Nothing cancels yet. But 3x and 6x are close: multiply the whole first equation by −2 and it becomes −6x + 8y = −20.
  2. Add to the second: (−6x + 6x) + (8y + 5y) = −20 + 7 → 13y = −13 → y = −1.
  3. Into the original first: 3x + 4 = 10 → 3x = 6 → x = 2.
  4. Check in the second: 12 − 5 = 7 ✓. Answer: (2, −1).
Multiply EVERY term, including the number on the right side. Forgetting the right side is the number-one elimination mistake.

Twin C: multiply BOTH equations

2x + 3y = 5
5x − 2y = −16

  1. No easy match. Look at the y's: +3y and −2y already have opposite signs. Make both into 6: multiply the first by 2 and the second by 3.
  2. 4x + 6y = 10 and 15x − 6y = −48. Add: 19x = −38 → x = −2.
  3. Into the first: 2(−2) + 3y = 5 → 3y = 9 → y = 3. Answer: (−2, 3).

Twin D: the equations are written out of order

−6 + 3x = 2y
10 = x + 2y

  1. Tidy up first: get every equation into Ax + By = C (letters on the left, x first, plain number on the right).
    First: subtract 2y and add 6 to both sides → 3x − 2y = 6.
    Second: just flip the sides → x + 2y = 10.
  2. Now −2y and +2y cancel. Add: 4x = 16 → x = 4.
  3. 4 + 2y = 10 → y = 3. Check in the original first: −6 + 12 = 6 = 2(3) ✓. Answer: (4, 3).

Twin E: fractions

½x + ⅓y = 4
x − y = 3

  1. Clear the fractions first. Multiply every term by a number both denominators go into (here 6): 3x + 2y = 24. No more fractions.
  2. Now solve as usual. Multiply the second by 2 → 2x − 2y = 6, then add: 5x = 30 → x = 6.
  3. 6 − y = 3 → y = 3. Check: ½(6) + ⅓(3) = 3 + 1 = 4 ✓. Answer: (6, 3).
Word problems

Turning a story into a system

The math is the same as above. The new skill is writing the two equations from the story.

The recipe: (1) Write down what x and y stand for (the problem often tells you). (2) Find the two separate facts in the story; each one becomes one equation. (3) Solve with any method. (4) Answer the actual question in words, with units ($, hours, people). (5) Sanity check: are the numbers reasonable?

Type 1: two purchases of the same two things

Twin: One group buys 3 adult and 2 child concert tickets for $64. Another buys 2 adult and 4 child tickets for $64. Find each price.

  1. x = adult ticket ($), y = child ticket ($).
  2. Each purchase is a fact: 3x + 2y = 64 and 2x + 4y = 64.
  3. Elimination: double the first → 6x + 4y = 128; subtract the second → 4x = 64 → x = 16. Then 2(16) + 4y = 64 → y = 8.
  4. Adult tickets cost $16, child tickets $8. Check: 3(16) + 2(8) = 48 + 16 = 64 ✓.

Follow-up questions like "how much for 2 adults and 5 kids?" just use your prices: 2(16) + 5(8) = $72.

Type 2: a starting fee plus a rate

Twin: Gym A charges $20 to sign up plus $15 a month. Gym B charges $50 to sign up plus $10 a month. When do they cost the same?

  1. x = months, y = total cost ($).
  2. Rate × x + starting fee: y = 15x + 20 and y = 10x + 50. These are slope-intercept lines: the rate is the slope, the fee is the y-intercept.
  3. Substitution: 15x + 20 = 10x + 50 → 5x = 30 → x = 6; y = 15(6) + 20 = 110.
  4. After 6 months both gyms cost $110. Before that Gym A is cheaper; after, Gym B is. That's where the two lines cross.

Type 3: a total count and a total cost

Twin: 12 students bought snacks. Some paid $3 and some paid $5, and they spent $46 in all. How many bought each?

  1. x = $3 buyers, y = $5 buyers.
  2. Two facts: the people add up, x + y = 12, and the money adds up, 3x + 5y = 46.
  3. Triple the first → 3x + 3y = 36; subtract from the second → 2y = 10 → y = 5, so x = 7.
  4. 7 students paid $3 and 5 paid $5. Check: 21 + 25 = 46 ✓.

Watch for this pattern: one equation counts things, the other counts money. Don't mix them in the same equation.

Type 4: "Is there enough information?"

Twin: On Monday a café sells 4 coffees and 6 muffins for $30. On Tuesday it sells 6 coffees and 9 muffins for $45. Can you find each price?

  1. 4x + 6y = 30 and 6x + 9y = 45.
  2. Look closely: Tuesday is exactly 1.5 times Monday (4×1.5 = 6, 6×1.5 = 9, 30×1.5 = 45). Multiply Monday by 3 and Tuesday by 2 and you get the same equation, 12x + 18y = 90, so subtracting leaves 0 = 0.
  3. No, there isn't enough information. The two days are really the same fact (the same line), so there are infinitely many possible price pairs. Tuesday adds no new information.
How to explain it in words: "The second equation is just a multiple of the first, so the lines are the same and the system has infinitely many solutions. We can't find a single price for each." If the multiples matched on the left but not the total, that would be parallel lines and no solution: the numbers would contradict each other.
Putting it together

Which method when?

If the system looks like…Use
The question says "by graphing," or you just need a pictureGraphing (exact only if the crossing is on grid lines)
One equation already says y = … or x = …, or a letter has no number in frontSubstitution
Both are in Ax + By = C form, especially with matching or opposite numbersElimination

Every method gives the same answer. Your worksheet tells you which one to use for each section, so show that method's steps.

Making up a system with a given answer

Twin: write a system whose solution is (2, 5). Pick any x and y combination and work out what it equals at that point:

  • x + y at (2, 5) is 2 + 5 = 7, so one equation is x + y = 7.
  • 2x − y at (2, 5) is 4 − 5 = −1, so another is 2x − y = −1.

Both are true at (2, 5), so (2, 5) is where they cross. Just make sure the two equations aren't multiples of each other, or you'd get the same line twice.
