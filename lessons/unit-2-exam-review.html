The thread through the whole unit: these "second-wave" civilizations were bigger empires than the First Civilizations, they invented ways to hold huge, mixed populations together (roads, bureaucracies, law, religion, philosophy), and their legacies are still alive. For each one, ask: how did they rule? who counted and who didn't? why did it end?

Blocks 1–3: the quiz material, short version

1 · The Classical Era (Strayer reading + intro slides)

  • Classical = bigger and more complex: empires, trade networks, armies, governments, plus lasting legacies (religions, philosophies, law, languages).
  • Stayed the same: monarchs, men over women, elites over everyone, slavery. No economic breakthrough: elites lived off peasant surplus, peasants knew gains would be taken, merchants were looked down on.
  • Where: centralized civilizations in Persia, China, India, the eastern Mediterranean and Mesoamerica. Nomads and settled farming civilizations met through trade, the spread of ideas and technology, and conflict, along grassland, desert and sea trade routes.
  • Changed: population growth, much larger empires, "wisdom traditions" (Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, Greek rationalism, Zoroastrianism, Judaism, Christianity), modest technology, denser exchange networks (goods, ideas, disease).

2 · Persia

  • Cyrus the Great ruled by tolerance (Cyrus Cylinder; freed the Jews), not Assyrian terror. Darius organized: 20 satrapies, satraps checked by the "Eyes and Ears of the King," standard coins, the Immortals, the Royal Road (Sardis to Susa, ~7 days for messengers). Zoroastrianism: good (Ahura Mazda) vs. evil, free will, final judgment. Herodotus was Greek, so check his bias.

3 · Greece and Alexander

  • Hard overland travel (no navigable rivers, a resource-poor land that drew Greeks to the sea) → independent poleis. Athens = democracy for free male citizens only (ostracism), slavery grew alongside it. Sparta = oligarchy and a military society over the helots; women freer. Persian Wars (Marathon 490 BCE, Thermopylae 480 BCE) → "East vs. West" story. Peloponnesian War weakened every city-state → let Alexander, then the Romans, conquer Greece → the Hellenistic Era. Judge Alexander's legacy (accomplishments vs. by-products; beware "great man history").

Block 4: Han China

From the "Han Dynasty China" slides, Getz's "Han Dynasty China" reading, and the "Golden Age of the Han" document worksheet.

Before the Han: the Qin put China back together

  • China was a First Civilization (Yellow River, about 2200 BCE). Its unity broke down into the Warring States period (to 221 BCE). Unlike Rome, the Han were restoring something old.
  • The Qin dynasty (221–206 BCE) under Shi Huangdi ("First Emperor") reunited it: put aristocrats under the state, started the Great Wall against northern nomads (his attacks united those nomads into the Xiongnu confederacy, a threat for centuries), standardized measurements, coins and language, and ruled by Legalism (strict rewards and punishments, a pessimistic view of human nature). He also killed opposing scholars and burned books. Effective, but harsh and short-lived. His tomb holds the Terra-Cotta Warriors.
  • Liu Bang, born a peasant, led the revolt and founded the Han (206/202 BCE – 220 CE), about 400 years. The imperial system the Han set up lasted into the twentieth century.
  • Geography: a fertile plain around the Yellow River and the Yangtze, protected by natural barriers (deserts, mountains, ocean). Farming fed a big population but left it open to drought and the effects of deforestation.

How the Han ruled

  • Shifted from Legalism to Confucianism as the state's philosophy. Confucianism was secular (not religious), taught obedience to parents and the emperor, used the Mandate of Heaven to legitimate emperors, and validated social inequality. Buddhism reached China during the Eastern Han and mixed with Confucianism and Daoism.
  • Mandate of Heaven: the emperor rules because heaven chose his dynasty. If he is cruel or unsuccessful, people can say he lost the Mandate and rebel.
  • Bureaucracy and the exams: the biggest, best-organized government of its time, bigger than Rome's. Officials got jobs by studying Confucian texts and passing an exam (a merit system). They collected taxes, enforced laws, defended the borders, judged cases, ran building projects and governed regions, and the bureaucracy grew because of rebellions, invasions and conquest. The catch: education cost money, so officials came mostly from the landlord class and became a privileged group.
  • Assimilation: the Han actively absorbed non-Chinese peoples into Chinese culture. Chinese people today still call themselves ethnically Han.

Society

  • An elite of male scholar-bureaucrats on top. Peasants were the vast majority and were praised by Confucianism (they feed the country) but vulnerable to nature, the state and landlords. Merchants were richer but viewed with suspicion (less virtuous). Under 1% were enslaved.

Why it's a "Golden Age"

  • Paper was invented (his worksheet's Doc 4 maps how papermaking spread) (and it's why we know so much about the Han). Silk-making, one of many new technologies; wealthy people wore brightly dyed silk. Merchants made China rich from trade that brought goods from as far as Rome. Han emperors conquered more territory. Canals and irrigation. Sima Qian, one of the first great historians. Poetry, pottery, sculpture, painting.

Collapse (220 CE)

  • Heavy taxes on farmers to pay for wars; the exam system became corrupt; the rich bought up land and pushed peasants into banditry or homelessness; the royal family fought for power.
  • The Yellow Turban Rebellion (184 CE), inspired by Daoism, demanded equality and shared property. It was crushed, but it wrecked the economy and weakened the state.
  • Also: overextension, epidemic disease, deforestation and soil erosion (it turned the Yellow River yellow-brown), floods and droughts, and raids by nomads like the Xiongnu.
  • China split into three kingdoms, but reunified about 350 years later (Sui, Tang, Song). Shared culture and the Confucian bureaucracy carried China from dynasty to dynasty.

How to answer a scenario question (a practice one, not from class)

Scenario: In a four-person group project, one member hasn't done any work and the deadline is in two days.

  • A Confucian would have the group leader set a good example and talk to the member about their duty to the group, aiming to restore harmony and proper roles. The fix is a better relationship.
  • A Legalist would set a clear rule (everyone's contribution is tracked) with a clear penalty (no contribution, no credit) and enforce it the same way for everyone. The fix is the rule.
  • A Daoist wouldn't force it: simplify the project, let each person do what flows naturally, and let natural consequences happen. The fix is less pushing.
  • "Which is most sustainable?" has no single right answer. Pick one and say why it lasts: for example, Legalism works fast but breeds resentment (like the Qin), while Confucian harmony keeps the group working together next time.

Block 6: Rome

From the "Ancient Rome" slides (Rome Notes). The Roman Empire OER article from "Intro to Rome" isn't online in Classroom, so use your own worksheet answers for its six questions.

The Republic (509 BCE – 27 BCE)

  • Began when the Romans threw off the Etruscans (509 BCE). Borrowed from Greece: warfare, gods (Zeus became Jupiter); conquered the last Greek city-state by 146 BCE.
  • Patricians (wealthy landowners) could vote and hold office in the Senate; plebeians (everyone else) could only vote, and patrician votes counted more. The paterfamilias (oldest male) had absolute authority over the family. Women were citizens but couldn't vote (no suffrage).
  • Roman law: equal treatment under the law, innocent until proven guilty, the accuser carries the burden of proof, punish actions, not thoughts.

Slavery

  • 30–40% of Rome's population by the common era: conquered peoples, chattel slavery, gladiators. Not tied to race. Even modest families owned two or three people. Slavery on this scale didn't happen again until the antebellum South, the Caribbean and Brazil. His notes trace slavery's growth to war, patriarchy and private property.
  • Spartacus's rebellion (73 BCE): started with 70 gladiators, grew to about 120,000. Crassus crushed it and crucified up to 6,000. Nothing that big happened again until the Haitian Revolution (1790s). His notes: "great man history" hides this kind of resistance from below.

Expansion breaks the Republic

  • Rome called its wars "defensive." Generals recruited soldiers with promises of land, so soldiers became loyal to their general, not the state. Each conquest created new borders to defend, which needed more conquest.
  • Rich families bought up huge estates worked by slaves from the conquests; displaced small farmers crowded into cities to compete for work. Growing wealth gaps made the Republic unstable.

From Republic to Empire

  • Julius Caesar: a winning general who became dictator (46 BCE), then "dictator for life." A populist: very rich but appealing to the poor (public jobs, free grain, entertainment, simple slogans like "veni, vidi, vici"). Stabbed by senators on the Ides of March, 44 BCE. His name became the word for ruler (caesar, tsar, kaiser).
  • His grandnephew Octavian beat his rivals (31 BCE) and became Caesar Augustus. He kept the forms of the Republic (the Senate) but held the real power. Emperors ruled from then on.
  • Characteristics of empire (his list): aggressive conquest and extraction, many peoples and cultures, imposed peace and security, masculinity defined as conquest, patriarchy.
  • Pax Romana (about 31 BCE–180 CE): two centuries of peace that boosted trade and living standards. 50,000 miles of roads, arches, concrete, aqueducts (some still used).

Christianity and Rome

  • Jesus (born about 5 BCE in Judea) appealed to the poor. Pontius Pilate executed him on political charges (stirring unrest, claiming to be king). He didn't claim to be divine or mean to found a new religion; Jewish authorities in Jerusalem handed him to Pilate. Paul spread the faith to non-Jews along Roman roads. Both Jesus and Paul treated women as equals; later leaders folded the faith into the patriarchy.
  • Christians were persecuted for refusing to worship Roman gods and the emperor, yet by 380 CE Christianity was the empire's official religion.

Block 7: Why Rome fell (and how it compares with Han)

These are the causes from his slides. Sorting them is your job in class: see Block 9.

  • Political and military chaos: short-lived, bad emperors (41 emperors or claimants between 235 and 284 CE); disloyal soldiers; Germanic warriors brought into the legions.
  • Economic decline: fighting with Germanic tribes disrupted farming and trade; emperors put less and less silver in coins, so money lost value (inflation) and people went back to barter; taxes fell on the poor while big landowners dodged them; no more expansion meant no new gold; the empire was too big and expensive to run, with no technological breakthrough to raise productivity.
  • People left the cities for the countryside and the protection of rich landowners.
  • Environment: smelting, large-scale farming and a growing population caused deforestation and soil erosion; lead pollution from smelting and lead pipes and pots (found in Roman burials as far away as Greenland; ice caps show more lead in the air in Roman times); climate swings after 250 CE hurt farming.
  • The split and the end: Diocletian split the empire in two (around 284 CE). The West fell to Germanic tribes in 476 CE and broke into small, decentralized states (feudalism). The East (Byzantine Empire) lasted until the Ottoman Turks took it in the mid-1400s.
  • The big contrast with Han: China reunified; Europe never rebuilt the unity of its classical empire.

Block 9: The writing piece (intro paragraphs)

This week's class work builds an intro paragraph for his essential question: "Why did the Roman Empire fall? Were internal or external causes more important?" The exam may ask you to write. Here is the method, practiced on a different empire so the Rome one stays yours.

The four ingredients (his slides)

  1. Hook: relevant and short: a vignette, a striking number, a rhetorical question, a misconception revealed.
  2. Context: 2–3 sentences of who, what, where, when, specific and relevant to the prompt; the "when" should cover roughly the 100–200 years before.
  3. Thesis: a direct, arguable claim that acknowledges complexity ("Although…, …"), answering "how so?"
  4. Roadmap: your 2–3 points in the order you'll argue them (it can be rolled into the thesis).

His feedback on the Early Humans DBQ said to fix missing point sentences, evidence dropped in without an introduction, and missing intro elements.

Practice question: "Were internal or external causes more important to the fall of the Han dynasty?"

Internal (inside the empire)

  • Heavy taxes on peasants to pay for wars
  • Corrupt exams and officials
  • Land grabbed by the rich
  • Royal family fighting for power
  • Yellow Turban Rebellion
  • Deforestation, floods, drought, disease

External (from outside)

  • Raids by the Xiongnu and other nomads
  • The cost of defending a huge frontier (the Great Wall)

Notice the sorting takes judgment: is the cost of defending the frontier internal (a money problem) or external (a nomad problem)? Explaining that kind of overlap is what "acknowledges complexity" means.

A model intro paragraph

HookIn 184 CE, peasants inspired by Daoism rose up in the Yellow Turban Rebellion, demanding equality, harmony and shared property.
ContextFor nearly four hundred years, the Han dynasty had ruled China through a Confucian bureaucracy of exam-trained officials, defending its northern frontier against the nomadic Xiongnu while peasant farmers fed a population of more than fifty million. By the second century CE, wealthy families were buying up the land those farmers worked, and the court was divided by rival factions.
ThesisAlthough wars and the fight against the Xiongnu nomads were costly, the Han dynasty fell mainly because of internal decay,
Roadmapas heavy taxes and land seizures turned peasants into rebels, corruption hollowed out the bureaucracy, and environmental damage left the state unable to survive floods and droughts.

Now try the same four steps for Rome. Use the causes in Block 7, decide which are internal and which external, and build your own thesis.

8.2 + 9.2 study guide and 18-question quiz

Self-check: answer first, then peek

1

How did the Qin dynasty reunify China, and why didn't it last?

Show answer

Shi Huangdi conquered the Warring States, put aristocrats under the state, standardized measurements, coins and language, began the Great Wall, and ruled by Legalism (strict laws, harsh punishments). It worked for unifying China but was so harsh (killing scholars, burning books) that it collapsed after his death, and Liu Bang's revolt founded the Han.

2

What is the Mandate of Heaven, and how could it justify a rebellion?

Show answer

The belief that heaven chooses a dynasty to rule. A good emperor keeps it; a cruel or unsuccessful one can be said to have lost it, which gives people a reason to overthrow him.

3

How did the Han choose government officials, and what was the weakness of that system?

Show answer

Officials studied Confucian texts and passed exams, a merit system that gave China better-trained officials than anywhere else. But schooling cost money, so most officials came from landlord families and became a self-perpetuating privileged class; late in the Han the exams became corrupt.

4

Name two achievements that make the Han a "Golden Age."

Show answer

Any two: paper (and its spread); silk-making; trade that reached as far as Rome; territorial expansion; canals and irrigation; the historian Sima Qian; poetry and the arts; the large, organized bureaucracy.

5

How did Confucianism rank peasants and merchants, and why?

Show answer

Peasants were respected because they fed the country (even though they were poor and vulnerable). Merchants were often richer but seen as less virtuous.

6

Confucianism, Legalism, Daoism: where does each say order comes from?

Show answer

Confucianism: from good people: moral example, proper roles (filial piety), education. Legalism: from strict laws and harsh punishments enforced by a strong ruler. Daoism: from not forcing things: living simply in harmony with the Dao (wu wei, yin and yang).

7

Why was the Yellow Turban Rebellion important, and which philosophy inspired it?

Show answer

A huge peasant uprising in 184 CE, inspired by Daoism, demanding equality and shared property. It was crushed, but it devastated the economy and weakened the state, helping bring down the Han in 220 CE.

8

Patricians vs. plebeians: who could do what in the Roman Republic?

Show answer

Patricians (wealthy landowners) could vote and hold office in the Senate. Plebeians (farmers, merchants, everyone else) could only vote, and patrician votes counted more. Women were citizens but could not vote.

9

Name two principles of Roman law.

Show answer

Any two: equal treatment under the law; innocent until proven guilty; the accuser carries the burden of proof; people are punished for actions, not thoughts.

10

How did conquest and expansion destabilize the Roman Republic?

Show answer

Generals paid soldiers with land, so armies became loyal to generals, not the Republic. Every conquest needed more conquest to defend it. Rich families built huge estates worked by conquered slaves, pushing small farmers into the cities, so wealth gaps grew. Ambitious generals like Caesar could then seize power.

11

What did Julius Caesar and Augustus each change?

Show answer

Caesar made himself dictator (then dictator for life), ending republican rule, and won the poor with jobs, grain and entertainment; senators killed him in 44 BCE. Augustus (Octavian) beat his rivals and became the first emperor, keeping the Senate for show while holding real power, and began the Pax Romana.

12

Why would the Pax Romana and Roman roads help Rome rule?

Show answer

His slides say the peace boosted trade and raised living standards, and Paul spread Christianity along Roman roads. The rest is his think-question for you ("How would roads help the Caesar rule?"): reason it out from what roads let a ruler do, and compare the Persian Royal Road.

13

Why were early Christians persecuted, and what had changed by 380 CE?

Show answer

They refused to worship the Roman gods and the emperor. By 380 CE Christianity had become the empire's official religion.

14

Name three causes of Rome's fall and say whether each is internal or external.

Show answer

Examples: bad, short-lived emperors (internal); debased coins and inflation (internal); taxes on the poor and wealth gaps (internal); environmental damage (internal); Germanic invasions (external). Some overlap: Germanic soldiers inside the army are both. Any well-explained sort works.

15

Rome and Han: what is the big difference in their views of good government?

Show answer

Rome: good laws, applied equally to everyone, make good government. Han China (Confucian): good men, moral and educated officials leading by example, make good government.

16

How did Rome and Han treat conquered or outside peoples differently?

Show answer

Han China assimilated them into Chinese culture (people today call themselves ethnically Han). Rome extended citizenship as a legal status and let peoples keep their own cultures and religions, so the empire stayed much more diverse (Greek, Persian, Egyptian, Christian).

17

Compare the role of slavery in Rome and Han China.

Show answer

Rome was a true slave society: 30–40% of the population, mostly conquered peoples, chattel slavery (Spartacus's revolt shows its scale). In Han China, under 1% were enslaved; most labor came from peasant farmers.

18

What happened after each empire fell, and why the difference?

Show answer

China reunified about 350 years later (Sui, Tang, Song). The Western Roman Empire broke into decentralized states (feudalism) and was never rebuilt; the eastern half survived as the Byzantine Empire until the 1400s. The difference: China had more shared culture, and the Confucian bureaucracy gave each new dynasty a ready-made way to govern.

19

From earlier in the unit: how did Persia's Cyrus and Darius hold a huge, mixed empire together?

Show answer

Cyrus ruled by tolerance (respected local gods, freed the Jews). Darius built the system: 20 satrapies under satraps, checked by the "Eyes and Ears of the King," standard coins and taxes, the Royal Road and a standing army. (Compare: Han used a bureaucracy and Confucianism; Rome used roads, law and citizenship.)

20

Write a one-sentence thesis (with "Although") for: "Were internal or external causes more important to the fall of Rome?"

Show answer

This one is yours. Check it has: a clear side (internal or external), an "Although…" clause that admits the other side, and 2–3 reasons you could argue in order. Compare its shape with the Han model in Block 9, not its words.
