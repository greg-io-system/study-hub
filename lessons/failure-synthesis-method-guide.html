Failure Synthesis — a method guide

The capstone of the whole failure unit. One move: understand each writer's position, then move past it to yours. That move — not agreeing, not disagreeing — is what Mr. Evans calls sophistication.

New to how he builds an assignment? See "How Mr. Evans's assignments work" and the "Loser Edit" method guide on the AP English card — this is the same synthesis move, one level harder.

The prompt, plainly: What is your position on Ryan Leak's position on failure? Read that twice. It is not "do you agree with Leak?" Mr. Evans calls simple agree/disagree an "elementary view." The task is two-step: (1) understand what Leak actually claims about failure, then (2) stake out where you stand in relation to that claim — using Fratto, Gladwell, and Whitehead as the voices that sharpen your position.

1 · Path 1 vs. Path 2 (the shift he's asking for)

Mr. Evans says outright he is moving the class from Path 1 to Path 2. Know which one you're writing:

Path 1 — about the sources
A tour of what each writer said. "Leak says chase failure. Fratto says measure adaptability. Gladwell says difficulties can be desirable." Accurate, and still a book report. The sources are the subject.
Path 2 — YOUR position, sources as support
You have a claim about failure, and you pull each writer in because they help you make it. The sources are your evidence. You are the subject — your exigence, your curiosity, driving the paragraph.

He even says you might "fail" trying Path 2 — which is the whole unit's joke, and its point. Aim for Path 2.

2 · Know each voice's position in ONE sentence (the "They Say")

You can't put writers in conversation until you can state each one's position cleanly. Do this from your own notes first — here's the shape to aim for (in your words, not copied):

Ryan Leak — "Chasing Failure" (cite the book / the idea, NOT the video)

Pursue failure on purpose; the fear of it is what actually holds people back, and the people we admire have failed the most. This is your anchor — every other source gets pulled toward it.

Natalie Fratto — "3 ways to measure your adaptability" (TED)

"AQ" (adaptability quotient) can be measured. Her three markers — your notes should name all three in your own words, because writing a term verbatim won't put it in your memory or your argument.

Malcolm Gladwell — David and Goliath (excerpt)

Some setbacks are "desirable difficulties." Learning forced by necessity (compensation learning) is more powerful than learning that comes easily (capitalization learning). Where do these land on your "failure continuum"?

Colson Whitehead — "The Loser Edit" (carried in from last week)

We curate a redemption arc after we fall — how we present failure vs. how we live it.

Notice the blanks in his email ("Leak's position on ______"): he wants YOU to name the exact thing the conversation is about — risk? growth? identity? Fill that blank yourself; it becomes your throughline.

3 · The synthesis move: put a source in conversation with Leak

The engine of Path 2 is one repeatable move: take a specific idea from one source and aim it at Leak's claim, ending on your sentence. Here it is on a made-up pair, so the method is visible and your real texts stay yours:

Made-up anchor
"Fall Forward," Dana Ruiz (2018): argues you should seek out failure because comfort teaches you nothing.
Made-up second source
"The Grit Meter," Dr. Ellis (2021): claims resilience is a trainable skill you can score, like a muscle.
Worked synthesis (on the made-up pair)

Ruiz tells you to chase failure, but Ellis quietly changes the terms: if resilience is a "trainable skill you can score," then failure isn't a thing you bravely run toward — it's reps, the boring daily kind. Put next to Ellis, Ruiz's grand "fall forward" starts to sound like a slogan for something that is actually just practice. And that's where I land: I don't think we need to romanticize failure to get its benefit — we need to make it routine enough to stop fearing.

See the shape: specific idea from source B → aimed at the anchor's claim → last sentence is clearly mine. That last sentence is the position.

Now you (on your real sources)

Run the same move with Fratto, then Gladwell, then Whitehead, each aimed at Leak. Guiding questions — answers are yours to write:

  • Fratto → Leak: if adaptability can be measured, does that support Leak's "chase failure," or complicate it? (Is chasing failure the same as being adaptable, or a louder version of it?)
  • Gladwell → Leak: is failure a "desirable difficulty"? Does compensation learning explain why Leak's chased failures paid off — or show that the difficulty, not the chasing, is what matters?
  • Whitehead → Leak: Leak tells a clean comeback story. Is that a "loser edit" of his own failures? Does that make his advice more honest or less?
  • Your throughline: after all three, what do YOU think about Leak's position on failure? End each paragraph on your sentence, not theirs.

5 · Don't lose the thesis point

He front-loaded the thesis reminder on purpose — so you'd use it in this reply. On the AP essays (there are 3, each scored on a 6-point scale), a real thesis is one guaranteed point — the difference between the 0 and the 1. A thesis that only summarizes ("these writers all discuss failure") scores the 0. A thesis that takes a position ("failure only teaches when it's involuntary — which is where Leak's advice breaks down") scores the 1. Put a real, arguable claim near the top and let the paragraphs defend it.

6 · Before you reply to his email — the check

  • Can I state Leak's position on failure in one sentence, in my own words?
  • Did I fill his blank — Leak's position on ______ — with a specific idea that is my throughline?
  • Fratto: did I name her three AQ markers in my own words and aim them at Leak?
  • Gladwell: did I place desirable difficulties / compensation vs. capitalization on my failure continuum?
  • Whitehead: did I connect "the loser edit" to how Leak tells his failure story?
  • Path test: is this about the sources (Path 1) or about MY position with the sources as support (Path 2)?
  • Is there a real thesis up top that takes a position, not a summary?
  • Did I actually use the They Say / I Say templates — a turn word in each key sentence?
  • Am I building on my earlier Leak notes, not starting from scratch? (He asked you to reward your past self.)
