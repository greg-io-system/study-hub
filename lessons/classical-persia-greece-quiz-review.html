The review, in four blocks

1 · The Classical Era (500 BCE – 500 CE): the big picture

From the Strayer reading "The Classical Era in World History" + the Classical Societies Intro slides.

  • What makes a society "classical": it's bigger and more complex than the First Civilizations: empires, developed trade networks, organized militaries, complex governments. These societies are also celebrated for their legacy: languages, politics, religion, law, philosophy and literature that still shape the world today.
  • Where: centralized civilizations in Persia, China, India, the eastern Mediterranean (Greece/Rome), and Mesoamerica. These are the "second wave" that replaced the First Civilizations. Strayer calls them a "second wave"; a third wave follows from about 500 to 1500 CE.
  • Continuities (what did NOT change): monarchs still ruled; men dominated women; a sharp divide between the elite and everyone else; slavery persisted. There was no big economic or technological breakthrough like the Agricultural Revolution, because elites lived off peasants' surplus, peasants knew any gains would be taken, and merchants were held down by states and looked down on.
  • Changes (what DID change):
    • Population grew faster than ever, but unevenly.
    • Much larger empires (Roman, Persian, Indian, Chinese) that ruled many different peoples.
    • "Wisdom traditions": Confucianism, Daoism, Hinduism, Buddhism, Greek rationalism, Judaism, Zoroastrianism, Christianity. These are the most lasting legacy.
    • Modest technology. China led: paper, the compass, iron casting, the wheelbarrow, gunpowder. India pioneered crystallized sugar and cotton textiles; Rome excelled at roads, aqueducts and engineering.
    • Denser networks of exchange: trade routes carried goods, ideas, religions and disease. Example: a plague hit Athens in 430 BCE. Another example is sugar spreading from India to the Middle East, then Europe, then the Americas.
  • Nomads and settled peoples: when nomadic peoples met settled, farming civilizations, three things happened: trade, diffusion of ideas and technologies, and conflict. Trade routes ran across grasslands, deserts and seas.
  • Why "classical"? These legacies are still alive. Iran celebrated the Persian Empire's 2,500th anniversary in 1971, and China celebrated Confucius's birthday in 2004.

2 · Persia (the Achaemenid Empire)

From the Persia notes + the Herodotus "Customs of the Persians" source analysis.

  • Origins: an Indo-European people of southwestern Iran, originally nomadic pastoralists (horses, archery, clans). They paid tribute to the Medes until Cyrus revolted in 550 BCE.
  • Cyrus the Great (r. 559–530 BCE): conquered Media (550), Lydia (546) and Babylon (539). His son Cambyses took Egypt (525). The empire stretched from Anatolia and Egypt to northern India.
  • Rule by tolerance, not terror: unlike the Assyrians (deportations, torture), Cyrus respected local gods and customs. The Cyrus Cylinder shows him entering Babylon as a liberator. He freed the Jews from the Babylonian Captivity and paid to rebuild their Temple. That is why the Medes, Babylonians and Jews accepted him, and why he was called "the Great."
  • Zoroastrianism: founded by Zoroaster (Zarathustra). It holds that the world is a battleground between good and evil: Ahura Mazda (truth, light) against Angra Mainyu / Ahriman (deception). It teaches free will ("good thoughts, good words, good deeds") and a final judgment at the end of time.
  • Darius I (r. 521–486 BCE), the organizer:
    • Split the empire into 20 satrapies (provinces), each run by a satrap (governor) who collected taxes, kept justice and security, and recruited soldiers. Lower posts stayed with local elites.
    • Checks on the satraps: separate military commanders, plus inspectors called the "Eyes and Ears of the King."
    • Standard coins (the daric) and a standard tax system.
    • A professional standing army, including the Immortals: 10,000 elite troops, always kept at exactly 10,000 because every loss was replaced at once.
  • The Royal Road: about 1,677 miles from Sardis (Lydia) to Susa, with 111 relay stations offering fresh horses. Royal messengers covered it in about 7 days; caravans took about a month. It let the king hear about revolts fast, move armies, and helped trade.
  • Herodotus was a Greek historian writing about Persians, Greece's enemy. When you analyze him, ask about his point of view and bias: who wrote it, for whom, why, and how being Greek shapes what he says about Persian customs.

3 · Ancient Greece (750–336 BCE)

From the Greece teacher notes + the class's Greek student presentations.

  • Before: the Minoans (Crete), then the Mycenaeans, then a Dark Age (about 1150–800 BCE), which ended when Phoenician ships arrived.
  • Geography → politics: mountains and no navigable rivers divided Greece and left it resource-poor, so Greeks turned to the sea for trade (metals, timber, grain). There was no unified country. Each polis (city-state) was independent, and people saw themselves as Athenians or Spartans, not "Greeks," until the Persian Wars.
  • Acropolis vs. agora: the Acropolis is a fortified hilltop used for religion (the Parthenon). The agora is the marketplace and public square, the political, commercial and social heart of the city.
  • Religion: polytheistic, with human-like gods led by Zeus. Greeks offered sacrifices and asked oracles for advice, such as the Oracle of Apollo at Delphi, where the priestess Pythia spoke. Delphi was called the omphalos ("center") of the world.
  • Who counted: only free adult male citizens had rights, about 10–15% of Athens. About one-third of Athens was enslaved; slavery grew alongside democracy. Women married in their mid-teens and upper-class women stayed indoors.
  • Athens — democracy: Cleisthenes, the "father of democracy," set up rule by the citizens: an assembly, a council and courts. Ostracism was a vote to send away the man seen as the biggest threat to democracy for 10 years. Athens is also known for Greek rationalism: logic, argument and questioning everything. Socrates wrote nothing; his student Plato wrote dialogues. Together they mark a shift from myth to reason.
  • Sparta — oligarchy: ruled by a few (two kings + a Council of Elders). A military society with a permanent army. About 10,000 Spartans vs. 200,000 helots (state-owned slaves), controlled by force. Coins and trade were banned to keep citizens equal. Spartan women had more freedom than Athenian women: they could own land, did sports, and moved freely.
  • Olympics: started in 776 BCE to honor Zeus, held every 4 years, and open only to free Greek men. The Romans banned them around 393 CE.
  • Corinth: had two harbors and sat near the Isthmus, which made it a rich trade hub. Corinth pushed Sparta into the Peloponnesian War. Thebes: its general Epaminondas and the Sacred Band broke Spartan dominance at Leuctra (371 BCE).
  • Persian Wars (499–449 BCE):
    • Began with the Ionian Revolt (499): Greeks under Persian rule rebelled and Athens helped them, so Darius retaliated.
    • Marathon (490): Athenian hoplites in a phalanx beat a much larger Persian army by attacking from strong wings. Pheidippides ran about 26 miles to Athens with the news, which inspired the modern marathon.
    • Thermopylae (480): Leonidas and 300 Spartans (about 7,000 Greeks total) held a narrow pass against Xerxes. The Greeks lost, but showed Persia could be beaten.
    • Salamis: poor rowers (thetes) won the naval battle and then demanded full citizenship, which radicalized Athenian democracy.
    • The victory created the "East vs. West" story (Persia = Asia and despotism; Greece = Europe and freedom). Your notes call this Eurocentrism.
  • Peloponnesian War (431–404 BCE): the Athenian empire vs. a Sparta-led alliance, with Persia backing Sparta. Athens acted ruthlessly, for example by slaughtering neutral Melos. Sparta won, but the war weakened every city-state, so Philip II of Macedon, and then Alexander, could take over.

4 · Alexander of Macedon

From the teacher notes + your "Alexander the Great? Evaluation of Legacy" worksheet. Your own worksheet answers are your best notes here.

  • Son of Philip II, tutored by Aristotle. He never lost a battle and conquered the Persian Empire.
  • The by-product: the Hellenistic Era, when Greek culture spread and blended across his conquests.
  • The worksheet's frame: separate accomplishments (what he actually did) from by-products (what happened as a result). Then judge his legacy: how should he be remembered? Your notes warn about "great man history," telling the story only through one famous man.

Self-check: answer first, then peek

1

What makes the "classical" civilizations (500 BCE–500 CE) different from the First Civilizations?

Show answer

They were bigger and more complex: large empires, developed trade networks, organized militaries and complex governments. They also left lasting legacies in religion, philosophy, politics, law and language that still shape the world today.

2

Strayer says that from far away the classical era looks like "not much" changed. What stayed the same, and why was there no big economic breakthrough?

Show answer

Monarchs still ruled, men dominated women, elites stayed far above everyone else, and slavery continued. No breakthrough happened because elites lived off the peasants' surplus and had no reason to innovate, peasants knew any gains would be taken, and merchants were controlled and looked down on.

3

Name two things that DID change in the classical era.

Show answer

Any two of: faster population growth; much larger empires; new wisdom traditions (Confucianism, Buddhism, Greek rationalism, Zoroastrianism, etc.); modest technology (Chinese paper and the compass, Indian sugar, Roman roads and aqueducts); denser trade and exchange networks that spread goods, religions and disease.

4

When nomadic peoples met settled agricultural civilizations, what three kinds of interaction happened?

Show answer

Trade, diffusion (the spread of ideas and technologies), and conflict.

5

How did Cyrus the Great's way of ruling conquered peoples differ from the Assyrians', and give one example.

Show answer

The Assyrians ruled by terror (deportations, torture). Cyrus ruled by tolerance: he honored local gods and customs and acted as a protector rather than an oppressor. Examples: he entered Babylon as a "liberator" and honored its god Marduk (the Cyrus Cylinder), and he freed the Jews from captivity and funded the rebuilding of their Temple.

6

What were satrapies and satraps, and how did Darius keep satraps from getting too powerful?

Show answer

Darius divided the empire into 20 provinces (satrapies), each run by a governor (satrap) who collected taxes, kept order and justice, and recruited soldiers. To check them he used separate military commanders and royal inspectors, the "Eyes and Ears of the King," who made surprise visits and reported straight to the king.

7

What was the Royal Road, and why did it matter?

Show answer

A road of about 1,677 miles from Sardis to Susa with 111 relay stations that had fresh horses. Messengers crossed it in about a week. It let the king learn about revolts quickly, move armies fast, and boosted long-distance trade, which helped hold a huge empire together.

8

Who were the Immortals?

Show answer

Persia's elite 10,000-man professional infantry, part of a permanent standing army. Any soldier who died or fell sick was immediately replaced, so the unit always stayed at 10,000. That's where the name comes from.

9

What are the main beliefs of Zoroastrianism?

Show answer

Founded by Zoroaster. The world is a battle between good, Ahura Mazda (truth and light), and evil, Angra Mainyu / Ahriman (deception). People have free will and should live by "good thoughts, good words, good deeds." At the end of time good wins and everyone faces a final judgment.

10

Why should you be careful using Herodotus as a source on the Persians?

Show answer

Herodotus was a Greek historian, and Greece was at war with Persia. His point of view and Greek audience may have shaped him toward bias: making Persians seem foreign, strange or inferior. You check this by asking who wrote it, when, for whom, and why.

11

How did geography shape Greek politics?

Show answer

Mountains split Greece into isolated valleys, so it formed many independent city-states (poleis) instead of one country, and people identified with their own polis. Poor soil and no navigable rivers pushed Greeks to the sea for trade and colonies.

12

Compare Athens and Sparta: government, society and women.

Show answer

Athens: a democracy of male citizens (assembly, council, courts; ostracism), a center of trade, art and philosophy; women were secluded at home and needed a male guardian. Sparta: an oligarchy (two kings + Council of Elders), a military society resting on a huge population of state-owned slaves (helots); coins and trade banned; women had more freedom (they owned land, played sports, moved freely).

13

What was ostracism, and what was it for?

Show answer

A yearly vote in Athens where citizens picked the man they saw as the biggest threat to democracy. The "winner" was exiled for 10 years. Its purpose was to stop would-be tyrants before they could take power.

14

How could a society that invented democracy also depend so heavily on slavery and exclusion?

Show answer

Democracy in Athens was only for free adult male citizens, about 10–15% of the population. Enslaved people (about a third of Athens) and excluded women did the labor that gave citizens time for politics. Your notes say slavery grew alongside democracy and citizenship. (This was a class discussion question, so be ready to explain it in your own words.)

15

Give the cause and the outcome of the Persian Wars, and one key battle.

Show answer

Cause: the Ionian Revolt (499 BCE), when Greeks under Persian rule rebelled and Athens helped them, plus Persia's push into Europe. Battles: Marathon (490), where the Athenian phalanx beat a larger Persian force; Thermopylae (480), where Leonidas and 300 Spartans held the pass; and Salamis at sea. Outcome: the Greek city-states cooperated and drove Persia back. Athens became a more radical democracy because the poor rowers demanded rights, and the war created the "East vs. West" idea.

16

What was the Peloponnesian War, and why does it matter for what came next?

Show answer

A war (431–404 BCE) between Athens and the Sparta-led alliance, with Persia backing Sparta. Sparta won, but the war exhausted all the city-states, so Philip II of Macedon and then Alexander could conquer Greece. That led to the Hellenistic Era.

17

Alexander: what is one accomplishment, one by-product, and how might you argue he should be remembered?

Show answer

Accomplishment: he conquered the Persian Empire without losing a battle. By-product: Greek culture spread across his conquests (the Hellenistic Era). Legacy: any position works if you back it with examples, such as a brilliant conqueror who spread culture, or a destroyer whose fame shows "great man history." Use your worksheet.

18

Quick match: Olympics · Delphi · Acropolis · Agora.

Show answer

Olympics: religious festival for Zeus, every 4 years, 776 BCE to about 393 CE, free Greek men only. Delphi: Apollo's oracle (the Pythia), the "center of the world." Acropolis: fortified hilltop for religion (the Parthenon). Agora: marketplace and public square, the political and social heart of the city.
