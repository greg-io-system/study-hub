MLA format, Mr. Evans's way

What his one-page MLA Template asks for, what it looks like on the page, and his notes on citing sources and marking ethos. His emails of 10/5, 10/6 and 10/9 link the template: "Use this MLA Template to help you with headings, headers, and formatting."

The big idea: Mr. Evans (10/5): "Please note, I am not grading you based on your formatting; however, credibility can be lost with sloppy formatting." Formatting is an ethos choice.

How-to (Google Docs)

His template's screenshot shows the Google Docs Format menu. The items you need are all in it:

  1. Header / page #: Format → Page numbers (the item his screenshot highlights), then your last name in the header, top right.
  2. Font: Times New Roman, 12 pt.
  3. Spacing: Format → Line & paragraph spacing → double.
  4. Heading: the four lines at the top left.
  5. Title: Format → Align & indent → center, for the title line only.

Citing sources without a Works Cited

The citation block in his template is the Rhetorical Précis, Sentence 1: the author, the title, the publication date in parentheses, "a rhetorically accurate verb (such as 'assert,' 'argue,' 'suggest,' 'imply,' 'claim,' etc.); and a THAT clause containing the major assertion."

Author + title + (date) + an accurate verb (argues, claims, suggests, asserts) + that + the main point.

A made-up example of the pattern:

In “Why Boredom Is Good for You” (2019), Sam Rivera argues that “a little nothing to do is where ideas start” (3).
  • In his template's example, a quoted line ends with the page number in parentheses, then the period.
  • Naming the author (Mr. Evans, 9/1, "Learn this"): the first time, use their full name; the second time, the last name only; after that, if you have not changed authors, a pronoun.
  • Quoting a quote (Mr. Evans, 10/9): "In her essay, A Nation of Wimps, Marano quotes psychologist David Elkind, professor at Tufts University, who argued, '_________'"

Marking ethos in blue (position papers)

Mr. Evans (10/5, 10/8, 10/9): "As you intentionally appeal to ethos, those points in your paper will be changed to the color blue by you, the author."

How-to: select the sentence or phrase and change its text color to blue. Use the same blue every time.

What he said about ethos:

  • "Your credibility is earned or lost in your tone, in your logic, in your interaction with sources, in your awareness and engagement with alternative perspectives, in your word choice, in your personal experience, etc. It all depends on who your audience is" (10/8).
  • "Ethos is found in the Writing. Ethos sections help; however, they do not inherently lead to credibility" (10/5).
  • "Every choice can increase or decrease your credibility depending on YOUR AUDIENCE" (10/5).
  • "a text does not have ethos, but instead an author appeals to ethos" (10/9).
  • "Before you identify BLUE, you must have a layered understanding of who your exigence, audience, and purpose" (10/8).

A made-up paragraph (on a different topic) with its ethos marked:

Rivera claims that boredom breeds ideas, and the research she cites mostly supports her. I spent last summer with no phone for two weeks at a camp job, and the first four days were miserable — so I don't think boredom is automatically good for anyone. But by the second week I was writing every night. Critics are right that one summer is not a study; still, it matches what Rivera found: the payoff comes after the discomfort, not instead of it.

Made-up example. Matched to his list: the first blue sentence is personal experience; the second is awareness and engagement with alternative perspectives. The unmarked sentences are the argument and the evidence.

The trap: Mr. Evans (10/8): "Your BLUE must move well beyond who you are ('I am a member of Gen-Z') and toward how you develop credibility as an academic."
