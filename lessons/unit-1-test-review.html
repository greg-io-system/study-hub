The review, in four blocks

1 · How historians think

  • Causation: historians weigh short-term triggers AND long-term conditions behind an event — not just the spark.
  • "Hidden history": the experiences of groups left out of traditional written records — worth recovering because the official record is incomplete.
  • Studying history builds the critical-thinking & analysis skills employers want.

2 · Human origins & migration

  • Earliest humans emerged in Africa (processed ochre = early activity), then migrated worldwide in the Paleolithic.
  • Routes: land bridges during the Ice Age (Eastern Siberia → the Americas); boats to Australia & the Pacific.
  • Paleolithic society: small bands of 25–50, hunting & gathering, highly egalitarian, fairly equal gender relations, seasonally mobile, no permanent leaders, a cyclical view of time, strong ritual life.

3 · The Agricultural Revolution

  • ~4,000–12,000 years ago, developed independently in several regions (Fertile Crescent, NE Africa, the Americas).
  • Let people settle permanently → denser populations, food surplus, specialized labor, more complex social structures, and personal property.
  • Compare three ways of life:
    • Paleolithic — nomadic hunter-gatherers, little hierarchy.
    • Agricultural village — permanent settlement, crops, kinship, basic organization.
    • Pastoral — herders who move their animals (sheep, cattle, goats) to grazing land. Mobile like Paleolithic people, but they produce food from herds instead of foraging. They trade meat, milk and hides to farmers for grain — yet also clash with them over land, water and grazing rights (herds trample fields; farmers fence off the range). That nomad-vs-farmer tension recurs all through world history.
  • The environmental cost: farming reshaped the land — not always for the better. Centuries of intensive irrigation caused salinization (salt building up until soil goes barren) and soil exhaustion/erosion, degrading the very farmland it depended on — an early case of a society damaging its own ecosystem.

4 · The first civilizations

  • A civilization = a complex society with cities, specialized workers, complex institutions, writing/record-keeping, and advanced technology.
  • Sumer (Mesopotamia, ~3500 BCE): city-states, cuneiform. Egypt (the Nile): unified under pharaohs, irrigation, hieroglyphs. Also Indus Valley, Shang China, Olmec, Norte Chico/Chavín.
  • Social classes: upper (rulers, priests, nobles) · middle (artisans, merchants, farmers) · lower (laborers, enslaved people, POWs). Societies were patriarchal; women's roles varied by class.
  • Power & law: rulers claimed divine authority to justify their rule; early law codes (Hammurabi) put inequality in writing — punishments differed by class.

Self-check — answer in your own words first

Drawn from Mr. Angelopulos's Unit 1 review questions. Say your answer out loud or jot it, THEN reveal to check. Model answers are a guide, not the only right wording.

1

How did Ice Age environmental changes shape where humans migrated?

Show answer

Lower sea levels during the Ice Age exposed land bridges (e.g. Eastern Siberia to Alaska), opening routes into the Americas on foot; elsewhere people used boats to reach Australia and Pacific islands. Climate and coastlines, not just wandering, set the paths.

2

What routes and methods did early humans use to reach oceanic and isolated regions?

Show answer

Overland via land bridges when seas were low, and watercraft/boats to cross open water to Australia and the Pacific. Migration out of Africa spread humans across every habitable continent by the end of the Paleolithic.

3

How do historians decide what caused an event?

Show answer

They weigh both short-term triggers (the immediate spark) and long-term conditions (underlying pressures building for years). Good causal analysis names both layers rather than blaming a single cause.

4

Why study the history of groups left out of the official record ("hidden history")?

Show answer

Traditional records were written by and about the powerful, so they leave gaps and biases. Recovering the experiences of excluded groups gives a fuller, more accurate picture and corrects a one-sided story.

5

How did human economic roles change from foraging to specialized trade?

Show answer

Farming produced a food surplus, so not everyone had to grow food. That freed people to become specialists — artisans, priests, merchants, officials — and surplus goods fed trade between regions.

6

What kept some regions from adopting farming?

Show answer

Environment and geography: places lacking domesticable plants and animals, or with climates/terrain unsuited to crops, stayed with hunting-gathering or herding because farming wasn't viable or worthwhile there.

7

How did the availability of domesticable plants and animals differ by continent, and why did it matter?

Show answer

Some regions (like the Fertile Crescent) had many species suited to domestication; others had few. More domesticable species meant an earlier, richer farming base, bigger surpluses, and faster development toward cities and states.

8

Did the Agricultural Revolution improve life? Give both sides.

Show answer

For: more food, population growth, permanent homes, specialization, technology. Against: harder labor, poorer/less varied diet, new diseases from crowding, social inequality, and loss of the egalitarian, mobile Paleolithic life. A real trade-off, not a clear win.

9

How do early settled villages show complex organization without strict social classes?

Show answer

Village societies had permanent settlement, crop cultivation, and shared kinship structures that coordinated real group life — yet still ran on relatively equal, kin-based arrangements rather than the rigid upper/middle/lower classes that came later with cities and surplus wealth.

10

What caused friction between pastoral nomads and settled farmers?

Show answer

Competition over land and resources — grazing rights, water, and farmland. Herders needed open range and moved through farmed areas; farmers needed protected fields. Different land uses put the two ways of life in conflict (though they also traded).

11

How did environments and river systems shape early societies' worldviews?

Show answer

Rivers (Tigris/Euphrates, Nile) set the rhythm of life — flooding, farming, survival — so religion and philosophy grew out of that environment. Egypt's dependable Nile bred a more optimistic outlook; Mesopotamia's unpredictable rivers bred anxious, easily-angered gods.

12

What long-term environmental consequences did intensive irrigation have?

Show answer

Over centuries, heavy irrigation caused salinization (salt building up in the soil until crops fail) and soil exhaustion / erosion. Farming raised food output short-term but degraded the land long-term — an early example of a society damaging its own environment.

13

How did early rulers use religion and law to hold power — and how did law codes reflect inequality?

Show answer

Rulers claimed their authority came from the gods (divine kingship), making obedience a religious duty. Written codes like Hammurabi's then locked inequality into law — punishments and protections differed by social class, so the law itself reinforced who was above whom. As surplus wealth grew, law increasingly existed to protect property and administer the state.
