The review, in four blocks

1 · How historians think

  • Causation: historians analyze both the immediate and the long-term circumstances behind an event. Not all causes are equally important, so they rank them: primary (the spark), secondary, tertiary (the broader context).
  • "Hidden history": the experiences of groups overlooked or ignored in traditional research. Written sources often speak only of elites, so since the 1960s historians have studied history "from the bottom up," using church records, newspapers and court hearings.
  • Studying history builds the critical-thinking & analysis skills employers want.

2 · Human origins & migration

  • Earliest humans emerged in Africa (processed ochre = early evidence of human activity), then migrated worldwide in the Paleolithic. Your slides: Homo sapiens evolved in Africa about 300,000 years ago; migration out of Africa 80,000–60,000 years ago (some argue ~120,000).
  • Why it worked: the last Ice Age (~120,000–12,000 years ago) froze water in glaciers, which lowered sea levels and created land bridges. Eastern Siberia was the point of departure for the Americas; boats reached Australia (first boats ~60,000 BCE in your slides) and the Pacific islands.
  • Paleolithic society: small bands of 25–50, gathering & hunting, highly egalitarian, relatively equal gender relations, seasonal mobility, no permanent leaders, a cyclical view of time, strong ceremonial/religious practices. Your slides call it "the original affluent society": 3–5 hours a day securing food, clothing and shelter.

3 · The Agricultural Revolution

  • ~4,000–12,000 years ago, developed independently in several regions (Fertile Crescent, Northeast Africa, the Americas). It transformed the human–environment relationship: humans began sculpting landscapes and directing plant and animal evolution.
  • Major changes: permanent settlements, increased population density, more food, more complex social structures, specialized skills, and personal possessions.
  • Compare three ways of life:
    • Paleolithic — nomadic hunter-gatherers, minimal social hierarchy.
    • Agricultural village — permanent settlement, crop cultivation, basic social organization, shared kinship structures. Your slides: kinship/lineage systems governed, with only modest inequalities; chiefdoms (inherited power and privilege) came next.
    • Pastoral — animal husbandry and a mobile life, relying on animals where farming was difficult or impossible. Pastoral women milked and made textiles; men and women were relatively equal. They traded with agricultural societies, but also had conflict with farming neighbors (your slides use Cain, "a tiller of the ground," and Abel, "a keeper of the sheep").
  • The environmental cost: in Mesopotamia, intensive irrigation brought deforestation, soil erosion and salinization. Egypt, by contrast, practiced sustainable agriculture working with the natural flow of the Nile.

4 · The first civilizations

  • A civilization = a complex society with advanced cities, specialized workers, complex institutions, record keeping/writing, and advanced technology.
  • How states began: agricultural surplus was necessary but not sufficient; irrigation required coordination. Carneiro's circumscription theory: in bounded environments with dense populations, war forced states into existence; voluntaristic theories say people chose central government through cooperation.
  • Sumer (Mesopotamia, modern-day Iraq, ~3500 BCE): city-states, invented writing (cuneiform). Egypt (along the Nile): unified state under pharaohs, advanced irrigation, hieroglyphic writing. Others: Norte Chico/Chavín (Peru), Indus Valley (India), Shang (China), Olmec (Mexico).
  • Social classes: upper (rulers, priests, nobles, high-ranking officials) · middle (artisans, merchants, farmers, lower-level officials) · lower (enslaved people, prisoners of war, laborers).
  • Gender: patriarchal societies; women's roles varied by class — upper-class women had a restricted home life, lower-class women worked in public.
  • Agriculture was the primary economic foundation: it enabled a food surplus, supported population growth and led to specialized labor. Technology: irrigation, writing, metalworking, advanced building techniques.
  • Power & law: rulers claimed divine authority — Egyptian pharaohs were gods, Zhou emperors ruled by the Mandate of Heaven, Hammurabi claimed the divine right of his laws. Early law codes (Hammurabi) put class differences in writing.

Self-check — answer in your own words first

These are Mr. Angelopulos's 15 Unit 1 review questions (lightly shortened). Say your answer out loud or jot it, THEN reveal to check. Model answers are a guide, not the only right wording.

1

How did environmental changes during the last Ice Age influence global human migration patterns?

Show answer

The last Ice Age (roughly 120,000–12,000 years ago) locked water in northern glaciers, which lowered sea levels and created land bridges. That opened routes on foot — e.g. from Eastern Siberia across the Bering land bridge into the Americas (about 18,000 years ago in your slides). Your slides also give other theories for the Americas: boats along the "kelp highway" (~16,000 years ago) and a North Atlantic crossing.

2

What geographical routes and methods did early humans use to reach oceanic regions and isolated landmasses?

Show answer

Overland via land bridges when seas were low, and boats across open water: migration into Australia came via the first boats (circa 60,000 BCE in your slides); the textbook says they must have used rafts, crossing short distances between islands. Your slides also list later Austronesian migrations by agricultural people. Collective learning helped humans spread across the whole world.

3

How do historians evaluate both short-term triggers and long-term conditions to determine causation?

Show answer

They look at both the immediate circumstances (the spark) and the long-term ones behind an event, then rank the causes, since not all are equally important: the primary cause is the most immediate; the tertiary cause gives the broader context. Historians can disagree about the ranking.

4

Why is it important to study the history of groups traditionally excluded from official written records?

Show answer

Written sources often speak only of elites, so the lives of ordinary people, women and the poor stay hidden. Studying history "from the bottom up" — with church records, newspapers and court hearings — recovers those experiences and gives a fuller picture of the past.

5

How did human economic roles evolve from foraging to specialized trade?

Show answer

Farming produced a food surplus, so not everyone had to grow food. That freed people to become specialists — scholars, officials, merchants, priests and artisans. Even early settlements traded: obsidian from Çatalhöyük reached as far as Syria and Cyprus, and cities became centers of trade and manufacturing.

6

What environmental or geographic constraints prevented certain regions from adopting farming?

Show answer

Your slides: people kept gathering and hunting in areas unsuitable for farming and in regions of natural abundance, and turned to herding where farming was difficult or impossible.

7

How did the availability of domesticable plants and animals vary across continents, and how did that impact development?

Show answer

The Fertile Crescent, with abundant rainfall and many plants and animals capable of domestication, was the first to have a full agricultural revolution. Eurasia's east–west axis let farming spread faster, while the north–south orientation of the Americas slowed it. The Americas lacked cereal grain (maize was domesticated in southern Mexico by 4,000 BCE), and the Secondary Products Revolution (milk, wool, plows, transport) was only available in the Eastern Hemisphere.

8

What are the main arguments for and against the idea that the Agricultural Revolution improved quality of life?

Show answer

For (Howells): farming broke "one of nature's bonds," freeing people from the limits of the natural food supply; camps became villages, and people could escape constant moving and food-getting to specialize. Your slides add: a dependable food source proved decisive. Against (Diamond, "The Worst Mistake"): one group of Bushmen spent only 12–19 hours a week getting food (the Hadza, 14 hours or less), and foragers ate a better diet; at Dickson Mounds, farmers had about 50% more malnutrition and four times the anemia; one failed crop meant starvation; crowding spread disease; and farming brought class divisions and inequality between the sexes — "an elite became better off, but most people became worse off." Your slides' "Second Thoughts": less leisure, disease and drought, inequality, animal cruelty.

9

How do early settled villages show that complex social organization can exist without strict social classes?

Show answer

Agricultural villages had permanent settlement, crop cultivation, basic social organization and shared kinship structures; your slides say kinship/lineage systems governed them, with only modest social and economic inequalities. Chiefdoms with inherited power came later. Göbekli Tepe, a huge temple built by pre-agricultural people, also shows organized group work before farming.

10

What factors driven by resources and land use caused friction between pastoral nomads and settled farmers?

Show answer

Pastoral peoples relied on animals in regions where farming was difficult or impossible and moved with their herds, while farmers stayed on their land — your slides list "conflict with farming neighbors" as a feature of pastoral life. The two also traded, so the relationship was both exchange and conflict. (The resources at stake beyond that are for your class notes.)

11

How do natural environments and river systems influence the cultural, religious and philosophical worldviews of early societies?

Show answer

Mesopotamia: unpredictably flooding rivers, unprotected land and few resources → capricious gods and a hopeless afterlife (the textbook: fickle, destructive gods easily stirred to anger; a pessimistic worldview). Egypt: a predictably flooding Nile and natural barriers → a life-affirming worldview and an eternal afterlife, and, reflecting the environment, a worldview of recurrent cycles and renewal.

12

What long-term environmental consequences did early agricultural practices, such as intensive irrigation, have on local ecosystems?

Show answer

In Mesopotamia, intensive irrigation caused deforestation, soil erosion and salinization. Egypt's agriculture, working with the natural flow of the Nile, was sustainable. Your slides also note foragers probably killed off large animals in places like the Americas and Australia.

13

How did early rulers use religious beliefs and divine authority to justify their political power?

Show answer

States were presented as normal, natural and ordained by the gods. Kings (likely military leaders who kept power into peacetime) sought religious authority: Egyptian pharaohs were gods, Zhou emperors ruled by the Mandate of Heaven, and Hammurabi claimed the gods Anu and Bel "called by name me, Hammurabi." Temples, monuments and lavish rituals ("theater states") convinced subjects of that power.

14

In what ways did early written legal codes reflect and reinforce social and economic inequalities?

Show answer

Hammurabi's Code set penalties by rank: put out a man's eye and lose your own (196), but put out a slave's eye and pay half his value (199); strike someone higher in rank and get sixty lashes in public (202), strike an equal and pay one gold mina (203). Men and women were not equal under the Code either. Writing it down fixed these class differences in law.

15

How did the accumulation of wealth and surplus property change the purpose of laws and state administration?

Show answer

Surplus let some people accumulate property and land, strengthening social divisions. Royalty came to take care of defenses, irrigation, property rights and justice. Before Hammurabi's Code, individuals decided their own revenge; the Code had the government set punishments, partly to unify a growing empire — and many of its laws protect property (a careless dam that floods a neighbor's corn, 53–55) and regulate debt (117).
