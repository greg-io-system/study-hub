Is Google Making Us Stupid?

An annotation guide for the first 18 paragraphs of Nicholas Carr's essay. This shows you what to look for and how to note it — the finding and the writing are yours.

The one idea: annotation is not highlighting. A real annotation is a short written note that names a CHOICE the writer made and its PURPOSE. Mr. Evans is asking you to read the rhetorical situation first, then track the appeals — and to have those notes ready before the reading so they become a lens, not an afterthought. Reply to his email with a photo or link of the notes.

Plan first (his opener). A line of reasoning is not a stream of consciousness. Before you write anything up, take 4–5 minutes to outline where your thinking is going. Structure your logic, then write.

Know this word cold before you start. Mr. Evans says you MUST know the denotation of "implication."
Implication = a meaning a text suggests or entails without stating it outright. (Verb: to imply = to hint at / lead to as a consequence.) Watch it: an implication is what the writer makes you conclude, not what they literally say.

Step 1 · Read the rhetorical situation

Before appeals, name the three points of the wheel. Every choice Carr makes serves one of these:

Speaker
Who is talking, and why should we trust them? Their credibility, stance, and stake in the topic. (This is where ethos lives.)
Audience
Who is being addressed — often more than one group. Their values and worries explain the writer's moves. (This is where pathos aims.)
Message
The claim and its exigence — what's being argued and why now. (This is what logos supports.)

Naming the situation isn't busywork — it's what makes the appeals legible. You can't say what an appeal is doing until you know who is speaking to whom, about what.

Step 2 · The appeals — "appeal TO," not "use"

The three appeals are audience sensitivities a writer activates. The phrasing matters on the rubric:

Don't write
Carr uses ethos to…
Write
Carr appeals to ethos by… in order to…

Every appeal note is one sentence with an "in order to" clause — the choice, then its purpose and effect:

Carr appeals to [ethos / pathos / logos] by [the specific choice, par. __]
in order to [what it does to the reader].

Ethos — the credibility appeal (look hard here)

Ethos = trust in the speaker.
Not just "is he an expert?" — it's every move that makes you willing to listen. Four to watch for:
  • Credentials / knowledge — showing he understands the history and the science.
  • Candor — admitting the problem is happening to him too. Confessing a weakness is disarming: he's not lecturing from above, he's in it with you. (A surprisingly strong ethos move.)
  • Borrowed testimony — quoting other credible people who report the same thing, so it's not just one man's hunch.
  • Fairness — acknowledging the other side (the upside of the Net) before pressing his worry, so he reads as balanced, not alarmist.

Pathos — the emotional appeal

Pathos = the feeling stirred in the audience.
Pathos is not "sad words." It's any choice that makes the reader feel the stakes. Watch for:
  • Charged imagery — a picture that carries dread, loss, or unease before any argument is made.
  • Nostalgia — mourning a way of reading/thinking that's slipping away, so you feel you're losing something.
  • Shared fear — naming a worry the audience already half-feels ("this is happening to me"), which makes them lean in.

Logos (the logical case — his evidence, historical parallels, cause-and-effect) is the third appeal; note it too, but Mr. Evans flagged the appeals as a set, and ethos + pathos are where Carr's opening pages do their quiet work.

Step 3 · Worked model (on a made-up twin text, so Carr stays yours)

Made-up parallel text Dr. Lena Ortiz, a neurologist who has studied attention for twenty years, opens her essay by confessing that she — the expert — can no longer sink into a long novel the way she once could; she reaches for her phone at the first dull paragraph. She adds that a former student, now a surgeon, tells her he "reads in fragments" between operations. Then she describes a boy in a waiting room, thumbs moving, eyes never lifting — "a small ghost of the reader I was at his age."

Not Carr — a stand-in so you can see the moves annotated, then find the real ones yourself.

Rhetorical situation

Speaker: a neurologist, 20 years on attention — credentialed. Audience: thoughtful magazine readers who already sense their own focus fraying. Message: our capacity for deep attention is eroding, and it matters now.

Ethos — annotated

• "Appeals to ethos by naming her twenty years studying attention (par. 1) in order to earn the right to diagnose the problem."
• "Appeals to ethos by confessing that she can't read deeply either in order to lower the reader's guard — she shares the flaw, so she isn't scolding."
• "Appeals to ethos by quoting the surgeon in order to show it isn't just her — a credible outside voice reports the same thing."

Pathos — annotated

• "Appeals to pathos by the image of the boy as 'a small ghost of the reader I was' (par. 1) in order to make the loss feel personal and a little frightening — we grieve a reader disappearing."
• "Appeals to pathos by the detail 'eyes never lifting' in order to plant unease before she has argued anything."

See the shape every time: appeals to ___ by ___ (par. __) in order to ___. That single sentence IS the annotation.

Step 4 · Your turn — on Carr's ¶1–18

Now find the real moves. Read for these questions and write your own "in order to" notes. Tap a question for a method hint (what kind of appeal to look for and why) — not the answer. The annotation is yours to write.

How does Carr open, before he argues anything? What does it make you feel?
Pathos · imagery
He starts with a scene from a famous film about a thinking machine whose mind is being switched off. Ask: what emotion does that plant — dread? unease? — and why put it first, before the case? Write the "in order to." (Finding the passage and naming the effect is your job.)
Where does Carr admit the problem is happening to him?
Ethos · candor
Early on he confesses his own reading has changed — his focus drifts after a page or two. This is the same move as Dr. Ortiz's confession. Ask: how does owning the flaw build your trust in him? That's an ethos note, not a summary.
Whose other voices does he bring in, and what do they do?
Ethos · borrowed testimony
He reports other people — writers and a specialist — saying their reading has changed too. Ask: why does outside testimony strengthen him more than his own experience alone?
Where does he make you feel something is being lost?
Pathos · nostalgia
Watch for the mourning of an older, deeper way of reading. Ask: what does the sense of loss push the reader to want — and is that feeling doing work his evidence isn't?
Does he ever grant the other side? What does that do for him?
Ethos · fairness
He acknowledges the Net's benefits. Ask: how does conceding the upside make him read as balanced rather than a scold — and how does that protect his argument?

Remember the rubric words: your notes must address the rhetorical situation and the appeals, and know the implication of each choice — what it leads the reader to conclude without saying it. And bring your own experience: as someone raised inside digital reading, does Carr's worry match your life or not? That's fair material.

Before you reply — the check

  • Did I name the rhetorical situation — speaker, audience, message?
  • Are my appeal notes written as "appeals to ___ by ___ in order to ___" (not "uses ___")?
  • Did I mark at least one ethos and one pathos move, with the paragraph number?
  • Does each note name a choice AND its purpose — not just underline a line?
  • Can I state the implication of a choice — what it makes the reader conclude?
  • Did I connect at least one other source or my own experience?
