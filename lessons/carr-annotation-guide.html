Step 2 · The appeals — "appeal TO," not "use"

From his 9/15 class email: audiences "are sensitive to ones that are made by a credible writer (ethos)," "to arguments that create emotional responses (pathos)," and "to arguments that are logically sound (logos)." The appeals "are not tools to be used; instead, they are end goals for a writer or speaker. In other words, a writer does not USE Ethos."

What NOT to do (his 9/15 example)
"...uses ethos in order to___________"
What TO DO
"...appeals to ethos by ___________"

Try it — a study-aid sentence shape joining his "appeals to ... by" with the "in order to" clause from his basic model:

[Writer] appeals to [ethos / pathos / logos] by [the specific choice, par. __]
in order to [what it does to the reader].

Step 3 · Made-up example (a twin text, so Carr stays yours)

Made-up example · invented text Marta Bell, a baker who has kneaded dough at four in the morning for thirty-one years, opens her letter to the town paper: "I have fed this street every morning since before most of you were born." She describes the bakery on Elm Street after it closed, "flour dust still on the glass." Then she points out that the shop next door lost its morning customers the same month.

Everything in the box is invented: the baker, the town and the shops. It is a stand-in so you can see appeal notes written out, then find the real moves in Carr yourself.

Made-up example · rhetorical situation

Speaker: a baker of thirty-one years. Audience: her neighbors, readers of the town paper. Message: the town should keep its small bakeries open.

Made-up example · appeal notes

• "Bell appeals to ethos by naming her thirty-one years of four-a.m. baking (par. 1) in order to show she has earned the right to speak for the street."
• "Bell appeals to pathos by the image of 'flour dust still on the glass' in order to make the closed shop feel recent and personal."
• "Bell appeals to logos by linking the closing to the neighbor's lost customers in order to turn one closed shop into a cost the whole street pays."

See the shape every time: appeals to ___ by ___ (par. __) in order to ___.

Step 4 · Your turn — Carr, paragraphs 1–18

Read with these questions and write your own notes. The questions point at what to ask, not at where the answers are.

  • Speaker: who is talking, and what in the first 18 paragraphs makes you trust him or not?
  • Audience: who is he writing for? His 9/21 email calls this "one of those dividing essays": readers raised with these new forms of reading (YOU) and those who were not. Where do you sit?
  • Message: what is being argued?
  • Ethos: which choices could make a reader see him as credible?
  • Pathos: where does the writing try to create an emotional response, and what emotion?
  • Logos: where is the argument trying to be logically sound, and with what kind of evidence?
  • Implications: if what he says is true, what follows? Try his frame: "If ___ in 2008, then ___ in 2026."
  • Your experience: his email says "your own experience with the ideas within the essay are relevant here and should be included in your notes." Does the essay match your life?
  • Connections: "Keep in mind the connections you can make between other sources and this one. Dust off the library." Which earlier readings talk to this one?
