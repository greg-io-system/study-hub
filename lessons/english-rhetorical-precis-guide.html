Rhetorical Précis — the 4-sentence formula

For the smartphone article (the Google Doc from your teacher). A précis is EXACTLY four sentences, and each one has a fixed job. Learn the jobs, not just the example — this format comes back all year.

The four sentences (the decoder)

Sentence 1 — WHAT the author claims

Name the author (+ a credential if known), the genre and title, the year, then a rhetorically accurate verb + a "that" clause carrying the main claim.

Frame: [Author], [credential], in her [genre] "[Title]" ([year]), [verb] that [the main claim in your words].
  • Verb bank (pick what the author actually does): argues, contends, asserts, claims, warns, suggests, demonstrates. NOT "writes," "talks about," or "says" — those describe typing, not persuading.
  • The claim is ONE idea. If your "that" clause has an "and also," it's two ideas — cut to the one the whole piece defends.

Sentence 2 — HOW the author builds it

Describe the support strategy, in the order it appears.

Frame: She supports this claim by [move 1], [move 2], and [move 3].
  • Moves are things like: opening with a personal anecdote, citing national survey data, comparing generations, quoting interviews, conceding a counterargument.
  • Name the kinds of evidence — not a plot summary of the article.

Sentence 3 — WHY the author wrote it

State the purpose with an "in order to" clause.

Frame: The author's purpose is to [do what] in order to [change what in the reader — believe, worry, act, reconsider].
  • Purpose ≠ topic. "To discuss smartphones" fails — what does the author want to happen because you read it?

Sentence 4 — WHO it's for (and in what voice)

Identify the intended audience and the author's tone.

Frame: She writes in a [tone] tone for [specific audience — who is positioned to act on the purpose?].
  • "General audience" is the weak answer. Ask: whose behavior could this piece actually change? Who reads this magazine?

Worked example — on a different article

So the moves are visible without doing your assignment for you: Nicholas Carr's "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" (The Atlantic, 2008) — same magazine, same genre, neighboring topic.

(1) Technology writer Nicholas Carr, in his essay "Is Google Making Us Stupid?" (2008), contends that constant internet use is reshaping how people think, trading deep, sustained reading for shallow skimming. (2) He supports this claim by opening with his own struggle to stay focused on long books, adding similar reports from literary friends and bloggers, and then widening out to research on online reading habits and historical examples of technologies — the typewriter, the clock — that changed human thought. (3) His purpose is to make readers examine what efficient skimming is costing them in order to persuade them to protect deep reading before the habit is lost. (4) He writes in a reflective but alarmed tone for educated readers who live online and are beginning to suspect it is changing them.

Notice: sentence 1 is one claim; sentence 2 names three kinds of support in order; sentence 3 has the "in order to"; sentence 4 names a specific audience, not "everyone."

Now yours — finding each piece in the smartphone article

Don't copy the example's sentences — run the same four jobs on your article:

  1. Claim: The author studies generations for a living and says this one is different. What is her single central claim about smartphones and this generation? (Hint: it lands at the end of her opening section — the last sentence before the roller-rink story. "Phones exist" is not a claim; what does she say phones are doing?)
  2. Support: She uses at least three distinct kinds of evidence. One is a named teenager. What are the other kinds, and in what order do they appear? Where does she concede something? (Look for the paragraphs admitting what the data can't prove.)
  3. Purpose: She's a researcher writing in a magazine, not a journal, and she ends with advice. What does she want readers to DO? Finish the sentence: "...in order to ______."
  4. Audience: Teens don't read The Atlantic. Who does — and who has the power to act on her closing advice? That's your audience, and it explains why her tone stays measured instead of preachy.

Checks before you're done

  • Exactly four sentences.
  • Author + title + year + genre all in sentence 1.
  • A strong verb + "that" clause (one claim, not two).
  • Sentence 2 names kinds of evidence, not a summary of the story.
  • "In order to" appears in sentence 3.
  • Sentence 4 names a specific audience + a tone word you could defend with a line from the text.
