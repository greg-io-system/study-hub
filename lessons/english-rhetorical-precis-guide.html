The four sentences (from his Quick Guide)

A précis is a highly structured four-sentence paragraph that blends summary and analysis. His guide also expects brief quotations (to show the author's style and tone) and a citation at the end.

Sentence 1 — Who said it? What did they say?

Name of author, an optional phrase describing the author, the genre and title, the publication date in parentheses, then a rhetorically accurate verb and a THAT clause holding the work's major assertion (its thesis), paraphrased or quoted.

  • Verbs his guide names here: assert, argue, suggest, imply, claim. His Verb Bank has many more (advises, laments, proposes, recommends, reveals ...), and he says to strive for "the most connotatively precise words you can."
  • His 9/8 email, under Sentence 1, lists how to name the writer: "Jean Twenge," then "Twenge (NEVER first person)," then "She." If you're unsure what he meant by that, check your notes from class 9/8.

Sentence 2 — How did they say it?

Explain how the author develops and/or supports the thesis, usually in chronological order. His guide: "the trick is to convey a good sense of the breadth of the author's support/ examples."

  • His 9/8 question for this sentence: "What choices were made IN THE WRITING?"

Sentence 3 — Why are they saying it?

State the author's purpose, followed by an "in order to" phrase and explanation.

  • His 9/8 question: "What was the writer's aim?"

Sentence 4 — Who are they saying it to?

Describe the intended audience and/or the relationship the author sets up with that audience (or the essay's tone).

  • His 9/8 frame for this sentence: "Ostensibly______ ; however,______". His guide's example makes the same move.

Try it: the four jobs as fill-in frames

Study aid: our fill-in-the-blank version of his four sentence descriptions. The brackets are yours to fill.

1.  [Full name], [optional phrase describing the author], in [genre] "[Title]" ([date]), [precise verb]s that [the thesis, paraphrased or quoted].

2.  [Last name] [develops/supports] this by [‑ing phrase], by [‑ing phrase], and by [‑ing phrase] — usually in the order they appear.

3.  [He/She] [does what] in order to [the aim].

4.  [He/She] ostensibly addresses [one audience]; however, [the other audience or relationship, and the tone].

Then the citation (his guide's example cites the Barry essay in full after the paragraph).

Made-up example — a twin text, not your article

Made-up example. The author, essay, magazine and quotes below are invented for practice; none of it is a real fact. It shows the four jobs on a different topic so your Twenge précis stays yours.

The invented essay: Rosa Delgado's "Let the Kids Burn the Toast" (2024), from the made-up magazine Kitchen Table Weekly. She remembers learning to cook in her grandmother's kitchen, compares a boxed dinner with a meal a ten-year-old made herself, jokes about her own kitchen disasters, and ends with a list of easy first recipes for parents to hand over.

(1) Food columnist Rosa Delgado, in her essay "Let the Kids Burn the Toast" (2024), asserts that children who cook their own meals "learn more at the stove than at the screen." (2) Delgado supports this by recalling her grandmother's crowded kitchen, by contrasting a boxed dinner with a meal a ten-year-old made herself, and by poking fun at her own "smoke-alarm suppers." (3) She retells her failures cheerfully in order to persuade parents that a ruined dinner is a fair price for a capable child. (4) Delgado ostensibly writes for parents, offering them a starter list of recipes; however, her warm, teasing tone invites the kids themselves to take over the kitchen.

Delgado, Rosa. "Let the Kids Burn the Toast." Kitchen Table Weekly, 2024.

Notice: a precise verb + "that"; support in order; "in order to" in sentence 3; "ostensibly ... however" in sentence 4; brief quotations; a citation at the end.

Now yours — questions to ask the smartphone article

His 9/8 email calls the précis "a frame to read non-fiction through." Run his five questions on your own annotated copy:

  1. Who said it? What does the article itself tell you about the author?
  2. What did they say? Which one sentence of yours could carry her major assertion?
  3. How did they say it? What choices did she make in the writing, and in what order?
  4. Why are they saying it? What is her aim? Finish: "...in order to ______."
  5. Who are they saying it to? Who is she ostensibly writing for, and is there a "however"?

Checks before you're done

  • Exactly four sentences.
  • Sentence 1: author, genre and title, date in parentheses, a precise verb + "that" + the thesis.
  • Sentence 2: how she supports it, usually in order.
  • "In order to" appears in sentence 3.
  • Sentence 4: the audience and/or her relationship with it (or tone).
  • Brief quotations, and a citation at the end.
