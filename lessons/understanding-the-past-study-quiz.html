Study guide

Use this to review before the quiz. It's built around what the chapter actually wants you to be able to do, not just recall.

What you should be able to do

  • Explain why studying history builds real-world skills (critical thinking and analysis -- among the top skills employers want -- plus communication and cultural empathy).
  • Define a global citizen and connect it to the human-rights declarations the United Nations began issuing in 1948.
  • Tell a primary source from a secondary source, with examples.
  • Interpret a source using author, audience, intent, context.
  • Explain causation and rank primary / secondary / tertiary causes.
  • Describe how historical interpretation shifted from "great man" history to social, intellectual, and revisionist approaches.

Core concepts, in one line each

  • Causation: the why behind events -- how historians get at the heart of the matter. Names and dates are only history's building blocks.
  • Primary source: from the time period (letter, statue, diary).
  • Secondary source: made afterward (a biography, a 1960s map of WWII battle sites, a museum curator's blog post).
  • Historiography: the study of how historians have interpreted the past.
  • The 4 source questions: author · audience · intent · context.
  • Levels of causation: primary (the spark) → secondary (once removed) → tertiary (broad context).
  • Great man theory → social history: from "study elites" to "everyone makes history."
  • Revisionism: the story keeps getting revised as new lenses are added.
  • Hidden history: groups left out of the record because few sources talk about them. Since the 1960s historians study history "from the bottom up," using church records, newspapers and court hearings to recover ordinary people's lives.
  • Historical empathy: meeting the past on its own terms, without judging it by modern attitudes. Bias can even help, by letting us see the past in new ways -- the danger is using only one lens.

Anchor examples to memorize (they show up on tests)

  • Hagia Sophia — one building, a thousand-year story: Justinian's Christian basilica (6th c.) → Ottoman mosque after 1453 (Mehmed II). Point: context gives a source its meaning.
  • Cortés vs. the Aztec account — same event (Spanish in Tenochtitlán), opposite perspectives. Point: weigh competing primary sources.
  • U.S. enters WWII (1941) — the immediate cause = Pearl Harbor; but hostilities had been brewing before it (Roosevelt looking for ways to help the British against a possible German invasion; U.S.–Japan Pacific tensions). Point: an event has layers of causes.
  • Herodotus and Sima Qian — the "father of history" in the West (he wrote the history of the Greco-Persian wars) and in China (Records of the Grand Historian, on the Han dynasty).
  • Carlyle vs. Tolstoy — "Great Men" drive history vs. the "general mass of men" do. Point: interpretation is a choice historians argue about.

More terms the chapter defines

  • Iconography: the visual images and symbols used in a work of art (e.g. halos on the Hagia Sophia figures signal holiness).
  • Rhetoric: the use of language -- e.g. Roosevelt's "Day of Infamy" speech.
  • Writing: history technically begins with the advent of writing.
  • Social constructs: ideas created and accepted by a society, such as class distinction and gender.
  • Teleological history: history moving to a particular end.
  • Intellectual history: the ideas that drive people's choices; the history of human thought.
  • Other lenses: Marxism (history driven by class struggle), gender history (power differences between men and women), postcolonialism (places formerly occupied by colonial powers).
  • The textbook's feature boxes: In Their Own Words, Dueling Voices, Beyond the Book, The Past Meets the Present.

Common traps

  • Don't equate "primary source" with "trustworthy" — primary sources still have bias, intent, and missing perspectives.
  • Primary cause = the most immediate one (the spark); tertiary = the broader context. Historians also rank causes by importance, and they can disagree about the ranking.
  • "Progressive history" is a specific school (inevitable Western-style progress), not just "history that's forward-looking."

Explain why a primary source being "from the time period" does not automatically make it unbiased. Use one of the four source questions.

Show answer

Full credit: a primary source still has an author with an agenda, an intended audience, and an intent (to persuade, flatter, deceive), and it may omit other voices. Example: Cortés's letter is primary but was written to impress Charles V, so it's slanted. (1 pt for naming a source question, 1 pt for applying it.)

Part D — Source analysis (5 pts)

20

You're handed two accounts of the same battle: a victorious general's official report to his king, and a captured enemy soldier's diary. Walk through author, audience, intent, and context for each, and explain how you'd use both to get closer to what actually happened.

Show answer

Part D — Source analysis (5 pts) — rubric

Award up to 5:

  • +2 applies author/audience/intent/context to the general's report (author = victor with a stake; audience = his king; intent = to look successful / secure reward; context = wartime, written to impress -- the chapter warns that people often write things that present them in the best light).
  • +2 applies the same four to the soldier's diary (author = defeated participant; audience = was it meant to be public or private?; intent = why did he write it?; context = one participant's narrow view, from the losing side).
  • +1 for the synthesis: neither is the whole truth; corroborate the two, cross-check with other sources, and use the points where independent accounts agree to get closer to what happened.

Strong answers use the chapter's own pair as a model: Cortés's letter and the Aztec account -- one author among the victorious, one among the vanquished.
