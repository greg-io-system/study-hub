Study guide

Use this to review before the quiz. It's built around what the chapter actually wants you to be able to do, not just recall.

What you should be able to do

  • Explain why studying history builds real-world skills (critical thinking, analysis, communication, cultural empathy).
  • Define a global citizen and connect it to the post-1945 human-rights era.
  • Tell a primary source from a secondary source, with examples.
  • Interpret a source using author, audience, intent, context.
  • Explain causation and rank primary / secondary / tertiary causes.
  • Describe how historical interpretation shifted from "great man" history to social, intellectual, and revisionist approaches.

Core concepts, in one line each

  • History's real job: explain why, not just what/when.
  • Primary source: from the time period (letter, statue, diary).
  • Secondary source: made afterward (biography, textbook, later map).
  • Historiography: the study of how historians have interpreted the past.
  • The 4 source questions: author · audience · intent · context.
  • Levels of causation: primary (the spark) → secondary (once removed) → tertiary (broad context).
  • Great man theory → social history: from "study elites" to "everyone makes history."
  • Revisionism: the story keeps getting revised as new lenses are added.

Anchor examples to memorize (they show up on tests)

  • Hagia Sophia — one building, a thousand-year story: Justinian's Christian basilica (6th c.) → Ottoman mosque after 1453 (Mehmed II). Point: context gives a source its meaning.
  • Cortés vs. the Aztec account — same event (Spanish in Tenochtitlán), opposite perspectives. Point: weigh competing primary sources.
  • U.S. enters WWII (1941) — spark = Pearl Harbor; deeper causes = aid to Britain, U.S.–Japan Pacific tensions. Point: rank causes.
  • Carlyle vs. Tolstoy — "Great Men" drive history vs. the "general mass of men" do. Point: interpretation is a choice historians argue about.

Compare & contrast (know the difference)

PairKey difference
Primary vs. secondary sourceDuring the period vs. after it
Primary vs. tertiary causeImmediate spark vs. broad background
Great man vs. social historyElites only vs. all people

Quick self-check (say the answer out loud)

  1. Give one primary and one secondary source for studying Ancient Egypt.
  2. Why are maps considered biased historical evidence?
  3. Name the four questions you ask when interpreting a source.
  4. For "Why did Mehmed II take Constantinople?", name a primary and a tertiary cause.
  5. What did the 1960s counterculture change about how history was written?

Common traps

  • Don't equate "primary source" with "trustworthy" — primary sources still have bias, intent, and missing perspectives.
  • The primary cause is the most immediate one, not the most important background factor — don't mix up "primary" (spark) with "biggest."
  • "Progressive history" is a specific school (inevitable Western-style progress), not just "history that's forward-looking."

Give one primary source and one secondary source you could use to study World War II. Explain what makes each one primary or secondary.

Part C — Short answer (look for the reasoning, not exact wording)

Full credit: any correct pairing with justification. Primary = from the time (a soldier's 1943 letter, a Roosevelt speech recording, a wartime photograph). Secondary = made afterward (a 2000 biography, this textbook, a later documentary). 1 pt for the pair, 1 pt for correct reasoning (during vs. after the period).

17

Explain why a primary source being "from the time period" does not automatically make it unbiased. Use one of the four source questions.

Show answer

Full credit: a primary source still has an author with an agenda, an intended audience, and an intent (to persuade, flatter, deceive), and it may omit other voices. Example: Cortés's letter is primary but was written to impress Charles V, so it's slanted. (1 pt for naming a source question, 1 pt for applying it.)

18

Using the Hagia Sophia, explain the sentence: "Context gives a source its meaning."

Show answer

Full credit: the same building means different things once you know the context — a 6th-century Christian basilica under Justinian, then an Ottoman mosque after 1453 under Mehmed II. The physical object alone (dome, minarets, Greek + Arabic decoration) is confusing until context explains the layered history. (1 pt building/story, 1 pt the "context = meaning" point.)

19

For the question "Why did the United States enter World War II in 1941?", identify a primary cause and a tertiary (background) cause.

Show answer

Full credit: Primary (immediate) cause = the attack on Pearl Harbor. Tertiary/background cause = long-standing U.S.–Japan tension over the Pacific or Roosevelt's ongoing effort to help Britain against Germany. (1 pt each, correctly leveled.)

Part D — Source analysis (5 pts)

20

You're handed two accounts of the same battle: a victorious general's official report to his king, and a captured enemy soldier's diary. Walk through author, audience, intent, and context for each, and explain how you'd use both to get closer to what actually happened.

Show answer

Part D — Source analysis (5 pts) — rubric

Award up to 5:

  • +2 applies author/audience/intent/context to the general's report (author = victor with a stake; audience = his king; intent = to look successful / secure reward; context = wartime, written to impress → likely exaggerates success, downplays losses).
  • +2 applies the same four to the soldier's diary (author = defeated participant; audience = private/self; intent = personal record, less reason to spin; context = captivity/stress may color it, and it's one narrow view).
  • +1 for the synthesis: neither is the whole truth; corroborate the two, cross-check with other sources, and use the points where independent accounts agree to get closer to what happened.

Strong answers note the diary's private audience often makes it more candid, while the report's public audience makes it more polished/persuasive — but both still need corroboration.
