"The Loser Edit" — a method guide

Not what to write — how to write it. The whole assignment is one move done four times: have a view, don't summarize.

New to how he sets up homework? See "How Mr. Evans's assignments work" on the AP English card — the same five-move pattern is behind every one of his emails.

The one rule underneath everything Mr. Evans asks for: a summary reports what a writer said; a view takes a position and puts the writers in conversation with each other — and with you. Every paragraph in this assignment (C2, C3, C4) is graded on whether your thinking is in it, not on whether you retold the reading correctly. If a sentence could sit in a summary anyone could write, it isn't done yet.

1 · Summary vs. a view (the test to run on every paragraph)

Before you hand anything in, read each paragraph and ask: am I reporting, or am I claiming? Here's the difference, using a made-up example so your real ones stay yours:

Summary (not enough)
Whitehead says people get a "loser edit," and Twenge says smartphones changed teenagers. Both talk about how people look online.
A view (what he wants)
Whitehead's "loser edit" is really the same act Twenge is measuring: teenagers editing their own footage in real time. But where Twenge counts the damage, Whitehead makes it feel chosen — which raises a question she doesn't: what if the editing is the point, not the accident?

See the difference? The second one connects the two writers and stakes a position (the last sentence is the writer's own). That's the bar.

2 · What you actually need to understand first

You can't do any of this until you can say, in one sentence, what a "loser edit" is. Read Whitehead first and get this before you write a word:

  • On reality shows, once editors know who's losing, they cut that person's footage to make the loss look inevitable — the ominous music, the "telling" glances, the foreshadowing. That's the loser edit.
  • Whitehead's leap: we all get one — culture does it to people who fall, and (paragraph 16) we do it to ourselves online, endlessly tweaking our posts and personas.
  • That paragraph 16 idea — constant self-editing — is your bridge to Twenge. Hold onto it.

If you can't explain the loser edit to a friend without the essay in front of you, re-read it. Everything below fails without this.

3 · The synthesis move (C2 & C3): put sources in conversation

C2 asks you to take one specific quote (par. 16) and apply it to several specific ideas in another text — not to summarize both. Here's the shape, modeled on a made-up pair so you can copy the method, not the content:

Made-up "given" essay
"The Highlight Reel," Dana Ruiz (2019): "We have all become our own publicists. Every photo is a small campaign; every caption, a press release. We quietly delete the ordinary Tuesday." (par. 3)
Made-up second source
"The Comparison Trap," Dr. Ellis (2020): argues that teens who scroll curated feeds report higher anxiety and feel their real lives are the lesser, unedited version.
Worked C2 (on the made-up pair)

Ruiz's line that "every photo is a small campaign" (par. 3) names the exact behavior Ellis only measures from the outside: the teenagers in Ellis's study aren't just looking at feeds, they are running campaigns of their own, deleting every ordinary Tuesday before anyone can see it. Ellis calls the result anxiety; Ruiz suggests why — you can't run a campaign and relax at the same time. What I notice is that both leave out consent: no one signed up to be their own publicist, and that’s the part I’d push on.

Notice: a specific quote → specific ideas in the other text → and a last sentence that is clearly mine. That last sentence is the "view."

Now you (on your real texts)

Do the same with Whitehead's par. 16 and "Have Smartphones Destroyed a Generation?" (Twenge). Guiding questions — answers are yours to write:

  • Which specific Twenge ideas is par. 16 describing from the inside? (Find two or three, not one.)
  • Where do Whitehead and Twenge agree, and where does Whitehead add something Twenge doesn't?
  • What do YOU think about it? End the paragraph on your sentence, not theirs.

C3 is the same move with a third reading you choose from class — include title, author, date.

4 · The rhetorical-analysis move (C4): the formula

C4 is different: here you analyze how Whitehead writes, not what he argues. Mr. Evans gave you the formula — and you cannot do it until you can say what his purpose is (analysis of a strategy is analysis of a strategy for something).

[author] in the [date] essay "[title]" uses [device] in paragraph [#] in order to [intent + use + effect].

Worked C4 (on the made-up essay)

Ruiz, in the 2019 essay "The Highlight Reel," uses the extended metaphor of a publicist (par. 3) in order to reframe an ordinary habit as unpaid, anxious labor — which makes the reader feel faintly absurd for doing it, and that discomfort is the point.

Then he asks you to add a term you're still learning and try it out — application is how you learn a term. One familiar, one new.

For Whitehead: what's his purpose in one sentence? Then name a device you know (irony? second person? anaphora? the extended metaphor of the "edit" itself?) and one you're learning — and run each through the formula with its effect. Look at his sentence structure and even punctuation; that counts as strategy.

This is Exam Question 2, by the way — the "in order to" clause is the skill being tested.

5 · Before you reply to his email — the check

  • Can I say what a loser edit is in one sentence, without the essay open?
  • C2: did I connect one par.16 quote to two or three specific Twenge ideas (not summarize both)?
  • C3: third reading connected, with title, author, date correct?
  • C4: author + date + title + device + paragraph + "in order to" + effect — and do I know his purpose?
  • The view test: in each paragraph, can I point to a sentence that is clearly mine, not a summary anyone could write?
  • Did I actually use the They Say / I Say templates and the "appeals to" (ethos/pathos/logos) language? (That's what section B is nagging about.)
