"The Loser Edit" — a method guide

Not what to write — how to write it. His question for this assignment (9/14): "Where are you finding space for YOUR voice and YOUR position within this style of writing?"

The assignment in one line (his 9/14 email): it "will work into the area of synthesis. You will read an essay and then you will be guided to specific sections of the essay to reach out and into two other readings you have studied (one given, one chosen)."

His 9/15 class email names the line you are trying to cross: "Summary is a recap of what the writer or speaker said. Analysis is an investigation of what it means." And his check: "Was it about the sources, or was it about your thinking while using the sources to support your thoughts?"

1 · Summary vs. your thinking (a test to run on every paragraph)

Before you hand anything in, read each paragraph and ask: am I recapping, or am I investigating what it means? Here's the difference on a made-up pair of essays, so your real ones stay yours:

Made-up example · two invented essays
"The Participation Shelf," Dana Ruiz (invented): "Every trophy we hand out is another bill printed by a bank that never closes. The shelf fills; the money loses its value." (par. 3)
"The Practice Room," J. Ellis (invented): describes a music teacher who praised every attempt and watched her students stop choosing the hard pieces.
Recap (not there yet)
Ruiz says kids get too many trophies, and Ellis says a teacher praised her students a lot. Both are about praise.
Investigating what it means
Ruiz's "bank that never closes" explains what Ellis only describes: once praise is printed without limit, a hard piece earns the same reward as an easy one, so why choose it? What I'd push on is whether the students noticed the trade at all.

Made-up example. The second column connects the two writers and ends on a sentence that is the writer's own.

2 · Before you write: the essay and the par. 16 line

His steps (9/14): C1 read and annotate "The Loser Edit" by Colson Whitehead — "(The visual argument demands your attention.)" The section he points you to for C2 (par. 16):

From his assignment
"Everyone else has to do it by himself or herself, assembling our edits through a thousand compulsive Facebook tweaks, endless calibrations of Twitter personas, Instagram posts filtered of all disturbance" (par. 16)

Guiding questions (yours to answer from your annotations):

  • In your own words, what is a "loser edit"? Could you explain it to a friend without the essay open?
  • Who is "everyone else" in par. 16, and what is "it" that they have to do by themselves?
  • What is the argument being made, and what is your position on it? (His question at the 9/15 class.)

3 · The synthesis move (C2 & C3): put sources in conversation

C2: apply the par. 16 section, "in a paragraph or two" (in class 9/15 he added "lean more toward two"), to multiple sections/ideas from "Have Smartphones Destroyed a Generation?"

C3: add another paragraph from any other piece you have read in this class — "you choose, you connect." Remember title, author, date, etc. "as to be accurate in your connections."

Made-up example · worked "C2" on the invented pair

Ruiz's image of trophies as "another bill printed by a bank that never closes" (par. 3) names the cost Ellis only watches from the outside: her students aren't lazy, they are reading the exchange rate correctly — if every attempt pays the same, the hard piece is a bad investment. Ellis's story shows the result; Ruiz supplies the reason. What I notice is that neither asks the students what they wanted the praise for, and that's the part I'd push on.

Notice: a specific quote → specific ideas in the other text → a last sentence that is clearly mine.

4 · The rhetorical-analysis move (C4): his basic model

C4 (9/14): one paragraph "analyzing the rhetorical strategies Whitehead uses (Glossary of Terms) to achieve his purpose (you, obviously, must know what his purpose is)." He suggests sentence structure or even punctuation, and one Glossary of Terms term you are familiar with AND one you are still learning — "application is the best way to learn."

[author] in the [date] essay "[title]" uses [term] in paragraph [#] in order to [explains the intent, the use, and the e/affect].

His "basic model" (9/14), to learn "in the mechanical form" first; he says it later builds into style.

Fill the blanks and your sentence builds here.
Made-up example · worked "C4"

Ruiz, in the 2019 essay "The Participation Shelf," uses an extended metaphor (per his Glossary: "a metaphor developed at great length, occurring frequently in or throughout a work") of a bank that never stops printing money in paragraph 3 in order to make praise feel like a currency that loses value, so the reader sees each extra trophy as a small loss rather than a kindness.

His tip from class 9/15: "Uses vs. Appeals to"

What NOT to do: "In his essay The Loser Edit, Colson Whitehead uses ethos in order to___________"
What TO DO: "In his essay The Loser Edit, Colson Whitehead appeals to ethos by ___________"

His 9/15 email: the appeals "are not tools to be used; instead, they are end goals for a writer or speaker."

Guiding questions for Whitehead: what is his purpose, in one sentence of yours? Which Glossary term do you already know that he uses, and which one are you still learning? Where do his sentence structure or punctuation do work?

His 9/14 sidenote: "Question 2 on the Exam is entirely about the application of finding Glossary of Terms terms an author uses and being able to place the 'in order to clause' into a sentence which explains the intent, the use, and the e/affect."
