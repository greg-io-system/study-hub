Sensation & Perception

The one idea that ties both topics together:
Sensation is picking up information from the world (light, sound, chemicals, pressure, temperature) and turning it into neural signals.
Perception is selecting, organizing and interpreting those signals so you can tell what the stimulus is.
Sensation says "something is there." Perception says "what it is."

One word you'll see everywhere: transduction, which means converting the energy of a stimulus into neural activity. Stimulus (light, sound, chemicals, pressure) → sensory receptors (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin) → neural impulses → the brain's visual, auditory, smell areas and so on.

1.6 · How much is enough to notice?

Absolute threshold

The minimum stimulation needed to detect a stimulus at least 50% of the time. On a detection graph, it's the intensity at the 50% line. A lower threshold means you can detect fainter stimuli.

Just-noticeable difference (JND)

The smallest change in a stimulus you can detect. The absolute threshold asks "can I notice it at all?" The JND asks "can I notice it changed?"

Weber's law: the bigger it is, the bigger the change you need

The difference you can notice has to be proportional to the starting intensity. Turning up a quiet radio a little is noticeable; if it's already loud, you need a bigger jump to hear a difference.

Try it

Pretend the JND is 2% (a made-up number, just for this demo): someone has to add about 0.4 lb before you'd notice.

Slide it up: the heavier the bag, the bigger the change you need. That's Weber's law.

1.6 · Adaptation, interaction, and synesthesia

  • Sensory adaptation: receptors become less responsive to a stimulus that stays constant. A scent in a room seems to fade even though the amount in the air hasn't changed. That's the receptors, not you choosing to ignore it.
  • Sensory interaction: one sense influences another, as when smell affects taste. Eating pizza is taste, smell, texture (touch) and sight working together.
  • Synesthesia: stimulating one sensory pathway automatically and involuntarily triggers an experience in another. Two types: grapheme-color (letters or numbers seem colored) and chromesthesia (sounds bring colors).

1.6 · Vision

Vision is considered the dominant sense because it gives you so much information about your surroundings. Humans see only a sliver of all light waves: visible light runs from about 380 to 750 nanometers. Radio waves, microwaves and x-rays fall outside it, so they're beyond vision's absolute threshold.

The path of light: tap through it

Rods

For dim light and detecting motion. Black-and-white vision only, but very sensitive to light. (A night-lit bedroom: shapes and shadows, no colors.)

Cones

For color and fine detail in bright light. Three types (red, green, blue). Most concentrated in the fovea.

  • Fovea: the center of the retina, for sharp central vision. Cones only, no rods. Whatever you look straight at lands here.
  • Ganglion cells: retinal neurons that take signals from the bipolar cells and send them to the brain through the optic nerve. They respond to changes in light and help detect movement.
  • Accommodation: the lens changes its shape (its curvature) to focus near or far objects on the retina, mainly through the ciliary muscles. When accommodation goes wrong, the result is nearsightedness or farsightedness.
  • Blind spot: where the optic nerve leaves the eye. No rods or cones there, so no vision, and your brain fills in the gap from what's around it.

1.6 · How you see color: two theories

Trichromatic theory

Three kinds of cones: short wavelength (blue), medium (green) and long (red). The brain combines how much each fires to make every color.

Opponent-process

Color works in opposing pairs: red-green, blue-yellow, black-white. Activating one side inhibits the other. These opposing cells are ganglion cells in the retina, and some neurons in the thalamus work the same way. That explains afterimages.

Afterimage: a visual sensation that lasts after the stimulus is gone, from temporarily overstimulated cones. Stare at red, then look at white, and you see its opposite: green.

Try it: stare at the black dot for 30 seconds, then watch the white box.
The flag is green, black and yellow. What colors do you see after?
  • Color vision deficiency: irregular cones or ganglion cells. Dichromatism: perceiving only two of the three visual pigments. Red-green is the most common. Monochromatism: no color at all, only shades of gray. It's rare.
  • Ishihara test: a number made of colored dots on a background of other dots. Normal color vision reads "74"; someone with red-green deficiency may read "21".
  • Prosopagnosia (face blindness): you see faces but can't recognize them as familiar. It comes from a problem in the connection among the temporal lobe, occipital cortex and fusiform gyrus. About 60% of people with it also struggle to recognize animals' faces. People with it learn to rely on unique features, tone of voice and context.
  • Blindsight: after damage to the primary visual cortex, a person can still detect and locate things in the "blind" part of their vision, sometimes even movement or flicker, with no conscious awareness of seeing them.

How the brain knows the pitch

  • Place theory (Helmholtz): different pitches activate different places on the cochlea's basilar membrane. High frequencies vibrate near the beginning of the membrane; low ones vibrate more of it. It explains high pitches well but not low ones.
  • Frequency theory: the whole membrane vibrates, and the auditory nerve fires at the same rate as the sound wave: 100 waves per second, 100 pulses per second. It explains low pitches. The catch: one neuron can't fire more than about 1,000 times a second.
  • Volley theory: groups of neurons fire in rapid succession, taking turns like soldiers alternating shots while others reload, to encode sounds above 1,000 Hz. Place and frequency together cover the middle range.
  • Sound localization: sound reaches the closer ear sooner and with more intensity, and your brain reads that small difference. It's most accurate when the sound is not straight in front of or behind you.

Where things are: outer and middle ear = ear canal, eardrum, middle-ear bones (ossicles). Inner ear = the cochlea (with the basilar membrane) and the semicircular canals. Then the auditory nerve carries the signal to the brain.

Conduction deafness

A problem in the outer or middle ear: damage to the ear canal, eardrum or middle-ear bones.

Sensorineural deafness

Damage to the inner ear or auditory nerve. Soft sounds and speech get hard to hear, and it's often permanent.

1.6 · Smell (olfaction) & taste (gustation)

Both senses respond to chemicals.

Smell: olfaction
  • Odor molecules carried in the air dissolve into nasal mucus and stimulate olfactory receptors in the nasal cavity, which transduce them into neural messages.
  • Smell is routed through the limbic system instead of the thalamus. That's why scents are so strongly tied to memories and emotions.
  • Pheromones: chemical signals released by members of a species that affect the behavior of others of the same species (attracting a mate, signaling alarm, marking territory). True pheromones in humans are controversial; menstrual synchrony has been suggested as one effect.
  • Your sense of smell declines with age.
Taste: gustation
  • Taste cells sit in taste buds on the tongue (with the papillae), in the throat and on the soft palate. They renew about every week or two.
  • Sweet (sugars), sour (acids, like citrus), salty (salt), bitter (often plant foods), umami (savory: amino acids, protein foods like meat and cheese), and oleogustus, a proposed taste for fat that is still debated.
  • Supertasters (about 25%): many taste buds, very sensitive to some tastes including spicy and to a particular bitter chemical. Women are more likely to be supertasters. Medium tasters: average. Nontasters (about 25%): can't taste that bitter compound.

1.6 · Touch & pain

The touch pathway: touch receptors in the skin → sensory nerves → spinal cord → thalamus (the relay) → somatosensory cortex in the parietal lobe, where it becomes a touch perception. Damage there can leave someone unable to feel touch or temperature on part of the body even though they can move normally.

Warm + cold = hot: stimulating nearby warm and cold spots at once feels hot.

  • Pain is processed in both the body and the brain. The same stimulus can hurt one person and thrill another (a tattoo). Pain tolerance, mindset and past experience all change it.
  • Gate-control theory: a neural "gate" in the spinal cord can open to let pain signals reach the brain or close to block them. Rubbing a stubbed toe activates larger, non-pain fibers that help close the gate.
  • Phantom limb: feeling sensation, even pain, in a limb that's gone. The brain's map of the limb stays intact and keeps signaling it. About 7 in 10 amputees feel pain there.

1.6 · The two body senses

Kinesthetic sense

Sensing the position and movement of your body parts, from receptors in muscles, tendons and joints. It lets you coordinate walking, talking and gestures, and touch your nose with your eyes closed. A leg "falling asleep" disrupts it.

Vestibular sense

Balance, controlled in the inner ear by the semicircular canals, whose receptors detect head motion. Spinning in circles disrupts their fluid, and that's why you get dizzy.

2.1 · Bottom-up vs. top-down

Bottom-up processing

Details to big picture. Start with the small sensory details and build up to the whole, with no expectation guiding you. Memory trick: Build Up.

Top-down processing

Big picture to details. Interpret using context, prior knowledge and expectations. It's why you can rd ths sntnce. Memory trick: Think Down.

  • Schemas: mental frameworks that organize and interpret information. They shape what you expect and what you pay attention to. Your "restaurant" schema tells you what to do even in a restaurant you've never been to.
  • Perceptual set: a tendency to notice some parts of what you sense and ignore others, so you see what you expect to see. It's shaped by expectations, experiences and context. At dusk on a forest trail, a twisted branch looks like a snake.
    Picture this: the same shape reads as "B" between A and C, and as "13" between 12 and 14. That's context at work.

2.1 · Gestalt: the whole is different from the sum of its parts

Gestalt psychology says we perceive whole objects (gestalts), not just collections of parts. The brain is wired to find structure and pattern, like seeing a face in a painting made of fruits and vegetables.

Figure-ground: separating an object (the figure) from its surroundings (the ground), like your keys on a cluttered desk. In the Rubin vase you see either a vase or two faces, and the view can flip with attention.

  • Proximity: things close together group. You see three groups of dots, not 24 separate ones.
  • Similarity: things that look alike group. You see rows of color.
  • Closure: you see incomplete shapes as complete. You see a circle, not four curved lines.
  • Continuity: you perceive continuous patterns.

2.1 · Attention decides what you perceive

  • Selective attention: focusing on one stimulus while shutting out the others.
  • Cocktail party effect: tuning in to one voice among many conversations and background noise, and catching your own name across a noisy room. The auditory cortex boosts some sounds so the brain can prioritize, and the reticular activating system is involved.
  • Inattentional blindness: failing to notice an unexpected thing in full view because your attention is on a task. Most people are sure they'd notice it; many don't.
  • Change blindness: failing to notice a large change when it happens during a visual disruption. While you're scrolling your phone at dinner, someone swaps your water for soda, and it takes you minutes to notice.

2.1 · How you see depth

Binocular cues (need both eyes)
  • Retinal disparity: each eye gets a slightly different image because the eyes sit apart. The brain compares and fuses the two images, and the difference is a cue to depth.
  • Convergence: your eyes turn inward to focus on a close object. The closer it is, the more they converge, and the eye muscles' sensations tell your brain the distance. Try it: bring a finger slowly toward your nose.
Monocular cues (one eye is enough)
  • Linear perspective: parallel lines seem to converge in the distance, like a road meeting the horizon.
  • Interposition: the object that overlaps another is seen as closer.
  • Relative size: of objects expected to be the same size, the larger one looks closer.
  • Relative clarity: clear, detailed objects look closer; hazy ones look farther, like distant mountains. There are fewer particles in the air between you and near things.
  • Texture gradient: textures lose detail the farther away they are.

2.1 · Constancy and motion

Perceptual constancy: seeing objects as unchanging even when the image on your retina (its size, shape or color) changes.

  • Shape constancy: a door swinging open, or a car seen from the side vs. the front, keeps its real shape in your mind.
  • Size constancy: a plane overhead looks small enough to fit between your fingers, but you know it's huge.
  • Color constancy: a red apple looks red in bright or dim light.
  • Brightness constancy: white paper looks about as bright at dusk as in daylight, even though it reflects different amounts of light.

Apparent movement: seeing motion when nothing is actually moving.

  • Stroboscopic movement: a series of separate still images shown in quick succession looks like motion. That's how movies and animation work.
  • Phi phenomenon: lights flashed in sequence a short distance apart on a sign seem to move.
