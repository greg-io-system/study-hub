Sensation & Perception

For the AMSCO 1.6 and 2.1 reading (pp. 208–228 and 245–257). Read this first for the big picture, then the pages will make more sense. Use it to fill your workbook in your own words, not to copy from.

The one idea that ties both topics together:
Sensation is your body picking up energy from the world (light, sound waves, chemicals, pressure) and turning it into nerve signals.
Perception is your brain making sense of those signals: organizing them, interpreting them, deciding what they mean.
Sensation is the raw data. Perception is the story your brain tells about it.

One word you'll see everywhere: transduction, which means converting one kind of energy into another. Every sense does it: the eye turns light into neural signals, the ear turns vibrations into neural signals, and so on. Each sense has its own receptor cells that do the converting.

1.6 · How much is enough to notice?

Absolute threshold

The weakest stimulus you can detect half the time (50%). The faintest whisper, the dimmest light. Below it, the stimulus is subliminal: below conscious detection.

Difference threshold

The smallest change between two stimuli you can notice half the time. Also called the just-noticeable difference (JND).

Weber's law: the JND is a percentage, not a fixed amount

To notice a difference, the change has to be a constant proportion of the original. Adding one candle to a dark room is obvious. Adding one candle to a stadium's lights is invisible. Same candle, different starting point.

If the JND for weight is about 2% (an illustration; the exact percentage depends on the sense), someone has to add about 0.4 lb before you'd notice.

Slide it up: the heavier the bag, the bigger the change you need. That's Weber's law.

1.6 · Signal detection theory

Detecting something isn't only about how strong it is. It also depends on you: your expectations, motivation, alertness and what's at stake. Picture a lifeguard scanning for a swimmer in trouble. Tap each box.

1.6 · Adaptation, interaction, and synesthesia

  • Sensory adaptation: your sensitivity drops when a stimulus stays constant. You stop smelling your own house, stop feeling your socks. The point: it frees your attention for change, which is usually what matters for survival.
  • Sensory interaction: one sense affects another. Food tastes bland with a blocked nose, because smell is a big part of "flavor." Seeing lips move changes what you think you hear.
  • Synesthesia: stimulating one sense automatically triggers an experience in another. Some people see colors when they hear music, or "taste" words. It's rare and real.

1.6 · Vision

What light carries

Amplitude / intensityheight of the wave: how much energyBrightness

The path of light: tap through it

Rods

Work in dim light; see in black, white and gray; spread around the edges of the retina (peripheral vision). About 120 million.

Cones

Need brighter light; see color and fine detail; packed into the fovea, the retina's center. About 6 million.

  • Accommodation: the lens changes shape to focus near or far.
  • Blind spot: where the optic nerve leaves the eye. There are no receptors there, and your brain fills in the gap.
  • Dark adaptation: walking into a dark theater, you can barely see at first. Over about 20–30 minutes the rods get more sensitive.
  • Optic chiasm: where the optic nerves cross, so each side of the brain gets the opposite half of what you see.
  • Nearsighted vs. farsighted: nearsighted means the image focuses in front of the retina, so far things blur. Farsighted means it focuses behind the retina, so near things blur.

1.6 · How you see color (two theories, both right)

Young-Helmholtz (trichromatic)

The retina has three kinds of cones, most sensitive to red, green and blue. Every color is a mix of how strongly each fires. This explains color at the receptors.

Opponent-process

After the retina, cells work in opposing pairs: red vs. green, blue vs. yellow, black vs. white. When one side gets tired, the other side "wins." That explains afterimages.

Try it: stare at the black dot for 20 seconds, then watch the white box.
The flag is green, black and yellow. What colors do you see after?
  • Color blindness: dichromatism means one cone type is missing or weak, so you see only two-color combinations. Red-green is the most common, especially in males. Monochromatism means only one cone type or none, so the world looks like shades of gray. It's very rare.
  • Prosopagnosia: "face blindness" from brain damage. You see a face perfectly well but can't recognize whose it is.
  • Blindsight: damage to the visual cortex leaves someone consciously blind in part of their vision, yet they can still react to things there (point to them, dodge them) without knowing why. Seeing and being aware of seeing are separate systems.

Frequencyhow many waves pass per second (Hz)Pitch. High frequency = high pitch.
Amplitudeheight of the wave: how much energyLoudness, measured in decibels

The path of sound: tap through it

How the brain knows the pitch

  • Place theory: different pitches make different spots on the basilar membrane vibrate most. This works best for high pitches.
  • Frequency theory: the auditory nerve fires at the same rate as the sound wave. This works for low pitches.
  • Volley principle: a single neuron can't fire fast enough for middle-high pitches, so groups of neurons take turns, like soldiers firing in volleys, to keep up the combined rate.
  • Sound localization: a sound to your right reaches your right ear a split second sooner and slightly louder. Your brain uses that difference to locate it.
Conductive hearing loss

A mechanical problem: damage to the eardrum or ossicles, so vibrations don't get carried in. Often helped by hearing aids (which amplify) or surgery.

Sensorineural hearing loss

Damage to the hair cells in the cochlea or the auditory nerve, often from loud noise or aging. A cochlear implant can turn sound into electrical signals that stimulate the nerve directly.

1.6 · Smell (olfaction) & taste (gustation)

These are the chemical senses: receptors respond to molecules, not energy waves.

Smell: olfaction
  • Receptors high in the nasal cavity send signals to the olfactory bulb.
  • Smell is the one sense that skips the thalamus. It runs close to the brain's emotion and memory areas, which is why a smell can bring back a memory instantly.
  • Survival job: detect danger (smoke, spoiled food) and recognize people.
  • Pheromones: chemical signals that carry messages between animals of the same species. Their role in humans is debated.
Taste: gustation
  • Receptor cells sit in taste buds, mostly on the tongue's bumps (papillae).
  • The basic tastes each flag something useful: sweet = energy, salty = sodium your body needs, sour and bitter = possible spoiled food or poison, umami = protein. Many texts add fat (oleogustus) as a sixth.
  • Supertasters have many more taste buds, so flavors (especially bitter) hit them much harder.

1.6 · Touch & pain

Your skin has receptors for four basic sensations: pressure, warmth, cold and pain. Other feelings are mixes. "Wet," for example, is cold plus pressure.

  • Gate-control theory: the spinal cord has a neural "gate." Pain signals on small nerve fibers open it. Signals on larger fibers (like rubbing a bumped elbow) or messages from the brain can close it. That's why rubbing helps, and why distraction works.
  • Phantom limb sensation: people who've lost a limb can still feel it, even feel pain in it. Pain is built by the brain, not just the body part.
  • Changing pain: distraction, endorphins (the body's natural painkillers, released during exercise or stress), placebos, hypnosis, and expectations all change how much it hurts.

1.6 · The two "hidden" body senses

Kinesthesis

Knowing where your body parts are and how they're moving, from receptors in muscles, tendons and joints. It's how you can touch your nose with your eyes closed.

Vestibular sense

Your sense of balance and head movement, from the fluid-filled semicircular canals in the inner ear. Spin around, then stop: the fluid keeps moving, and you feel dizzy.

2.1 · Bottom-up vs. top-down

Bottom-up processing

Starts with the raw sensory data and builds up: lines, then shapes, then "it's a letter A." It's data-driven.

Top-down processing

Starts with what you already know or expect and uses it to interpret. It's why you can rd ths sntnce.

Perceptual set is a mental "readiness" to see one thing rather than another. It's shaped by:

  • Context: the same shape reads as "B" between A and C, and as "13" between 12 and 14.
  • Expectations: tell someone a blurry photo is a lake monster and they'll see one.
  • Culture: what you grew up seeing shapes what you notice and how you read images.
  • Motivation: when you're hungry, food-related things jump out at you.
  • Emotion: a hill looks steeper when you're sad or tired.

2.1 · Gestalt: the whole is more than its parts

Gestalt psychologists showed the brain organizes pieces into wholes automatically. The first move is figure-ground: splitting what you see into an object (the figure) and its surroundings (the ground). Then it groups:

  • Proximity: things close together group. You see three groups of dots, not 24 separate ones.
  • Similarity: things that look alike group. You see rows of color.
  • Closure: you fill in gaps to see a whole. You see a circle, not four curved lines.

2.1 · Attention decides what you perceive

  • Selective attention: you can only focus on a small slice of everything your senses pick up.
  • Cocktail party effect: in a noisy room you tune everyone out, until someone across the room says your name and you hear it instantly.
  • Inattentional blindness: missing something in plain view because your attention is elsewhere. In a famous study, people counting basketball passes missed a person in a gorilla suit walking through.
  • Change blindness: failing to notice a change, like a different person after a brief interruption, or an edit between movie shots.

2.1 · How you see depth in a flat image

Your retinas are flat, yet you see in 3-D. The visual cliff experiment showed babies who can crawl already refuse to crawl over what looks like a drop (it's covered by glass). So depth perception shows up early.

Binocular cues (need both eyes)
  • Retinal disparity: each eye sees a slightly different image. The bigger the difference, the closer the object. (Hold a finger near your nose and close each eye in turn.)
  • Convergence: your eyes turn inward more for close objects, and your brain reads the strain.
Monocular cues (one eye is enough)
  • Linear perspective: parallel lines seem to meet in the distance.
  • Interposition: whatever blocks something else is closer.
  • Relative size: of two similar objects, the smaller one looks farther.
  • Relative clarity: hazy things look farther (distant mountains).
  • Texture gradient: texture gets finer and denser with distance.

2.1 · Constancy, motion, and adapting

  • Perceptual constancy: you see objects as unchanging even when the image on your retina changes. Shape constancy: a door swinging open still looks rectangular. Size constancy: a friend walking away doesn't seem to shrink. Color/brightness constancy: a red apple still looks red in dim light.
  • Apparent movement: seeing motion where there isn't any.
    • Stroboscopic effect: a rapid series of still images looks like smooth motion. That's how flipbooks and movies work.
    • Phi phenomenon: lights blinking on and off in sequence seem to move, like a marquee sign.
    • Autokinetic effect: a single still point of light in a dark room seems to drift.
  • Perceptual adaptation: the brain adjusts to a changed view. People wearing goggles that shift or flip their vision are clumsy at first, then adapt within days. The survival value is that you can keep functioning when your senses change.
