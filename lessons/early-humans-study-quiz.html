Study guide

What you should be able to do

  • Put the human ancestors in order and say what made each a step forward.
  • Describe the tools and technologies early humans developed.
  • Explain why and how early humans migrated out of Africa.
  • Describe Paleolithic hunter-gatherer life (groups, diet, shelter, belief, equality, gender).
  • Explain the Neolithic Revolution: what changed, why, and the trade-offs.
  • Describe early Neolithic settlements (Çatalhöyük, Jericho, Mehrgarh, China).
  • Use Göbekli Tepe to explain why our understanding of prehistory keeps being revised.

The ancestor timeline (memorize the order + one fact each)

  1. Australopithecus (2.5–4 mya) — upright, small brain ("Lucy").
  2. Homo habilis (2–3 mya) — "handy," bigger brain; possibly the earliest member of the genus Homo.
  3. Homo erectus (~2 mya) — fully upright, fire, tools; began migrating beyond Africa 1.8–1.5 mya (to North Africa, the Near East, Europe, and East and South Asia).
  4. Neanderthals / Denisovans / Homo heidelbergensis — later species; some interbred with us.
  5. Homo sapiens (~200,000 ya in the textbook) — "wise"; the only human species left.

mya = million years ago; ya = years ago.

Walking upright moved the digestive organs lower in the body, which pushed the diet toward foods that were easier to digest.

Tools and technology

  • Oldowan tools — the first simple stone tools, likely made by Homo habilis.
  • Acheulean hand-axes — thinner and sharper; the main tool style until about 250,000 years ago.
  • Mousterian tools — smaller tools made from stone flakes, used for butchering, scraping leather and as spearheads (250,000–50,000 ya).
  • Fire — controlled by at least about 125,000 years ago: warmth, scaring off predators, cooking meat, making some tools.
  • Language — humans can produce about fifty phonemes (distinct sounds), enough for tens of thousands of words.
  • Art — the Venus of Hohle Fels (mammoth ivory, ~40,000 ya, the oldest such figure found) and the cave paintings of Altamira in Spain.

Migration — cause and mechanism

  • Cause: drying African climate → fewer resources → hunter-gatherers follow food.
  • Mechanism: ice-age low sea levels exposed land bridges (SE Asia, Bering Strait); rafts reached Australia. Waves back-and-forth over tens of thousands of years, not a straight march. People entered North America from Siberia.
  • The last glaciation began a little over 100,000 years ago, peaked about 18,000 years ago, and ended about 12,000 years ago.
  • Rough route/dates (textbook): out of Africa ~100,000 ya → Asia/India/China ~70,000 → Australia ≥45,000 → Europe ~40,000 → Americas ~15,000.
  • Two models: Out of Africa (Homo sapiens evolved in Africa and spread) vs. multiregional (Homo sapiens evolved from Homo erectus in several places around the same time).

Paleolithic life — the checklist

  • Groups: small bands, family-based, cooperative (see the band-size row above). Much bigger groups would have struggled to find enough food.
  • Diet: foraged plants + hunted/scavenged animals; environment-dependent.
  • Shelter: caves and built huts (wood, bone, hide).
  • Belief: early spirituality, likely animism; burials + cave art.
  • Society: largely egalitarian; labor split by sex (women gather, men hunt) but both essential. Modern San groups are largely leaderless.
  • Modern comparisons: San (Kalahari), Inuit (Arctic).
  • Your teacher's Unit 1 study guide adds: a cyclical view of time, strong ceremonial and religious life, seasonal mobility, no permanent leaders.

Neolithic Revolution — the heart of the chapter

  • What: shift to agriculture + animal domestication, ~12,000 ya.
  • Where: independently in several places, each with its own crops:
  • Near East (Fertile Crescent): wheat and barley, ~12,000 ya
  • China (Yangtze and Yellow Rivers): rice, millet, soybeans, ~10,000 ya
  • Sub-Saharan Africa (likely around modern Sudan): sorghum, possibly ~6,000 ya
  • Mesoamerica: squash, later maize
  • Andes: potatoes, ~5,000 ya
  • Animals: sheep and goats in the Near East ~10,000 ya; chickens in South Asia ~8,000 ya; horses in Central Asia ~6,000 ya; llamas in Peru.
  • Why: end of the ice age → drier climate → people tend/select plants → domestication (humans decide which plants grow).
  • Trade-offs table:
GainedLost
Larger populationsLess leisure (modern foragers need about 20 hours a week to get food)
A more reliable, storable food supplyMore epidemic disease
Specialization (artisans, traders, priests, rulers)Narrower, less healthy diet
Wealth + inheritanceEgalitarianism → class hierarchy
  • Gender shift: in farming societies women were relegated to the domestic sphere, while men had opportunities for leadership that women did not. (Studies the text cites found women lost the most leisure in farming societies, spending far more time on laborious tasks outside the home than hunter-gatherer women.)

Early Neolithic settlements

  • Çatalhöyük (southeastern Turkey): mud-brick houses packed together with no streets; farmers, hunters and craftspeople; bull imagery suggests they venerated cattle; neither fully farming nor fully foraging; obsidian traded as far as Syria and Cyprus.
  • Jericho (Jordan River valley): occupied as early as 8300 BCE; protected by a ditch and a thick stone wall.
  • Mehrgarh (modern Pakistan): the earliest evidence of farming in South Asia.

Why Göbekli Tepe matters

Built ~11,600 ya, before local farming. It flipped the old assumption ("farming first, then temples") and led to a reevaluation of the Neolithic. Another reading: archaeologist Lee Clare found it was a year-round settlement and suggested its builders may have been resisting agriculture.

Quick self-check

  1. Which species began migrating beyond Africa 1.8–1.5 million years ago, and what let it happen?
  2. Name two ways low ice-age sea levels shaped human migration.
  3. Give three trade-offs of adopting agriculture (one good, two bad).
  4. How did women's roles change with farming?
  5. What did Göbekli Tepe change about our understanding of the Neolithic?

Common traps

  • Paleolithic vs. Neolithic: Paleolithic = old stone age / hunter- gatherers; Neolithic = new stone age / farming. Don't swap them.
  • Agriculture was not simply "better" — it was a trade-off (more food and hierarchy, but more disease and less leisure).
  • The Neolithic Revolution happened in several places independently, not one origin that spread everywhere.
  • "Out of Africa" vs. "multiregional" are the two models — know both.

19

Farming produced more food, yet the chapter says women were "relegated to the domestic sphere" in farming societies. Explain why.

Show answer

Full credit (2): In farming societies men typically worked the fields (associated with plow strength) and gained public/leadership roles, while women were relegated to the domestic sphere — milling grain, pottery, weaving, child-rearing — and men had leadership opportunities women did not. Hunter-gatherer women, by contrast, contributed vital gathered calories and also protected homes, repaired shelters and made tools. (1 pt gender division in farming, 1 pt the comparison to hunter-gatherer life.)

Trade-off analysis (4 pts): The Neolithic Revolution is described as a trade-off, not simply "progress." List two benefits and two costs of adopting agriculture, and then argue in 2–3 sentences whether you think it was worth it for the people who lived through it.

Show answer

Trade-off analysis (4 pts) — rubric:

  • +1 each for two valid benefits: larger populations; storable food surplus; labor specialization (artisans/traders/priests/rulers); more workers and defenders.
  • +1 each for two valid costs: less leisure (esp. women); more epidemic disease; weather dependence with no easy migration; narrower/ less healthy diet; loss of egalitarianism → class hierarchy.
  • (Max +4 for the four items.) The final judgment is not graded for which side they pick — give full credit for any position that uses the listed trade-offs as evidence. Strong answers note it depended on who you were: elites gained wealth and status, while most people faced harder labor and worse health.
