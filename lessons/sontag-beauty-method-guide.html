Into the parlor with Sontag — a method guide

Not what to write — how to get from the reading to the paragraphs. The same moves carry into Friday's essay.

The assignment in his words (For Monday email, 11/13): "Develop a series of paragraphs that enter you into a parlor of conversation with Sontag around the idea of beauty."

The idea he asks you to use: "As a culture changes, so do its definitions."

What it is NOT: "Please do not merely agree or disagree with Sontag's definition or her argument; instead, consider, in writing, where your definitions collide or differ. Please do not merely summarize her definition."

What it IS: "Sontag is writing into her context: 1975. So, as our culture has changed (has it?), how has our definition or understanding of beauty changed (has it)?" And then: "Those two 'has it's' may just well be your writing."

Why this one counts twice. The tools you use here (F, O, E) are the same ones he asks for in the Topical Writing essay, "Define ___", due before class Friday 11/20. What you practice tonight is the method for that essay. The last section shows how it carries over.

1 · Two lenses, six margin tags

His email: "I asked you to read for her Exigence, Audience, and Purpose as well as her F, O, and E's." That is two lenses with three parts each. Give each part a short tag you can write in the margin.

Lens 1 · the Rhetorical Situation
  • ExExigence. His question (class 9/1): "What motivated the author (psychologically, emotionally) to write?"
  • AAudience. His questions (class 9/1): "Who do you think was the author's primary, immediate audience? Who do you think are important secondary, mediated audiences?"
  • PPurpose. His question (class 9/1): "What is the purpose of the piece in the direction of the audience?"

His rule for this lens (For Monday email): "each of these is specific and found IN THE TEXT. Do not assign or judge a writer's exigence; instead, analyze."

Lens 2 · the argument of definition
  • FFormal. His question: "What is her FORMAL DEFINITION?" From his Arguments of Definition handout: a formal definition puts the term in its class, then names the features that set it apart from the rest of that class.
  • OOperational. His question: "What are the conditions under which that definition exists (Operational)?" From the handout: operational definitions "identify an object or idea by what it does or by what conditions create it."
  • EBy example. His question: "What examples and evidence does she use to link her 'O' to her 'F'?" From the handout: definitions by example "define a class by listing its individual members."

The four steps below are modeled on a made-up essay about a different word, so Sontag's essay and your own thinking about beauty stay yours.

Made-up example · an invented essay
"The Fence Line," Ruth Hadley, 1962 (invented). A short argument of definition about the word neighbor.

2 · Step 1: mark up the essay itself

His instruction: "Read for specific moments that can become EVIDENCE. In other words, identify moments that you would like to respond to through these lenses."

Try it
As you read, write the tag in the margin beside the exact line that earns it. A tag with no line beside it is a guess; a tagged line is evidence you can quote later.
Made-up example · tagged lines from "The Fence Line"
  • Ex"Three houses on my street flooded this spring, and not one door was knocked on."
  • A"Those of us with tidy lawns and locked gates will say we were only minding our business."
  • F"A neighbor is a person whose trouble is yours because you can see it from your door."
  • O"That held when porches faced the street and somebody was always home."
  • E"The borrowed ladder, the casserole, the spare key under the mat."
  • P"It is time we walked the fence line again."

Notice the F line has both halves of a formal definition: the class (a person) and the feature that sets a neighbor apart (their trouble is yours because you can see it).

3 · Step 2: write her claims as a chain

His warning about this essay: "This is a complex argument (Understatement). Read carefully as to not miss the series of claims that lead to an overarching argument (which is what?). You will be talking through her line of reasoning in class. Consider how she builds what she builds."

Try it
One numbered line per claim, in her order, in your own words. The last line is the overarching argument: his "(which is what?)". If you can read your list top to bottom and each line leads to the next, you have her line of reasoning ready for class.
Made-up example · Hadley's chain
  1. A neighbor used to mean anyone whose trouble you could see from your door.
  2. Fences, cars and locked gates shrank that to the people we choose to wave at.
  3. So a "good neighbor" now means someone who leaves you alone.
  4. Overarching: we turned neighbor from a duty into a courtesy, and lost the help that came with the duty.

4 · Step 3: build a response list

This is where his two "has it's" get answered. Star the two or three claims in your chain that you most want to answer, and write three lines for each.

The three lines

Her momentThe line from the text you are answering (one of your tagged lines).

NowStill true? Changed? Changed on the surface only? Give one example from the culture today.

So whatWhat that tells you about how the definition has, or has not, moved.

His "(has it?)" is a real question. "It has not changed, and here is the evidence" is as much an answer as "it has."

Made-up example · one entry on "The Fence Line"

Her moment"A good neighbor, we now say, is one who leaves you alone."

NowStill true on the sidewalk. Changed on the phone: a street's group chat where someone asks for a ladder and gets three offers within the hour.

So whatThe definition did not go back to Hadley's. Its condition moved, from who you can see to who you can reach. "Their trouble is yours" survived; "from your door" did not.

Each entry is already a paragraph in outline. Look at what the lines hold: her moment is your evidence, the "now" line is a condition with an example (an O and an E), and the "so what" is your explanation.

5 · Step 4: four lines before you draft

His email: "What is YOUR Exigence, Audience, and Purpose when it comes to the conversation of beauty? Again, this should come through the writing itself and not be explained by the writer." And: "What is your definition (F, O, E)?"

Try it
Write these four lines at the top of your scratch page. They steer the writing; they do not go into it as sentences.
  1. Why now? What about this conversation matters to you (your exigence).
  2. Who am I talking to? Be specific (your audience).
  3. What do I want them to see? (your purpose).
  4. My definition in one sentence (your F). Your O's and E's are already sitting in the "now" lines of your response list.
Explained by the writer
My audience is people my age, and my purpose is to show that neighbors have changed.
Coming through the writing
A reader who has muted the street's group chat already knows what Hadley meant by a locked gate.

Made-up example. The second version never names its audience or purpose, but you can tell who it is talking to and what it wants them to notice.

6 · From the list to the paragraphs

He asks for "a series of paragraphs", and warns against "answering in a single chunk, one-paragraph response which does not denote attention to categorization". Your response list already gives you the categories.

One way to build it
  1. Open by placing her argument. His email: "Continue to build your work to experiment with the four sentences of the rhetorical precis." The last line of your claim chain (her overarching argument) is the THAT clause for sentence 1. The precis guide has the four sentences.
  2. One paragraph per starred entry: her moment, then now, then so what.
  3. Land on your own definition, stated as a position.

Why evidence and explanation matter, in his words:"Use Evidence and Explanation in your analysis of arguments and in your own arguments (redundant). This will move your analysis from opinion to POSITION. Without an informed position, you are not allowed into the parlor."

Agree / disagree (he rules this out)
I agree with Hadley that neighbors are less friendly now. I think people should help each other more.
In the parlor
Hadley's 1962 complaint that a good neighbor "is one who leaves you alone" still describes the sidewalk, but the street has grown a second front door. A block's group chat turns a missing ladder into three offers within the hour, from people who have never stood on each other's porches. Help did not vanish with the open porch; its condition changed, from who can be seen to who can be reached, and a definition of neighbor written today has to start there.

Made-up example, built from the entry in step 3. Her line is the evidence, the group chat is today's example, and the last sentence is the writer's own position.

Two more things he asks for in this email:

  • Write about the culture. "Sontag was a cultural critic. She rarely wrote about herself; instead, she wrote about the trends and movements of her culture. I encourage you to approach from a similar angle." The "In the parlor" paragraph above never says "I".
  • Leave these behind. "At this point in the year you should be well away from starting sentences with pronouns, answering in a single chunk, one-paragraph response which does not denote attention to categorization, and not listing the titles of the essay."

7 · Before you reply to his email — the check

  • Do my margin tags (Ex, A, P, F, O, E) each sit beside an actual line of her essay?
  • Can I say her overarching argument in one sentence, and the claims that lead to it?
  • Is it a series of paragraphs, each doing one job?
  • Does each paragraph have evidence (her words) and explanation (mine)?
  • Did I answer both "has it's": has the culture changed, and has the definition?
  • Is my own definition in there, with a condition and an example?
  • Do my exigence, audience and purpose come through without being announced?
  • Any sentence starting with a pronoun? Did I name the essay's title, author and date?
  • REPLY to his "For Monday (11/16)" email before class.

8 · Keep the method: Friday's essay uses it

For Friday 11/20 (before class) he asks for your own argument of definition on one term from his list: fun, risk, wealth, pleasure, intelligence, athletic, work, fear or guilt. His final notes (class 11/13) map straight onto what you just practiced:

  • F"You are taking a position on YOUR definition of the word of your choice. Your position is your 'F.'"
  • O"what are the conditions in place that makes your 'F' THE 'F' and perhaps when is your 'F' not THE 'F.'"
  • E"what are examples of your F under your O's?"

So the moves are the same, with a new word and no reading: a one-sentence definition like the one in step 4 is your F, and conditions and examples like your "now" lines in step 3 are your O's and E's. He adds one more challenge for Friday: "Can you identify and utilize a trap door? In other words, can you write an essay that slowly reveals rather than merely defines?"
