Burns: How Deep, and Why It Matters

The four types of burns, what each one destroys, how each one heals — and why a big burn is a whole-body emergency, not just a skin problem.

The one idea to hold onto: a burn is classified by how deep it goes — which layers it destroys. Depth decides everything else: what it looks like, whether it hurts, and whether the skin can regrow itself or needs a graft. Skin regrows from its living cells at the bottom of the epidermis (the stratum basale) and from cells lining the hair follicles and sweat glands down in the dermis. If a burn destroys those, the skin has nothing left to rebuild from.

The four types — tap to see the depth

Pain is the clue: 1st and 2nd degree hurt (the nerve endings in the dermis are alive and irritated). A 3rd- or 4th-degree burn is often painless in the center — not because it's mild, but because the nerve endings are destroyed. (The edges, where the burn is shallower, still hurt.)

Why a big burn is a whole-body emergency

Skin isn't just a covering — it's the body's barrier. It holds water in, keeps germs out, and helps hold in heat. A large, deep burn removes that barrier, and the problems ripple outward:

  • 1. Fluid loss → shock

    Burned skin can't hold water in, and damaged capillaries leak plasma out of the blood into the tissues (swelling) and out through the wound.

    fluid leaks out→less blood volume→blood pressure drops→heart speeds up to compensate

    This is hypovolemic (burn) shock — "hypo" = low, "vol" = volume, "emia" = blood. Treatment: IV fluids right away to refill the blood vessels, raise blood pressure, and keep blood reaching the kidneys, brain and heart. Fluid loss is heaviest in the first day or two.

  • 2. Infection

    Healthy skin is covered with harmless bacteria (normal flora) that stay outside because the barrier holds. Once the barrier is gone, they — and anything else — get in. Infection is a leading cause of death in burn patients. Doctors start a broad-spectrum antibiotic (one that kills many different kinds of bacteria) because they can't wait to find out which one is invading.

  • 3. Heat loss + a metabolism in overdrive

    Water evaporating off the wound carries away heat, so the body burns fuel just to stay warm. Repairing huge areas of tissue costs energy and protein, and protein also leaks out in the wound fluid. The result is hypermetabolism: a patient can lose weight even on a very high-calorie diet.

  • 4. The skin can't rebuild itself

    In a full-thickness burn, the regrowing cells (stratum basale, follicles, glands) are gone. Healing only from the edges would take months, leave the body open to fluid loss and infection the whole time, and end in thick, tight scar. So the fix is a skin graft.

  • 5. Lying still: pressure sores

    A patient stuck in bed presses the same spots for hours. The pressure squeezes blood vessels shut, the tissue gets no oxygen, and it dies — a decubitus ulcer (bedsore). They form where bone sits close to the skin: tailbone (sacrum), hips, heels, shoulder blades, elbows, back of the head. Prevention: turning the patient often.

The healing process

Shallow burns (1st and 2nd) heal themselves

Surviving cells in the stratum basale — and, in a 2nd-degree burn, in the hair follicles and sweat glands that dip down into the dermis — divide and crawl across the wound to rebuild the epidermis. That's why a 2nd-degree burn can heal without a graft: the follicles act as "islands" of new skin.

Deep burns (3rd and 4th) need a graft

  1. Clean it out (debridement). Dead, burned tissue is removed — it can't heal and it feeds infection.
  2. Cover it. Healthy skin is laid over the wound. The best source is the patient's own skin from an unburned spot (an autograft — the body won't reject it). For very large burns, a small sample of the patient's cells can be grown in a lab into sheets of new epidermis; temporary coverings protect the wound while they grow.
  3. The graft "takes." Within a few days, new blood vessels grow up into the graft from the tissue below. Without a blood supply, the graft would die — this is the step everything depends on.
  4. Cells multiply and spread. The grafted cells divide and fill in, merging into a continuous new epidermis over a rebuilt dermis.
  5. Scar remodels. Over months, collagen in the scar reorganizes. Pressure garments and stretching help keep it flat and flexible.

What scar can't do

Scar tissue is mostly dense collagen. It has no hair follicles, no sweat glands, few oil glands, and few nerve endings, so it can't sweat to cool itself, feels less, and tends to be dry, itchy and fragile. And it shrinks and tightens as it matures: over a joint (like an elbow), that tightness is a contracture that limits how far the joint can move. Across the chest or trunk, tight scar can even make a deep breath harder.

Check yourself

1What decides whether a burn is 1st, 2nd, 3rd or 4th degree?

Show answer
How deep it goes — which layers it destroys. 1st: epidermis only. 2nd: into part of the dermis. 3rd: all the way through the dermis (full thickness). 4th: into muscle, tendon or bone.

2A burn has blisters and is very painful. What type is it, and will it need a graft?

Show answer
2nd degree (partial thickness). Usually no graft: surviving cells in the hair follicles, sweat glands and stratum basale regrow the epidermis.

3Why can a 3rd-degree burn be painless?

Show answer
It destroys the whole dermis — including the sensory nerve endings that live there. No nerves, no pain signal. It's a sign of a worse burn, not a milder one.

4A patient with large burns arrives with low blood pressure and a racing heart. Explain the link.

Show answer
Fluid leaks out of the blood through damaged capillaries and the open wound → blood volume falls → blood pressure drops → the heart beats faster to compensate and keep blood reaching the organs. This is hypovolemic (burn) shock; IV fluids replace the lost volume.

5What does "broad-spectrum" mean, and why start one before knowing which bacteria are present?

Show answer
It kills many different types of bacteria. With the skin barrier gone, infection can set in fast and is often deadly — waiting days for a lab to identify the germ would be too risky.

6In a skin graft, what has to happen for the graft to survive?

Show answer
New blood vessels must grow into it from the tissue underneath, within a few days. The epidermis has no blood vessels of its own — it depends on the layer beneath for oxygen and nutrients.

7Why might a burn patient lose weight while eating a very high-calorie diet?

Show answer
Hypermetabolism: repairing tissue takes huge amounts of energy and protein, heat escapes through the wound (so the body burns fuel to stay warm), and protein is lost in wound fluid.

8Where do bedsores usually form, and why there?

Show answer
Over bony spots close to the skin — tailbone, hips, heels, shoulder blades, elbows, back of the head. Pressure between bone and bed squeezes blood vessels shut, so the tissue is starved of oxygen and dies.

9What is a contracture?

Show answer
Scar tissue that shrinks and tightens as it matures, pulling on a joint and limiting its range of motion — common when a deep burn crosses an elbow, knee, hand or neck.
