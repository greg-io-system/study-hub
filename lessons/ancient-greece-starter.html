City-States: Sparta, Athens, Olympia

In your textbook · 6.2

Greece wasn't one country. It was hundreds of independent cities, each called a polis, with its own government, laws, and gods. They shared a language and religion but fought each other constantly. Athens became a democracy, Sparta a military state with two kings. Olympia wasn't a city-state like those two; it was a sacred site to Zeus where the Games were held.

Questions to chase
  • What was a Spartan childhood like, for boys AND girls?
  • Who actually got to vote in Athens, and who didn't?
  • Who were the helots, and why did Sparta depend on them?
Search: polis agoge helots Cleisthenes direct democracy

Ancient Olympics

In your textbook · 6.2 (boxed feature)

Held every four years at Olympia in honor of Zeus, traditionally starting in 776 BCE. Athletes came from all over the Greek world. The events trained skills for war. The Games ran for over a thousand years before a Roman emperor stopped them.

Questions to chase
  • What was the main event, and what modern word comes from it?
  • Who was not allowed to watch, and what was the penalty?
  • What did winners get, and did athletes become professionals?
Search: stadion Olympic truce pankration Pindar

Philosophy

In your textbook · 6.2 + 6.3

Greek thinkers tried to explain the world with reason instead of myth, then turned to big questions about right and wrong. The famous chain is teacher to student: Socrates → Plato → Aristotle. Later, after Alexander, new schools asked how to live a calm, happy life.

Questions to chase
  • Why was Socrates put on trial, and what happened?
  • Where did Plato and Aristotle disagree?
  • What's the difference between a Stoic and an Epicurean?
Search: Socratic method Plato's Academy Lyceum Stoicism sophists

The Greco-Persian War (Thermopylae, Marathon)

In your textbook · 6.2

The giant Persian Empire (the one you just studied) invaded Greece twice. The first time, Athens won at Marathon (490 BCE). The second time, a small force mostly of Spartans held the pass at Thermopylae (480 BCE) to buy time, then the Greek fleet won at Salamis.

Questions to chase
  • Why did Persia want to punish Athens in the first place?
  • What were the Spartans at Thermopylae actually trying to do? (Hint: not win.)
  • What does the modern "marathon" race have to do with this battle?
Search: Darius Xerxes Leonidas Salamis Herodotus

Greek Mythology and Religion

Mostly research · textbook touches it

Greeks worshipped a family of gods led by Zeus, believed to live on Mount Olympus. Every city had its own patron god and temples (Athens had Athena). Big shared sacred sites included Delphi, home of Apollo's oracle.

Questions to chase
  • Who were the twelve Olympians, and what did each one rule over?
  • What did people ask the Oracle at Delphi, and how did it answer?
  • How did religion and city life mix (festivals, temples, sacrifices)?
Search: Twelve Olympians Oracle of Delphi patron deity Panathenaia

Architecture and Innovation

Partly in your textbook · 6.2

The Parthenon on the Acropolis in Athens is the famous one, rebuilt after the Persians destroyed the old temples. Your textbook has a twist: it was paid for with money from Athens' allies, which made them angry. "Innovation" can also mean the Greek alphabet (they added vowels) or coins.

Questions to chase
  • What are the three Greek column styles, and how do you tell them apart?
  • What tricks did the Parthenon's builders use to make it look perfectly straight?
  • Which Greek inventions or ideas are still in use?
Search: Doric Ionic Corinthian Parthenon Acropolis Greek alphabet vowels

Greek Theater

In your textbook · 6.2

Athens was the birthplace of theater. Plays were performed at religious festivals, with masks, music, and a chorus. Tragedies (Sophocles, Euripides) used old myths to ask hard questions. Comedies (Aristophanes) made fun of politicians and the war.

Questions to chase
  • What was the chorus for?
  • Why masks? And why did men play every part?
  • What is Antigone about, and why is it still performed?
Search: Dionysia Greek chorus tragedy vs comedy Theatre of Epidaurus

Foundations of Science and Math

Research-only · barely in your textbook

The textbook mentions thinkers who explained the universe without gods: Democritus said everything is made of tiny "atoms." For math, the names to look up are Pythagoras, Euclid, and Archimedes. Alexandria in Egypt, with its huge library, became a research center after Alexander.

Questions to chase
  • What did each of those three mathematicians figure out?
  • Which of their ideas did you use in a math class this year?
  • What was the Library of Alexandria, and what happened to it?
Search: Euclid Elements Archimedes Pythagorean theorem Eratosthenes

Medicine

Research-only · not in your textbook

The name to start with is Hippocrates, often called the "father of medicine." The big shift was treating illness as something with natural causes you could observe, not a punishment from the gods. (Good one if you like Anatomy.)

Questions to chase
  • What were the "four humors," and how did they explain sickness?
  • What is the Hippocratic Oath, and do doctors still take a version of it?
  • What was an Asclepion?
Search: Hippocrates four humors Hippocratic Oath Asclepius Galen

Alexander the Great

In your textbook · 6.3

King of Macedon at 20. He conquered the whole Persian Empire, reaching as far as the Indus River (modern Pakistan), then died at 33. He left no plan for who would rule next, so his generals split the empire. His real legacy was spreading Greek culture across a huge area.

Questions to chase
  • Why did his army refuse to keep going in India?
  • Who burned Persepolis, and why? (Historians disagree. Your textbook has both sides.)
  • What does "Hellenistic" mean?
Search: Philip II Gaugamela Alexandria Hellenistic Bucephalus

Epic Hero Tales

Mostly research · textbook touches it

The two big ones are the Iliad (the Trojan War, starring Achilles) and the Odyssey (Odysseus's ten-year trip home), credited to the poet Homer. They were sung aloud for generations before anyone wrote them down. Alexander the Great modeled himself on Achilles.

Questions to chase
  • What makes someone a Greek "hero," and how is that different from a superhero?
  • What's the Trojan Horse story? The "Achilles' heel"?
  • Was Homer a real person? (Historians aren't sure.)
Search: Iliad Odyssey Homer Heracles labors oral tradition

For the "Modern Connection" box

This is the box teachers grade hardest, because it shows you understood why it mattered. A strong one is specific. "We still have the Olympics" is a start. Saying what carried over, and what changed, is the full answer. Try one of these frames:

  • "We still use…": a word, an idea, a design, or a tool that came from it.
  • "It's like today's…, except…": a modern equivalent and one real difference.
  • "Without it, we wouldn't have…": a thing that grew out of it.
