Hand vs. foot: the names match, but a C means hand (carpals, metacarpals) and a T means foot (tarsals, metatarsals). Fingers and toes are phalanges either way.

Axial vs. appendicular skeleton

Axial = the central column

Skull, hyoid, vertebral column, thorax (sternum + ribs).
Job: hold you upright and protect the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs.

Appendicular = what hangs off it

Pectoral girdle, arms and hands, pelvic girdle, legs and feet.
Job: movement: reaching, walking, grabbing.

Trap: the girdles (clavicle, scapula, coxal bones) are appendicular even though they sit close to the trunk. They're the anchors the limbs attach to.

Compact vs. spongy bone

Compact boneSpongy bone
Looks likeSolid and denseLight, porous, like a honeycomb of struts
WhereThe hard outer wall of every bone; the thick shaft of long bonesInside the ends of long bones, and inside flat and irregular bones
JobStrength; resists bendingAbsorbs impact, keeps bones light, and holds red marrow in its spaces

Bone tissue is called osseous tissue. Blood vessels reach the bone cells through tiny tunnels: Haversian canals run along the bone and Volkmann canals cross between them.

Phalanges are long bones even though they're small. "Long" is about shape (longer than wide), not size.

To name a joint from a question: first ask how much it moves (none = fibrous, a little = cartilaginous, freely = synovial). If it's synovial, ask which way it moves: everywhere = ball & socket, one way = hinge, twisting = pivot.

2. Function

The 5 jobs of bones, and the structure behind each

JobWhat bones doWhich structure makes it possible
SupportFrame that gives the body its shape, holds posture, and carries body weightHard compact bone in the long bones of the legs and spine
ProtectionSkull → brain, eyes, ears · rib cage → heart, lungs · vertebrae → spinal cord · pelvis → lower abdominal and reproductive organsFlat bones and irregular bones that form shields and rings
MovementMuscles pull on bones; joints let bones swing; bones act as levers to multiply forceTendons join muscle to bone; ligaments join bone to bone and steady the joint; the periosteum is where they anchor

Hemo = blood, poiesis = making. A downside of storage: bones can also soak up harmful metals such as lead.

Bone maintenance: resorption vs. reformation

Bone is alive and is constantly being torn down and rebuilt. That's why exercise strengthens it, and why your whole skeleton turns over about every 10 years.

Resorption (tear down)

Osteoclasts dissolve bone and release its minerals into the blood.

Reformation (build up)

Osteoblasts lay down new bone matrix.
The imbalance that causes osteoporosis: if resorption runs faster than reformation, more bone is removed than is put back. Bone density drops, bones become porous and brittle, and they break more easily.

Ossification: how bones grow

  • Ossification = making new bone. In a fetus and in children, much of the skeleton starts as cartilage and is gradually replaced by bone. Osteoblasts do the building; osteocytes maintain the result.
  • Growth plates are bands of cartilage near the ends of long bones. New cartilage keeps forming at the plate and gets turned into bone, which pushes the ends apart, so the bone gets longer.
  • When growth finishes, the plate is fully converted to bone and closes. That's why you stop getting taller.

How bones and muscles work together: origin & insertion

  • A muscle has to attach to at least two bones, crossing a joint, to move anything. Think of pushing a box: you need your feet on the floor (the still bone) and your hands on the box (the bone that moves).
  • Origin = the end attached to the bone that stays still. Insertion = the end attached to the bone that moves.
  • When the muscle contracts it pulls the insertion toward the origin.

Example: the biceps has its origin on the scapula and its insertion on the radius. When it contracts, the forearm comes up toward the shoulder, and the elbow (a hinge) bends.

The 4 stages of fracture healing

1
0–2 weeks
HematomaBlood from torn vessels clots around the break. Inflammation brings in immune cells to clean up.
2
2–3 weeks
Soft callusCollagen fibers and cartilage bridge the gap, a soft splint that steadies the break.
3
3–6 weeks
Hard callusThe cartilage is replaced by real bone. Now it's firm, but lumpy.
4
8 weeks → months
RemodelingOsteoclasts trim the extra bone and osteoblasts reinforce what's needed, back toward the original shape.
Order hook: Clot → Cartilage → Bone → Reshape. The callus goes soft before it goes hard.

What speeds up or slows down healing

FactorEffect
AgeYounger people heal faster: more active bone cells and a better blood supply.
NutritionNeeds calcium, vitamin D (to absorb that calcium) and protein (for collagen). A poor diet slows healing.
Overall healthChronic conditions (e.g. diabetes, poor circulation) slow it.
ImmobilizationA cast or splint keeps the pieces lined up and still, so the callus can form. Moving a fresh break disrupts it.
SmokingSlows healing by reducing blood flow (less oxygen reaching the break).
MedicationsSome medicines (e.g. long-term steroids) slow bone repair.

3. Disorders & injuries

Fractures: the big split

Closed fracture

Bone breaks, skin stays intact.
  • Stress: hairline cracks from repeated force (runners, dancers)
  • Avulsion: a tendon or ligament yanks off a chip of bone where it attaches
  • Displaced: broken all the way through and the ends have shifted out of line; often needs pins, plates or screws

Open (compound) fracture

Bone breaks and pierces the skin.
  • Needs surgery right away: the wound lets bacteria reach the bone (infection risk), and the bone has to be realigned.

Six disorders: compare & contrast

Sort by what's damaged first: ligaments/tendons → sprain · bursae → bursitis · joint cartilage → arthritis · bone density → osteoporosis · bone cells → cancer · joint position → dislocation.
DisorderWhat it isCauseEffects / treatment
SprainLigaments (and/or tendons) stretched or tornTwisting a joint past its limit (rolled ankle)Pain, swelling. Heals slowly, because ligaments have little direct blood supply. Rest, ice, support.
BursitisInflamed bursae (fluid sacs that cushion a joint) swell with fluidRepetitive motion or constant pressure (kneeling, throwing)Pain and swelling at the joint; rest and anti-inflammatories
ArthritisPainful breakdown of movable (synovial) jointsEither the immune system attacks the joint lining and cartilage, with bone laid down in its place, or the cartilage is worn down by repeated damagePain, stiffness, less range of motion; managed with medication, exercise, sometimes joint replacement
OsteoporosisLoss of bone density: porous, brittle bonesResorption outpaces reformation; linked to the drop in sex hormones with ageEasy fractures (hip, spine, wrist). Prevent with weight-bearing exercise and calcium in your teens and twenties.
Bone cancerCancer in bone. Usually it spread there from another cancer (metastasized); cancer that starts in bone is rareSpread from elsewhere; primary type is rareMain symptom: pain. Surgery, amputation, chemotherapy, radiation.
DislocationBone ends forced out of their place in a jointA hard blow or fall; most often shoulders and fingersPainful and deformed; the joint can't move. Must be put back promptly to avoid cutting off blood flow.

Osteoporosis up close

  • The imbalance: osteoclasts remove bone faster than osteoblasts replace it, so density falls year after year.
  • Why women are at higher risk: smaller bones to start with (less to lose); calcium lost over menstrual cycles; a sharp drop in sex hormones (estrogen) at menopause, and those hormones help protect bone.
  • Prevention works early: you build most of your peak bone mass in your teens and twenties. Weight-bearing exercise plus a calcium-rich diet now means more bone in the bank later.
