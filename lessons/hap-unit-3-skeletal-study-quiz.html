1. Structure

Bone basics

  • There are 206 bones in the human body.
  • Each bone is built from several kinds of tissue, and that is what makes a bone an organ.
  • Bone is about 22% water.
  • The smallest bones in your body are in your ear.

Compact vs. spongy bone

Compact boneSpongy bone
What it isThe dense wall of the boneLight and porous
What else to know—Absorbs impact. Red marrow (which makes blood cells) is found in the spongy bone of the skull, ribs, sternum, clavicles, vertebrae and pelvis.

Bone tissue is called osseous tissue. Osteocytes are the mature bone cells. Haversian and Volkmann canals are passageways that let blood vessels into and out of the bone.

Joints in general

  • Joints are the junctions between bones. They bind the parts of the skeleton together, allow for growth, and let the body move.
  • A joint can be immovable, slightly movable, or freely movable.
  • There are 360 joints in the body.

Memory trick — naming a joint: first ask how much it moves (immovable = fibrous, slightly movable = cartilaginous, freely movable = synovial). 2. Function

The 5 jobs of bones, and the structure behind each

JobWhat bones doWhich structure makes it possible
SupportForm the body's framework, giving it shape and structure · hold the body upright (posture) · support the head and trunk · give muscles and skin a base to attach to · bear the body's weight when standing and movingThe skeleton is the body's framework
ProtectionSkull → brain, ears, eyes · rib cage → heart, lungs · vertebrae → spinal cord · pelvis → lower abdominal organs and reproductive organs · bones are hard barriers against impacts from outsideHard bone around the organs (skull, rib cage, vertebrae, pelvis)
Movement (muscle attachment)Bones give muscles surfaces to attach to · muscles pull on bones to move them · joints between bones allow flexibility and range of motion · the skeleton works as a system of levers that increase the force and efficiency of movementTendons join muscle to bone to help it move; ligaments join bone to bone to stabilize joints; both attach at the periosteum

Hemo = blood, poiesis = creating. A downside of storage: bones can also build up harmful elements such as lead, radium and strontium.

Bone maintenance: resorption vs. reformation

Bone is constantly being broken down and formed again, to support growth, repair and strength. You replace your whole skeleton about every 10 years.

Resorption (break down)

Osteoclasts break down and dissolve bone tissue to release bone minerals.

Reformation (build up)

Osteoblasts build new bone in the bone matrix.
The imbalance behind osteoporosis: if resorption happens faster than reformation, bone is broken down faster than it's rebuilt, and that leads to bone loss (osteoporosis).

Ossification: how bones grow

  • Ossification = creating new bone, especially during growth and development. In a fetus or child, cartilage is turned into bone. The main cells involved are osteoblasts (form bone) and osteocytes (maintain it).
  • Growth plates are areas of cartilage near the ends of long bones. As a person grows, new cartilage is made at the plate over and over and then replaced by bone (ossification), so the bone gets longer.
  • When growth is complete, the growth plate closes.

How bones and muscles work together: origin & insertion

  • A muscle must be attached to at least two bones to create movement. Mrs. Kalec's picture: pushing a box. The box is the bone that moves, the floor is the bone that stays still, and you are the muscle. You can't move the box by sitting on it or without touching it: you need to touch the box and the floor.
  • Origin = the muscle's stationary end (on the bone that stays still). Insertion = its movable end.
  • When a muscle contracts, it pulls the insertion toward the origin, which causes movement at a joint.

3. Disorders & injuries

Fractures: the big split

Closed fracture

The bone breaks but doesn't puncture the skin.
  • Stress: tiny cracks in the bone, usually from force applied over and over
  • Avulsion: a tendon or ligament pulls off a piece of the bone
  • Displaced: broken all the way through; usually needs surgery with pins, plates or screws to help the bones realign and heal

Open (compound) fracture

The bone breaks and punctures or tears the skin.
  • Needs surgery right away, to keep the bone from becoming infected and to help the bones realign and heal.

Osteoporosis up close

  • The imbalance: osteoclasts break bone down faster than osteoblasts rebuild it, which leads to bone loss.
  • Linked to: a decline in sex hormones.
  • Why women are more at risk: their smaller bones can't afford to lose as much tissue; they lose a lot of calcium during the menstrual cycle; and sex hormones decline at menopause.
  • Prevention: regular exercise and a calcium-rich diet in your teens and twenties.
